\begin{abstract}
We present \PORTER{} (\emph{Plan-Oriented Router for
Teleportation-Enabled aRchitectures}), a router for multi-core
distributed quantum computers that minimises inter-core EPR-pair
consumption. \PORTER{} unifies the three inter-core communication
primitives used in prior work --- state teleportation (\TeleData{}),
gate teleportation (\TeleGate{}), and multi-gate cat-entangler packets
(\TeleGatePacket{}) --- under a single \emph{plan-committed} decision
loop: where \TeleSABRE{} and \dSABRE{} score every inter-core
candidate together with its staging SWAPs in one flat action pool,
\PORTER{} first scores whole remote plans on their physical and
DAG-lookahead merits alone, commits to the best one, and only then
generates the preparation SWAPs specific to that plan, so preparation
effort is never spent on a plan that is not selected. A two-tier
capacity-relief mechanism proactively routes qubits toward reservoir
cores when candidate plans are blocked by insufficient free capacity,
and a deterministic, seed-free \emph{temporal locality-greedy} initial
layout co-locates qubits by temporally discounted interaction weight
in first-use order. On MQT-Bench suites at 25, 64, and 80 logical
qubits, a \emph{single} \PORTER{} run from this single layout posts
the lowest geometric-mean EPR count of the three routers on every
suite -- reducing EPR against \TeleSABRE{} by $54.3\%$, $49.8\%$, and
$65.8\%$, and beating \dSABRE{}'s best-of-three protocol ($43.1$
vs.\ $47.0$, $86.4$ vs.\ $89.0$, and $14.0$ vs.\ $15.8$) -- while
routing $11$--$61\times$ faster per attempt than \dSABRE{}. Routed
from identical externally supplied layouts across thirty
instances, \PORTER{} also matches or beats \dSABRE{}'s routing stage
(fifteen wins to twelve, three ties). \PORTER{} is the only router
of the three to complete every benchmark: on an 80-qubit, 23{,}381-CX
circuit it finishes in 37\,s where both baselines exceed a uniform
900\,s compiler timeout. The implementation, an independent
replay verifier, and the full comparison harness are publicly
available.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Distributed quantum computers (DQCs) interconnect several small,
high-fidelity quantum processing units (QPUs, or \emph{cores}) with
inter-core links that support remote entanglement
generation~\cite{cuomo2020towards}, offering a near-term path to scale
beyond what a single monolithic chip can host.  Compiling a circuit
onto a DQC decomposes into three coupled sub-problems~\cite{escofet2024review}:
\emph{(i)} statically assigning logical qubits to cores, \emph{(ii)}
choosing, for each non-local two-qubit gate, which communication
primitive realises it, and \emph{(iii)} routing and scheduling the
resulting sequence of intra-core SWAPs and inter-core operations
subject to communication-port capacity.  Every communication
primitive consumes one or more EPR pairs, and EPR generation is slow,
noisy, and rate-limited on current
hardware~\cite{stephenson2020high,daiss2021quantum}, making EPR
minimisation the dominant compilation objective.

\PORTER{} --- a \emph{Plan-Oriented Router for Teleportation-Enabled
aRchitectures} --- addresses stage~(iii), ordering intra-core SWAPs
and inter-core operations to minimise EPR consumption, and ships a
deliberately lightweight answer to stage~(i): a deterministic,
seed-free initial layout built in microseconds, so that the full
pipeline needs neither an external partitioner nor a per-circuit
layout search (external layouts remain supported, and are used
throughout the evaluation as controlled probes). The name is also a
job description: like a
hotel porter, the router's task is to decide which guests (logical
qubits) to carry to which rooms (cores), when, and by which means ---
and to do so only after a plan for the whole errand has been settled.
Unlike \dSABRE{}~\cite{lidsabre}, which restricts itself
to state teleportation (\TeleData{}), \PORTER{} follows
\TeleSABRE{}~\cite{russo2025telesabre} in also supporting gate
teleportation (\TeleGate{}) via the cat-entangler
protocol~\cite{eisert2000optimal,yimsiriwattana2004generalized}, and
extends it with a same-control \emph{packet} primitive
(\TeleGatePacket{}) that amortises one EPR pair over an ordered run of
consecutive remote CX gates sharing a control qubit, in the spirit of
gate-sequence amortisation used by AutoComm~\cite{wu2022autocomm}.

The router departs from \TeleSABRE{} and \dSABRE{} in
\emph{when} it commits to core-preparation SWAPs. Both
score inter-core candidates and their
staging SWAPs together in one flat action pool.  \PORTER{} instead
separates the decision into two phases: first, every legal remote
\emph{plan} --- a teleport direction, a single telegate, a same-control
packet, or a capacity-blocked variant of any of these --- is scored
using only its physical and DAG-lookahead properties, independent of
how many preparation SWAPs it would take to make it physically legal
right now; only \emph{after} one plan is selected does the router
generate and apply preparation SWAPs specific to that plan, repeating
until the plan becomes physically ready or no further preparation SWAP
improves it. This plan-first ordering means preparation effort is
never spent scoring or partially staging a remote action that a
better one will preempt.

\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A plan-first remote-action loop} that separates
  candidate scoring from preparation-SWAP generation
  (Section~\ref{sec:sfc-plan-first}), unifying teleport, telegate, and
  same-control packet primitives under one direct/latent/lookahead
  gain decomposition (Section~\ref{sec:sfc-scoring}).
\item \textbf{A two-tier capacity-relief mechanism}: a breadth-first
  search for the nearest reservoir core when a plan is capacity-blocked
  (Section~\ref{sec:sfc-relief}), plus an opportunistic comparator that
  lets a severely blocked candidate pre-empt a merely-good normal one.
  An ablation (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}) shows this mechanism is
  what makes the deterministic-greedy design complete at scale:
  without it, four of the six 80-qubit benchmarks deadlock.
\item \textbf{A deterministic, seed-free initial layout} --- temporal
  locality-greedy (Section~\ref{sec:sfc-layout}): each CX pair is
  weighted by temporally discounted interaction multiplicity, qubits
  are visited in first-use order and co-located with their heaviest
  placed neighbour under a per-core free-slot reservation. A single
  layout built in microseconds replaces per-router layout search
  entirely, yet outperforms \dSABRE{}'s SabreLayout-based
  best-of-three protocol end-to-end on every suite we test.
\item \textbf{An independent replay verifier}
  (\appVerification\footnote{Appendices A--D are provided in the
  separate supplementary-material document
  (\texttt{sfc\_appendix.pdf}).}) that re-executes the emitted
  operation stream against its own physical-layout simulation and
  checks gate coverage, DAG-order faithfulness, and every physical
  legality constraint (coupling-edge adjacency, port occupancy,
  cat-section isolation), used to certify correctness across 60
  randomized circuits and the full evaluation suite --- and itself
  validated by fault injection: ten classes of single-point stream
  corruption, every mutant rejected by the intended check.
\item \textbf{A head-to-head evaluation} against \TeleSABRE{} and
  \dSABRE{} on identical physical topologies at 25, 64, and 80
  logical qubits (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}), under each router's
  own initial-layout protocol and under shared fixed layouts (with an
  external \texttt{pytket-dqc}-generated layout source in
  \appPytket).
\end{itemize}

The \PORTER{} implementation, the replay verifier, the full
cross-router comparison harness, and the raw per-circuit results are
publicly available at
\url{https://github.com/ebony72/porter}.

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Quantum Circuits and Routing}
\label{sec:qcircuits}

A quantum circuit acts on $n$ logical qubits through a sequence of
gates; we work in the restricted basis $\{\texttt{id}, \texttt{rz},
\texttt{sx}, \texttt{x}, \texttt{cx}\}$ used by MQT-Bench's
Qiskit-opt3-transpiled circuits, i.e.\ arbitrary one-qubit (1Q) gates
plus the two-qubit (2Q) CX gate. A circuit is represented as a
directed acyclic graph (DAG): for every logical qubit, consecutive
gates acting on it are connected by a dependency edge. The
\emph{front} (or \emph{DAG front}) is the set of gates with no
unexecuted predecessor; only front gates can execute or be routed
towards execution at a given step. Figure~\ref{fig:circuit-dag} shows
a five-gate circuit and its DAG; note that gates sharing a qubit
(e.g.\ two CX gates with the same control) are chained by a dependency
edge and therefore \emph{never} appear in the front simultaneously ---
a fact that matters for packet collection
(Section~\ref{sec:sfc-packet}).

Physical qubits are partitioned into \emph{cores}; each core has an
intra-core coupling graph, and pairs of cores may be connected by an
inter-core link whose two endpoints are designated \emph{communication
ports} (Figure~\ref{fig:arch}).  A \emph{layout} maps every logical
qubit to a physical
position.  A 2Q gate is \emph{local} if both operands share a core and
\emph{remote} otherwise. Routing a local gate that is not yet adjacent
requires intra-core SWAPs; routing a remote gate requires an inter-core
communication primitive.

\textbf{State teleportation (\TeleData{}).} Consumes one EPR pair to
move a logical qubit's state from a physical position adjacent to a
free source-side communication port to the (necessarily free)
destination-side port in a neighbouring
core~\cite{bennett1993teleporting}. The qubit then participates in
local gates in its new core.

\textbf{Gate teleportation (\TeleGate{}).} Executes a single remote CX
directly across two adjacent cores via a cat-entangler
protocol~\cite{eisert2000optimal,yimsiriwattana2004generalized}: one
EPR pair entangles the two communication ports, the remote CX is
applied using the target-side port as a proxy for the control qubit,
and the entanglement is consumed on disentangling. Neither logical
qubit physically moves.

\textbf{Same-control packets (\TeleGatePacket{}).} When several
consecutive remote CX gates in the DAG front share the same control
qubit and land in the same target core, \PORTER{} amortises them onto a
\emph{single} cat-entangler resource: the entangling and disentangling
steps bracket an ordered sequence of remote CX and target-core 1Q
gates, all executed while the two communication ports stay reserved.
This is the same amortisation idea used by
AutoComm~\cite{wu2022autocomm}, specialised to the shared-control case
that arises naturally at the DAG front (Section~\ref{sec:qcircuits}).

\subsection{SABRE}
\label{sec:sabre}

\SABRE{}~\cite{li2019tackling} routes a single-core circuit by
repeatedly selecting the SWAP that minimises a front-layer distance
term plus a weighted extended-set lookahead term, both expressed as
$\Delta$ (old distance minus new distance) on gates sharing a qubit
with the candidate SWAP. \PORTER{} inherits this scoring form for its
intra-core stage (Section~\ref{sec:sfc-intra}) and extends the same
direct/lookahead decomposition to its inter-core plan scorer
(Section~\ref{sec:sfc-scoring}).

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\PORTER{} \texttt{compile}$(C, \ell_0)$}
\label{alg:sfc}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE $\ell \leftarrow \ell_0$; initialise DAG active/frontier sets from $C$
\WHILE{DAG has active gates}
  \STATE $F \leftarrow$ DAG front (bounded by \texttt{dag\_front\_limit})
  \STATE classify $F$ into singles, direct-local CX, local-CX-needing-SWAP,
         adjacent-remote CX, non-adjacent-remote CX \hfill\textit{(Sec.~\ref{sec:sfc-classify})}
  \IF{singles or direct-local CX present}
    \STATE execute them all; remove from DAG; \textbf{continue}
  \ENDIF
  \IF{local-CX-needing-SWAP present}
    \STATE generate candidate intra-core SWAPs, apply the best-scoring one
           (Eq.~\ref{eq:sfc-local}); \textbf{continue} \hfill\textit{(Sec.~\ref{sec:sfc-intra})}
  \ENDIF
  \STATE generate normal remote plans $\mathcal{P}$ and blocked-teleport
         plans $\mathcal{B}$ \hfill\textit{(Sec.~\ref{sec:sfc-scoring})}
  \IF{$\mathcal{P}\neq\emptyset$}
    \STATE $p^\ast \leftarrow$ best ready-or-preparable plan in $\mathcal{P}$
    \IF{some $b\in\mathcal{B}$ has gain $\gg$ gain$(p^\ast)$}
      \STATE $p^\ast \leftarrow$ best opportunistic capacity-relief plan for $b$
             \hfill\textit{(Sec.~\ref{sec:sfc-relief})}
    \ENDIF
  \ELSE
    \STATE BFS from each blocked target core to its nearest reservoir;
           $p^\ast \leftarrow$ best tail-relief plan \hfill\textit{(Sec.~\ref{sec:sfc-relief})}
  \ENDIF
  \IF{$p^\ast$ is \texttt{None}} \STATE \textbf{report deadlock; abort} \ENDIF
  \STATE apply $p^\ast$'s own preparation SWAPs until ready, then execute it
\ENDWHILE
\RETURN emitted operations, final layout, stats
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Initial Layout}
\label{sec:sfc-layout}

\PORTER{} constructs its own starting layout $\ell_0$ with a single
deterministic, seed-free heuristic and does not require an external
partitioner or a best-of-$N$ search. It respects the capacity invariant
the routing loop depends on: every core keeps at least $f_\mathrm{min}$
free physical positions ($f_\mathrm{min}{=}2$), so that from the first
iteration any core can serve as a \TeleData{} destination and as a
reservoir for capacity relief (Section~\ref{sec:sfc-relief}). A logical
qubit is therefore never placed into a core that already holds
$|c|-f_\mathrm{min}$ logicals, where $|c|$ is the core's physical
capacity.

\textbf{Temporal locality-greedy.} The heuristic runs in three
deterministic steps. \emph{(i) Weighting.} Each CX gate
$g=(q_i,q_j)$ contributes $\gamma^{\ell(g)}$ to the interaction weight
$w(q_i,q_j)$ of its qubit pair, where $\ell(g)$ is $g$'s
as-soon-as-possible layer in the CX-only DAG and $\gamma{=}0.99$; early
interactions thus weigh more than late ones, while repeated
interactions on a pair accumulate. \emph{(ii) Core assignment.} The
logical qubits are visited in the order they first appear as a CX
operand and each is placed in the core holding its heaviest
already-placed neighbour when that core still has headroom above
$f_\mathrm{min}$, falling back to the core with the most remaining
reserved capacity otherwise. \emph{(iii) Physical placement.} Within
each core, the assigned logicals are laid onto physical positions by
descending total interaction weight onto ascending intra-core
centrality (sum of local distances), so the most-connected qubits take
the most central slots; unused slots are left idle.

This single construction subsumes what the earlier design split across
two heuristics and a best-of-six search. The temporal discount
front-loads the opening layer of interactions, recovering the
first-CX-layer co-location a ``balanced first-layer'' scan gave; the
accumulated multiplicity together with the first-appearance visit order
tracks chain-structured circuits a first-layer scan cannot -- a
GHZ-style chain exposes only one disjoint pair in its first CX layer,
yet successive chain neighbours share weight and are visited in
sequence, so they land in the same core. Of the three steps the visit
order carries the most weight: replacing the first-appearance order with
a random permutation inflates geometric-mean EPR from $55.4$ to $103.3$
over the eighteen benchmark instances (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}),
whereas replacing centrality placement with a random within-core
shuffle costs only $55.4\!\to\!56.4$.

\textbf{External layouts.} When $\ell_0$ instead comes from an outside
mapper that may fill a core to capacity
(\appPytket), \PORTER{} first repairs it
(\texttt{repair\_full\_cores}): it evicts one logical qubit from every
saturated core into the core with the most spare capacity, restoring
the $f_\mathrm{min}$ reservation before routing. A core packed to its
physical capacity can never clear an occupied communication port with
local SWAPs, so without this step its qubits would be unreachable by
any teleport or telegate. \PORTER{}'s own heuristic reserves
$f_\mathrm{min}$ by construction and never invokes the repair.

\subsection{Front Classification}
\label{sec:sfc-classify}

Every gate $g$ in the (size-capped) DAG front is classified under the
current layout $\ell$: 1Q gates are always directly executable; a CX
gate $g=(q_i,q_j)$ with $\kappa_\ell(q_i)=\kappa_\ell(q_j)=c$ (same
core) is \emph{direct-local} if $\dL(c,\ell(q_i),\ell(q_j))=1$ and
\emph{local-needing-SWAP} otherwise; a CX gate with
$\kappa_\ell(q_i)\ne\kappa_\ell(q_j)$ is \emph{adjacent-remote} if the
two cores are core-graph neighbours and \emph{non-adjacent-remote}
otherwise. Directly executable gates are always drained first and
completely, so remote-stage decisions are only made once the front
contains solely gates that need routing.

\subsection{Intra-Core SWAP Scoring}
\label{sec:sfc-intra}

For a local-needing-SWAP gate set $F_\mathrm{loc}$, \PORTER{} builds
candidate SWAP edges from the coupling-graph neighbours of every
physical position touched by $F_\mathrm{loc}$, simulates each SWAP,
and scores it as
\begin{equation}
  J(a) = \bar d_{\mathcal{F}}(a) + \lambda_\mathrm{loc}\,\bar d_{\mathcal{E}}(a),
  \qquad \lambda_\mathrm{loc}=0.8,
  \label{eq:sfc-local}
\end{equation}
the mean post-SWAP local distance over $F_\mathrm{loc}$ plus a
weighted mean over an extended set $\mathcal{E}_\mathrm{loc}$: up to
$N_\mathrm{loc}{=}20$ local CX gates collected from the next
$L_\mathrm{loc}{=}12$ DAG layers via a virtual frontier simulation
(Section~\ref{sec:sfc-simstate}). Ties are broken first by the raw
front-layer distance, then, if still tied, by a decision uniformly
sampled from a seeded random-number generator, with every seeded
tie-break recorded in \texttt{RouterStats} for reproducibility
auditing.

\subsection{Plan-First Remote Stage}
\label{sec:sfc-plan-first}

\PORTER{} manipulates remote work as \emph{plans}, and the word is
chosen deliberately. A plan is a complete, committable specification
of one inter-core action: which primitive fires (\TeleData{},
\TeleGate{}, or \TeleGatePacket{}), which gate or same-control gate
run it resolves, its direction (which endpoint moves, or which side
hosts the control), and consequently its \emph{readiness conditions}
--- which logical endpoint must sit adjacent to which communication
port, and which ports must be free. What
a plan deliberately leaves unspecified is the SWAP sequence that will
establish those conditions. This is the classical deliberation-versus-%
execution split: alternatives are compared at the level of \emph{what}
remote work to do next, each scored as if its preconditions were met,
and only the one committed plan is handed to the preparation machinery
that works out \emph{how}. A flat action pool, by contrast, makes the
same choice implicitly and one micro-move at a time: every staging
SWAP it selects is also a vote for whichever remote action that SWAP
happens to serve, votes for competing actions interleave, and staging
effort is routinely spent on actions that are never taken
(Section~\ref{sec:sfc-example} gives a concrete front where this
changes the outcome).

When the front contains only remote CX gates, \PORTER{} generates two
disjoint candidate sets from the current layout:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Normal plans} $\mathcal{P}$: for every adjacent-remote CX
  gate, a single \TeleGate{}; if a same-control run of $\ge 2$ remote
  CX gates can be collected by simulating the DAG front forward
  (Section~\ref{sec:sfc-packet}), a \TeleGatePacket{}; and, for either
  endpoint, a \TeleData{} plan into the partner's core \emph{if} that
  core already has at least \texttt{min\_free\_before\_teleport}
  ($f_\mathrm{min}{=}2$) free physical positions. For non-adjacent-remote
  CX gates, only distance-reducing \TeleData{} hops (one core-graph
  step closer to the gate's other endpoint) with sufficient destination
  capacity are generated.
\item \textbf{Blocked plans} $\mathcal{B}$: the same directional
  \TeleData{} candidates, but for destination cores that currently fail
  the $f_\mathrm{min}$ capacity test. These are never selected
  directly; they exist to detect when \emph{no} normal plan exists
  because every candidate destination is saturated, or when a normal
  plan is technically available but a severely blocked plan promises
  much higher gain if capacity is relieved first.
\end{itemize}

Each plan is scored (Section~\ref{sec:sfc-scoring}) \emph{before} any
preparation SWAP for it is generated. The router then:
\begin{enumerate}
\item selects the best plan in $\mathcal{P}$ that is either already
  physically ready or admits at least one improving preparation SWAP
  right now (see the preparation rule below);
\item compares its gain against the best plan in $\mathcal{B}$; if the
  blocked plan's gain exceeds the selected normal plan's by more than
  a fixed margin $m_\mathrm{assist}{=}5.0$, the router instead searches
  for an \emph{opportunistic capacity-relief} plan for the blocked
  plan's saturated destination core (Section~\ref{sec:sfc-relief}) and,
  if one exists, executes that instead;
\item if $\mathcal{P}=\emptyset$ (every remote CX gate is
  non-adjacent), the router runs the same breadth-first capacity-relief
  search directly on $\mathcal{B}$'s destination cores, grouped by hop
  count, taking the shortest-hop group with a viable candidate.
\end{enumerate}
Once a plan is committed, the router repeatedly generates and applies
\emph{only that plan's} preparation SWAPs
until it becomes ready, no further improving preparation SWAP exists,
or the full decision state (plan identity, layout, RNG state) repeats
--- the latter raising a hard error rather than looping forever, bounded
by \texttt{max\_prep\_iterations\_per\_plan} ($10^4$).

\textbf{Preparation SWAPs.}\label{sec:sfc-prep} A committed plan's
readiness requirements are exactly two \emph{(core, neighbour-core,
endpoint)} triples (source and destination side for \TeleData{};
control and target side for \TeleGate{}/\TeleGatePacket{}, whose
target-side routing is deferred to execution time,
Section~\ref{sec:sfc-packet}). For each unmet requirement, candidate
preparation SWAPs are restricted to edges incident to either the
plan's logical endpoint or a nearest free physical position tied for
minimum distance to the relevant communication port (only while that
port remains occupied); each candidate is scored by the post-SWAP
value of the same readiness-cost function used to rank blocked plans
(Section~\ref{sec:sfc-scoring}), and at most one improving SWAP per
core side is applied per pass.

\subsection{Plan Scoring: Direct, Latent, and Lookahead Gain}
\label{sec:sfc-scoring}

Every plan's score is $\mathrm{total} = \mathrm{direct} +
\mathrm{lookahead}$, computed differently per plan kind but sharing
one $\Delta$-form convention: for a CX gate $g=(q_i,q_j)$ and a
candidate qubit relocation, $\Delta(g) = \dC(\kappa(q_i),\kappa(q_j))
- \dC(\kappa'(q_i),\kappa'(q_j))$, the reduction in core-graph distance
between $g$'s two cores.

\textbf{\TeleGate{}} contributes a constant direct gain of $1$ (one
remote CX resolved) and no lookahead term.

\textbf{\TeleGatePacket{}} contributes a direct gain equal to the
number of remote CX gates in the collected packet -- every gate in an
accepted packet is current, not speculative, work, since the whole
packet executes under one cat-entangler resource once started.

\textbf{\TeleData{}} decomposes its influence into three tiers, all
computed by simulating the DAG frontier under the hypothetical
post-teleport core assignment (Section~\ref{sec:sfc-simstate}):
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Direct}: front-layer CX gates already involving the moving
  qubit, plus the seed gate itself.
\item \emph{Latent-direct}: CX gates on the moving qubit that appear in
  the simulated DAG front while the simulator can still make virtual
  progress with only 1Q gates and now-local CX gates (i.e.\ gates whose
  post-teleport cores coincide); these gates are current pressure, not
  prediction, because the candidate teleport directly determines their
  post-move core distance the moment they surface.
\item \emph{Lookahead}: CX gates on the moving qubit found in up to
  $L_\mathrm{tp}{=}16$ further simulated DAG layers (capped at
  $N_\mathrm{tp}{=}50$ gates) beyond the point where the latent-direct
  simulation naturally stops (no further gate is virtually
  executable). Layer $k$'s contribution is weighted by
  $\max(w_\mathrm{min}, w_0\gamma^{k-1})$ with $w_0{=}0.9$,
  $\gamma{=}0.9$, $w_\mathrm{min}{=}0.5$.
\end{itemize}
\begin{align}
  \mathrm{direct} &= \sum_{g\in\mathrm{direct}}\!\Delta(g)
    + w_\mathrm{ld}\!\!\sum_{g\in\mathrm{latent}}\!\!\Delta(g), \\
  \mathrm{lookahead} &= \sum_{(g,k)\in\mathrm{la}}\!\!w_k\,\Delta(g),
  \label{eq:sfc-teleport}
\end{align}
with $w_\mathrm{ld}{=}1.0$ (\texttt{teleport\_latent\_direct\_weight}).
A capacity-risk term $1/\max(1,f_\mathrm{dst}{-}1)$ (post-move free
count in the destination core) is tracked alongside the score for use
as a tie-break dimension but does not enter $\mathrm{total}$ directly;
plans are primarily ranked by total gain (descending), then readiness,
then preparation cost, then plan kind (\TeleData{} $\succ$
\TeleGatePacket{} $\succ$ \TeleGate{}), then capacity risk, then
lookahead gain, with a fully deterministic stable key as the final
tie-break before any seeded random choice.

\subsection{Same-Control Packet Collection}
\label{sec:sfc-packet}

Starting from a seed adjacent-remote CX gate $g=(q_c,q_t)$ with
control core $c_c$ and target core $c_t$, \PORTER{} simulates the DAG
frontier forward for up to \texttt{extended\_packet\_window} (120)
layers, greedily absorbing into the packet: (i) any front CX gate
sharing control $q_c$ whose target is also in $c_t$, up to
\texttt{max\_packet\_size} (8) such gates; and (ii) any front 1Q gate
whose qubit is already in $c_t$, since these execute freely while the
packet's cat resource is live. Collection stops at the first layer
that contributes nothing, or once the size cap is reached. Because
this DAG-frontier simulation admits gates from later generations that
are not literally in the (bounded) current DAG front, a packet may
contain gates unreachable by a naively front-limited scan; the
collected order is preserved and replayed exactly at commit time, as
one \texttt{cat\_entangler} (consuming the packet's single EPR pair),
one \texttt{cat\_cx} per member --- each target routed to its
communication port by ordinary local SWAPs, forbidden from touching
the two live ports --- and a closing \texttt{cat\_disentangler}.

\textbf{What \PORTER{} does.} Table~\ref{tab:example-plans} lists the
generated plans. Seeding packet collection at $g_1$, the frontier
simulation absorbs $g_2$ and $g_3$ (same control $\logi{0}$, targets
in $c_1$): a three-gate packet with direct gain $3$. Both single
telegates and all \TeleData{} plans into $c_0$ gain ${\approx}1$.
\TeleData{}($\logi{0}\!\to\!c_1$) would gain more --- direct $1$ for
$g_1$, latent-direct $2$ for $g_2,g_3$, minus $0.9$ of lookahead for
$g_5$, whose partner $\logi{6}$ stays in $c_0$ --- but $c_1$ fails the
$f_\mathrm{min}$ test, so it lands in $\mathcal{B}$; its gain
${\approx}2.1$ does not exceed the packet's $3.0$ by
$m_\mathrm{assist}{=}5$, so no capacity relief pre-empts. Plans are
ranked by gain first --- readiness is only a tie-break --- so the
\emph{unready} packet (gain $3$) beats the \emph{ready} telegate for
$g_4$ (gain $1$). \PORTER{} commits the packet, generates its one
preparation SWAP ($\logi{0}$ next to the port), then emits one
cat-entangler, three cat-CXs (each target routed beside the port in
turn), and the disentangler: \emph{one} EPR pair for $g_1,g_2,g_3$.
The packet's completion exposes $g_4$ and $g_5$ together; the
locality-first stage drains the now-local $g_5$ first (one SWAP plus
a local CX), and the remote stage then re-generates plans for $g_4$:
the single telegate and \TeleData{}($\logi{5}\!\to\!c_0$) now tie at
gain $1$, and the plan-kind tie-break (\TeleData{} $\succ$
\TeleGate{}, Section~\ref{sec:sfc-scoring}) commits the teleport ---
$\logi{5}$ lands on $c_0$'s port and $g_4$ finishes as a local CX.
Two EPR pairs for the whole fragment. This is not a stylised
trace: it is exactly the operation stream the implementation emits
on this instance, replayed and certified by the independent verifier
of \appVerification{}, which includes the full
operation-by-operation certified trace.

\textbf{What a flat pool does.} A router that scores individual
actions --- SWAPs and inter-core operations in one pool --- fires the
zero-cost ready telegate for $g_4$ first. The deeper problem is the
staging SWAP the packet needs: exchanging $\logi{0}$ and $\logi{4}$
moves $g_1$'s control one step closer to the port while moving
$g_4$'s one step away, so under a front-averaged distance metric of
the kind used by \SABRE{} descendants its score is ${\approx}0$; the
information that makes this SWAP worth applying --- three remote CXs
behind $g_1$ amortised onto one EPR pair --- is a property of a
\emph{whole plan}, invisible at single-action granularity. Vocabulary
compounds the gap: $g_2$ and $g_3$ enter the front one at a time, so
\TeleSABRE{} pays one telegate each, four EPR pairs for the fragment;
\dSABRE{}, restricted to \TeleData{}, cannot move $\logi{0}$ into the
saturated $c_1$ without first evicting a resident (its
\texttt{\_force\_make\_room}, one more teleport), and ferrying
$\logi{1},\logi{2},\logi{3}$ out to $c_0$ instead costs three EPR
pairs before $g_4$ is even addressed. Commitment finally protects the
choice once made: only the packet's preparation SWAPs are generated
--- no staging effort is spent on the six losing plans --- and the
committed plan cannot be pre-empted mid-preparation by a candidate
that its own staging transiently improved, a churn a flat pool
permits by construction.

\subsection{Capacity Relief}
\label{sec:sfc-relief}

\textbf{Tail-relief BFS.} When every remote plan is capacity-blocked,
\PORTER{} runs a breadth-first search from each blocked destination core
to the nearest \emph{reservoir} core -- one with at least $f_\mathrm{min}$
free slots -- optionally chained beyond one hop
(\texttt{capacity\_relief\_chain\_enabled}). Every logical qubit
currently in the BFS path's penultimate core becomes a tail-relief
\TeleData{} candidate into the reservoir, scored and selected by the
same plan machinery, grouped and tried in ascending hop-count order.

\textbf{Opportunistic relief.} Even when a normal plan is available,
a severely blocked plan can still win if relieving its target core's
capacity would unlock substantially more gain
(Section~\ref{sec:sfc-plan-first}); this differs from tail-relief BFS
in firing pre-emptively, before any plan is actually starved, whenever
the margin $m_\mathrm{assist}$ is exceeded.

\subsection{Computational Complexity}
\label{sec:sfc-complexity}

Let $N$ be the circuit's gate count, $n$ its logical-qubit count, $K$
the number of cores, and $M$ the per-core physical-qubit count
($P{=}KM$ total physical qubits). Hardware distance tables ($\dL$
per-core, $\dC$ core-graph) are precomputed once at construction, in
$O(KM^2\log M + K^2\log K)$, matching the standard two-level
Dijkstra decomposition used by comparable
routers~\cite{lidsabre}. Per iteration, front classification and
direct-execution draining cost $O(|F|)$; intra-core SWAP scoring
evaluates $O(M)$ candidate edges each at $O(|F_\mathrm{loc}| +
N_\mathrm{loc})$ via one $O(|\text{frontier}|)$-cost DAG snapshot,
reused across candidates; remote-plan scoring evaluates $O(n)$
candidates each at $O(N_\mathrm{tp})$ via the same amortised snapshot
cost. Free-slot-queue maintenance is $O(1)$ amortised per layout
mutation since only the touched core(s) are refreshed
(Section~\ref{sec:perf}). Summing over $N$ iterations gives
$T_\mathrm{total} = O\!\left(N(Mn + K M^2\log M)\right)$ excluding the
one-time precomputation term, in line with the per-iteration cost of
other core-local distributed
routers~\cite{lidsabre,russo2025telesabre}.

\section{Experimental Evaluation}

\subsection{Implementation and Verification}
\label{sec:perf}

\PORTER{} is implemented in Python and is publicly available, together
with the verifier and comparison harness used below
(Section~\ref{sec:intro}). The implementation is fully
deterministic: for a fixed (circuit, hardware, random seed) triple the
emitted operation stream is byte-identical across runs, and every
seeded tie-break is counted in the emitted statistics, so behaviour
preservation across refactorings can be certified by hashing the
serialized operation stream. Compile time is kept low by incremental
data structures --- chiefly the
\texttt{DagFrontierSimulator}\label{sec:sfc-simstate} shared by
extended-set collection and teleport lookahead
(Sections~\ref{sec:sfc-intra}--\ref{sec:sfc-packet}
and~\ref{sec:sfc-scoring}), a copy-on-write view over the live DAG
state that tracks a private overlay of predecessor-count decrements
against the shared \texttt{active}/\texttt{pred\_base} maps, so that a
snapshot costs $O(|\text{frontier}|)$ rather than
$O(|\text{remaining circuit}|)$ and several independent virtual
simulations can run from the same live DAG state without interfering
--- together with incremental DAG-frontier maintenance, cached
hardware distance tables, $O(1)$ free-slot accounting, and
apply/restore SWAP scoring in place of layout cloning. These reduce
wall-clock compile time by roughly $4\times$ relative to a
straightforward implementation of the same algorithm, with
byte-identical output; Section~\ref{sec:time} reports absolute compile
times.

Every reported run is certified by an independent replay verifier
that treats the router as a black box: from the emitted operation
stream, the original circuit, and the initial layout alone it
re-derives gate coverage (each gate exactly once), per-qubit
dependency order, coupling-edge legality, the port and isolation rules
of all three communication primitives, and consistency of the emitted
statistics. The verifier passes on 60 randomized circuits and on every
instance in the evaluation below, and is itself validated by fault
injection; \appVerification{} details its checks, a
certified operation-by-operation trace of the running example, and
the fault-injection study.

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\textbf{Architectures.} We use the same two grid-of-grids topologies
introduced by \TeleSABRE{}~\cite{russo2025telesabre} and re-used by
\dSABRE{}~\cite{lidsabre}: architecture~A, a $2{\times}2$ array of
$3{\times}3$ cores (36 physical qubits, 4 inter-core links), and
architecture~H, a $2{\times}3$ array of $4{\times}4$ cores (96 physical

a call that exceeds it is recorded the same way as any other failure
to complete (missing entry, excluded from that protocol's geometric
mean). This is a harness-level bound applied uniformly across all
three routers and all three protocols below, not specific to any one
circuit.

\subsection{Initial-Layout Protocols}
\label{sec:protocols}

We evaluate under three initial-layout protocols, designed to separate
routing quality from initial-layout quality.

\textbf{Own-protocol comparison.} Each router is first evaluated under
its own established best-practice initial-layout protocol. For the two
baselines this is best-of-three, matching the convention already used
to compare \TeleSABRE{} and \dSABRE{} to each other~\cite{lidsabre};
for \PORTER{} it is a single deterministic run:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{\TeleSABRE{}}: the compiled C binary's built-in hungarian
  initial-layout heuristic, config seeds 0--2, best EPR count of the
  successful runs.
\item \textbf{\dSABRE{}}: Qiskit \texttt{SabreLayout} on the
  corners-removed architecture graph (seeds 0--2, with the two least
  useful corners of \emph{every} core removed --- see below) followed
  by \SABRE{}-style forward\,$\to$\,backward\,$\to$\,forward routing
  passes, best EPR count.
\item \textbf{\PORTER{}}: a \emph{single} routing pass from its single
  deterministic temporal locality-greedy layout
  (Section~\ref{sec:sfc-layout}), router seed~0. \PORTER{} performs no
  layout search and no seed sweep, so its column reports one attempt
  per circuit where each baseline's reports the best of three; every
  number is exactly reproducible from the circuit file alone.
\end{itemize}
\dSABRE{}'s corner removal reserves low-degree physical qubits as
communication slots by excluding them from the SabreLayout coupling
map. Its published selection takes the four lowest-degree vertices of
the \emph{whole-chip} graph; on both of our architectures, whose
physical-qubit ids are assigned core-by-core, these four vertices are,
by construction, the four corners of a single core (core~0 in our
numbering), so the remaining cores receive no reserved slots at all
and regularly start fully packed. We strengthen the baseline's
protocol here: we instead remove, in \emph{every} core, the two
corners farthest (by total shortest-path distance in the chip graph)
from all other physical qubits --- the least useful positions, whose
reservation costs the mapper the least. Two per core is also the
natural budget: removing all four corners of every core would leave
architecture~A only $4\times(9-4)=20$ usable slots, too few for its
25-qubit circuits, whereas two leave $4\times(9-2)=28$ there and
$6\times(16-2)=84$ on architecture~H, enough for the 80-qubit suite.
We still do not reuse these SabreLayout candidates for \PORTER{} in
this comparison, to keep each router's own-protocol result tied to
its own established initial-layout heuristic.

\textbf{Shared-layout comparison.} To isolate routing quality from
initial-layout quality, we additionally route both \dSABRE{} and
\PORTER{} from \TeleSABRE{}'s \emph{own} initial layout: for the winning
seed of the run above, we extract, from \TeleSABRE{}'s optional JSON
trace, the physical-to-logical mapping recorded at iteration~0 -- i.e.\
the layout \TeleSABRE{} itself settled on after its internal
forward/backward/forward layout refinement, immediately before its
accepted forward routing pass -- and feed that identical mapping,
unmodified, to a single \dSABRE{} run and a single \PORTER{} run (no
per-router layout search; the layout is now fixed and given).
\TeleSABRE{}'s hungarian heuristic
reserves free-slot headroom per core by construction (tuned to its own
teleport threshold), so it is compatible with \PORTER{}'s teleport
precondition without adjustment on every circuit we tested.

A third, independent layout source --- a placement generated by
\texttt{pytket-dqc}'s \texttt{HypergraphPartitioning}
allocator~\cite{pytketdqc2024}, routed by both \dSABRE{} and
\PORTER{} --- is reported and discussed in
\appPytket.

Across the three suites the own-protocol comparison supports one
headline claim: \emph{a single \PORTER{} run from its single
deterministic layout posts the lowest geometric-mean EPR count of the
three routers on every suite}, despite each baseline reporting the
best of three attempts -- $43.1$ vs.\ \dSABRE{}'s $47.0$ and
\TeleSABRE{}'s $94.3$ at 25 qubits, $86.4$ vs.\ $89.0$ and $172.1$ at
64, and $14.0$ vs.\ $15.8$ and $40.9$ at 80 (the 80-qubit gmeans are
over the two circuits \TeleSABRE{} completes; the fuller
circuit-by-circuit picture, including six instances on which \dSABRE{}
wins, is below). It is also the only router to complete all eighteen
instances (\dSABRE{} completes seventeen, \TeleSABRE{} thirteen). The
per-suite detail follows.

\textbf{25-qubit suite.} \PORTER{} reduces geometric-mean EPR
consumption by \textbf{54.3\%} against \TeleSABRE{}, with per-circuit
reductions from $-36.6\%$ (Random) to $-66.7\%$ (QNN) and no
regressions. Against \dSABRE{} it wins AE, QFT, QNN, and Random
outright and ties GHZ (both 4) and Graphstate (both 7), conceding
nothing at this scale. On the CX-dense circuits the margin is
clearest (QNN: 119 vs.\ 153; QFT: 74 vs.\ 89), consistent with
\PORTER{}'s \TeleGatePacket{}
primitive amortising runs of shared-control remote CX gates that a
teledata-only router must pay for individually
(Section~\ref{sec:ablation} isolates this effect).

\textbf{64-qubit suite.} \TeleSABRE{} fails to converge within
three seeds only on Random here; on QNN (8{,}126 CX) it succeeds at
1{,}365 EPR pairs and 6{,}292 SWAPs, exactly matching \dSABRE{}'s own
published number for this circuit~\cite{lidsabre} -- a useful
cross-check that our harness reproduces \TeleSABRE{} faithfully. The
gmean row is computed over the five circuits on which \TeleSABRE{}
converges. \PORTER{} improves on \TeleSABRE{} on every converged
circuit ($-36.1\%$ to $-73.7\%$); notably, the chain-structured GHZ
that defeats locally-greedy schedulers generally -- \dSABRE{}
regresses against \TeleSABRE{} on it both in our reproduction (14
vs.\ 11) and in its own published table~\cite{lidsabre} -- is now a
\PORTER{} \emph{win} (6 EPR pairs vs.\ 11 and 14): the
first-appearance visit order of the temporal locality-greedy layout
places successive chain neighbours in the same core, dissolving the
pathology at the layout stage rather than fighting it at routing
time. Against \dSABRE{}, \PORTER{} takes the gmean ($86.4$ vs.\
$89.0$), winning AE (177 vs.\ 262), GHZ, and Random (737 vs.\ 770,
where \TeleSABRE{} completes neither); \dSABRE{} retains Graphstate
(18 vs.\ 20), QFT (258 vs.\ 262, narrowly), and, most clearly, the
deep QNN (509 vs.\ 864). That QNN row is \PORTER{}'s weakest result in any
suite and an honest limit of the single-layout policy: it is the one
benchmark where the deterministic layout is decisively worse than
alternatives \PORTER{} itself can produce (its retired best-of-six
search reached 642 there), suggesting layout sensitivity that a
future refinement pass could recover.

\textbf{80-qubit suite.} \TeleSABRE{} converges on only two of the six
circuits here (GHZ and Graphstate; Table~\ref{tab:main80}), so its
gmean basis is thin. \PORTER{} edges that basis (14.0 vs.\ \dSABRE{}'s
15.8, TeleSABRE's 40.9), driven by GHZ (7 EPR pairs vs.\ 10 and 19).
Completion is the clearer story at this scale: \PORTER{} finishes all
six circuits, \dSABRE{} five (missing Random), \TeleSABRE{} two. Over
the five circuits \dSABRE{} also completes, however, the per-core
corner reservation pays off for the baseline: \PORTER{} wins AE (490
vs.\ 513) and GHZ (7 vs.\ 10) but loses Graphstate (28 vs.\ 25), QFT
(446 vs.\ 349), and, decisively, the deep QNN (1{,}517 vs.\ 869), for
a geometric mean of 145.4 against \dSABRE{}'s 131.2 -- the one
own-protocol basis in our evaluation on which a baseline's
best-of-three beats \PORTER{}'s single run.

The deep QNN deserves emphasis because it corrects a defect in the
original suite: at 80 qubits the stock \texttt{mqt.bench} QNN is a
79-CX chain \emph{identical} to GHZ (\appSuite), which
would have double-counted the easy chain case and hidden the hard one.
The reconstructed 12{,}718-CX QNN is a fully-connected interaction
graph, and on it \PORTER{} loses to \dSABRE{} (1{,}517 vs.\ 869) by the
same mechanism as the 64q QNN (864 vs.\ 509): its single deterministic
layout cannot match \dSABRE{}'s best-of-three SabreLayout partition on
an all-to-all circuit. The two QNN rows are now a consistent, honest
boundary of the single-layout policy -- the regime a future layout- or
route-refinement pass (Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}) most needs to
target.

On Random (23{,}381 CX, by far the largest circuit in any suite),
\PORTER{} is the only router to finish, in 37\,s at 11{,}040 EPR pairs,
while \dSABRE{} exceeds the uniform 900\,s per-call timeout
(Section~\ref{sec:setup}). Running \dSABRE{} past that cap to
characterize the failure shows it is not a deadlock: its
\texttt{max\_iterations=20000} budget (carried over from the 64-qubit
suite per \dSABRE{}'s own scaling guidance~\cite{lidsabre}) is reached
after 938\,s of its first forward pass alone, having cleared 1{,}233 of
the 23{,}381 CX gates with genuine forward progress -- a
resource-budget limitation on an unusually dense circuit
($3$--$18\times$ the CX/qubit ratio of any other benchmark), not an
in-principle inability. Still, the practical cost of a budget large
enough to finish (three layout candidates through up to three passes
each) would run to hours against \PORTER{}'s 37 seconds.

moving a qubit resolves not just the seed gate but its latent and
lookahead successors, so the plan scorer usually prefers it. Single
\TeleGate{}s are almost absent from the structured circuits and
concentrate on Random (93, 228, and 3{,}538 across the three scales)
--- isolated remote CX gates between qubits that share no further work,
exactly the case where entangling once and moving nothing is
cheapest. \TeleGatePacket{}s carry substantial CX volume on the dense

same-control runs nor capacity pressure --- which is why they are
omitted from the mechanism ablation below.

1{,}039). Tail-relief BFS, the last resort when no normal plan exists
at all, fires an order of magnitude less often and almost exclusively
on Random (114 teleports at 80q, 11 at 64q) --- consistent with its
role as the safety net that keeps the deterministic-greedy loop alive
at high occupancy rather than a routine routing device.

\subsection{Shared-Layout Results}
\label{sec:shared_results}

The own-protocol comparison mixes two effects: the quality of each
router's initial layout and the quality of its routing decisions.
Table~\ref{tab:shared} removes the first effect entirely -- both
\dSABRE{} and \PORTER{} route, single-shot, from the identical layout
\TeleSABRE{} built for itself -- so what remains is a pure
routing-quality comparison on a mapping optimised for neither router.

\textbf{25-qubit suite.} Under \TeleSABRE{}'s own initial layout,
both \dSABRE{} and \PORTER{} still improve
substantially on \TeleSABRE{} ($-37.3\%$ and $-40.1\%$ gmean
respectively), with \PORTER{} the better of the two on four of six
circuits (AE, GHZ, QNN, Random) and on the gmean. \dSABRE{} keeps
Graphstate and QFT, the circuits its partition-then-refine strategy
suits best under its own protocol too.

\textbf{64-qubit suite.} \TeleSABRE{}'s initial layout is available
for five of six circuits. \dSABRE{} wins
the gmean here ($-47.9\%$ vs.\ \PORTER{}'s $-37.8\%$): it takes AE,
Graphstate, and QNN outright, ties GHZ (both $-27.3\%$), and is edged
out only narrowly on QFT (228 vs.\ 227 EPR). On architecture~H's
larger, 16-slot cores, \dSABRE{}'s forward--backward--forward routing
passes evidently extract more from a fixed externally supplied layout
than \PORTER{}'s single forward pass does -- the one setting in our
evaluation where they do. \PORTER{} nonetheless wins this suite
end-to-end (Table~\ref{tab:main64}), because its own layout is enough
better than the one being shared here to more than repay the
difference; a backward-refinement pass over \PORTER{}'s route is the
natural mechanism to close the residual gap
(Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}).

\textbf{80-qubit suite.} \TeleSABRE{}'s initial layout can be
extracted only for the two circuits on which it converges (GHZ and
Graphstate), so this block is indicative rather than conclusive.
\PORTER{} edges \dSABRE{} on the two-circuit
gmean ($-31.7\%$ vs.\ $-29.8\%$), winning GHZ (11 vs.\ 15 EPR pairs)
while \dSABRE{} keeps Graphstate. GHZ is a win for \PORTER{} even from
a layout it did not build, so its chain-circuit strength is a property
of the router as well as of its layout.

\textbf{Fixed-layout comparison, pooled.} A second source of
externally supplied layouts --- \texttt{pytket-dqc}'s
\texttt{HypergraphPartitioning} placements, routed by both remaining
routers on the 25q and 64q suites --- is reported in
\appPytket. Pooling that grid with
Table~\ref{tab:shared} gives thirty instances on which \dSABRE{} and
\PORTER{} routed the \emph{identical} externally supplied layout:
\PORTER{} uses fewer EPR pairs on fifteen, \dSABRE{} on twelve, with
three ties, and \PORTER{}'s pooled geometric mean is lower ($73.8$
vs.\ $76.7$). Whatever share of each router's end-to-end result its
own layout contributes, the routing stages themselves are at worst at
parity -- and \PORTER{} reaches that parity in a single forward pass,
without \dSABRE{}'s forward--backward--forward schedule.

raise-on-deadlock failure model (Section~\ref{sec:sfc-plan-first})
from a liability into a non-event: with both tiers active, no
benchmark instance in any protocol deadlocks.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{\TeleSABRE{}}~\cite{russo2025telesabre} is our closest
architectural relative: both routers support teledata and gate
teleportation and both build on the \SABRE{} extended-set scoring
idea. The two differ chiefly in decision structure: \TeleSABRE{}
scores inter-core candidates via a contracted-communication-graph
Dijkstra energy that mixes staging cost and lookahead into one flat
action pool per candidate, whereas \PORTER{} scores remote \emph{plans}
independently of their preparation cost and only generates
preparation SWAPs for the one plan selected
(Section~\ref{sec:sfc-plan-first}), and adds a same-control packet
primitive that amortises runs of shared-control remote CX gates onto
one EPR pair. \PORTER{}'s capacity handling is also more proactive:
alongside \TeleSABRE{}'s safety-valve iteration cap, \PORTER{} maintains
an explicit blocked-plan set and both a breadth-first tail-relief
search and an opportunistic pre-emptive relief comparator
(Section~\ref{sec:sfc-relief}).

\textbf{\dSABRE{}}~\cite{lidsabre} (we compare against its default
\texttt{dSABRE\_BurstExt} variant, ``\dSABRE{}'') is the stronger of
the two baselines and the closest competitor on EPR count. Its
strategy inverts \PORTER{}'s cost profile: a SabreLayout-based initial
allocation refined through forward--backward--forward routing passes,
run over three seeds, buys excellent core partitions -- it remains the
strongest router on the sparse, highly partitionable Graphstate family
at 64 and 80 qubits in every protocol we test -- at a compile-time
cost that is $11$--$61\times$
\PORTER{}'s per attempt (Section~\ref{sec:time}). Under each router's
own protocol \PORTER{}'s single run posts the lower geometric-mean EPR
on all three suites (Section~\ref{sec:main_results}), and across
thirty instances routed from identical externally supplied
layouts, \PORTER{} wins fifteen to \dSABRE{}'s twelve
(Section~\ref{sec:shared_results}); \dSABRE{}'s remaining advantage is
concentrated in its multi-pass refinement of an externally supplied
layout on large cores (Section~\ref{sec:shared_results}) and on the
densest own-protocol instances -- the all-to-all QNN at 64q and 80q
and the 80q QFT -- where its best-of-three
partition beats \PORTER{}'s single deterministic layout. \dSABRE{}
restricts itself to teledata; \PORTER{}
follows \TeleSABRE{} in also supporting gate teleportation and extends
it with same-control packets, which matters on circuits with long runs
of shared-control remote CX gates (e.g.\ QFT-like phase-kickback
patterns) where amortising many remote CX gates onto one EPR pair can
outweigh a less-optimal core partition
(Section~\ref{sec:ablation}).

\textbf{Single-QPU qubit mapping.} The front+extended-set scoring
loop~\PORTER{} and both baselines build on originates with
\SABRE{}~\cite{li2019tackling}; \LightSABRE{}~\cite{zou2024lightsabrelightweightenhancedsabre}
formalises the $\Delta$-form SWAP score \PORTER{} reuses for its
intra-core stage (Eq.~\ref{eq:sfc-local}) and inter-core direct-gain
term (Eq.~\ref{eq:sfc-teleport}).

\textbf{Allocation and scheduling.} Hypergraph-partitioning allocation
methods~\cite{andres2019automated,pytketdqc2024} and time-aware
schedulers~\cite{baker2020time,ferrari2021compiler} address the
upstream allocation and downstream scheduling stages respectively.
Both remain complementary to \PORTER{}: its own temporal
locality-greedy allocation is a one-pass greedy heuristic, not a
global optimiser, and \appPytket{} shows a
hypergraph partition can still improve the most partitionable
circuits when its generation cost is acceptable.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We presented \PORTER{}, a plan-committed router for multi-core
distributed quantum computers whose central design choice is to
separate remote-plan \emph{selection} from remote-plan
\emph{preparation}, in contrast to \TeleSABRE{} and \dSABRE{}, which
score inter-core candidates and their staging SWAPs together in one
flat action pool: every legal teleport, telegate, and same-control
packet candidate (including capacity-blocked variants) is scored on
its physical and DAG-lookahead merits before any core-preparation SWAP
is generated, and preparation effort is then spent exclusively on the
one plan chosen. The router is paired with a two-tier capacity-relief
mechanism (breadth-first tail relief when every plan is blocked,
opportunistic pre-emptive relief when one is severely blocked) and a
deterministic, seed-free temporal locality-greedy initial layout that
co-locates qubits by temporally discounted interaction weight in
first-use order.

The combination is deliberately minimal -- one layout, one forward
pass, no seed sweep, no rollback -- and the evaluation shows the
minimalism costs nothing on this benchmark family: a single \PORTER{}
run posts the lowest geometric-mean EPR count of the three routers on
all three suites ($54.3\%$, $49.8\%$, and $65.8\%$ below \TeleSABRE{};
below \dSABRE{}'s best-of-three on each suite), completes all eighteen
benchmarks where \dSABRE{} completes seventeen and \TeleSABRE{}
thirteen, and runs $11$--$61\times$ faster per attempt than
\dSABRE{}. Across thirty instances routed from identical externally
supplied layouts, \PORTER{}'s single forward pass matches or beats
\dSABRE{}'s forward--backward--forward schedule (fifteen wins to
twelve). The ablation attributes the gains concretely: packets save up
to $42\%$ EPR on shared-control-heavy circuits, and capacity relief is
what makes the deterministic-greedy design complete at all at 80
qubits.
At the largest scale we tested (a 23{,}381-CX circuit), \PORTER{}
completed in 37\,s where both baselines exceed a uniform 900\,s
per-call compiler timeout -- for \dSABRE{} a resource-budget
limitation rather than a deadlock (Section~\ref{sec:main_results}),
but a practical throughput gap of two orders of magnitude
nonetheless. Every reported run was certified by an independent
replay verifier that re-derives, from the emitted operation stream
alone, gate coverage, dependency order, and physical legality.

Two limits are equally concrete. \dSABRE{} remains the strongest
router for extreme partitionable structure (Graphstate at 64 and 80
qubits, in every protocol),
and its best-of-three SabreLayout partition beats \PORTER{}'s single
deterministic layout on the densest instances: the deep 64q and
80q QNN rows (864 vs.\ 509 and 1{,}517 vs.\ 869 EPR pairs) and the
80q QFT (446 vs.\ 349) are
\PORTER{}'s weakest results and a consistent boundary of the
single-layout policy. Natural next steps follow directly: a
backward-refinement pass over \PORTER{}'s single forward route, a
lightweight exchange-refinement of the temporal locality-greedy
partition targeting the dense-QNN case, and extending the packet
primitive beyond the shared-control case.

independent replay verifier, the cross-router comparison harness
(including the patched \TeleSABRE{} binary and its documentation), and
raw per-circuit JSON results are publicly available at
\url{https://github.com/ebony72/porter}. Appendices A--D
(the 80-qubit benchmark reconstruction, the pytket-dqc layout
