\begin{abstract}
We present \SFC{}, a \SABRE{}-style router for multi-core distributed
quantum computers (DQCs) that unifies the three inter-core communication
primitives used in prior work --- state teleportation (\TeleData{}),
single-gate teleportation (\TeleGate{}), and multi-gate cat-entangler
packets (\TeleGatePacket{}) --- under one plan-first decision loop.  At
each iteration the router drains directly executable gates, resolves
intra-core routing with a \SABRE{}-style extended-set SWAP score, and,
only when the DAG front contains solely inter-core gates, scores every
legal remote plan (teleport, telegate, and same-control packet
candidates together with capacity-blocked variants) \emph{before}
committing to any core-preparation SWAPs, so that preparation effort is
never spent on a plan that is not selected.  A breadth-first
capacity-relief search evacuates the nearest reservoir core when the
best remote plan is blocked by insufficient free capacity, and an
opportunistic-relief comparator lets a severely blocked plan pre-empt a
merely-good normal plan when the margin is large enough.  We audit the
reference implementation with an independent replay verifier across 60
randomized circuits and the full MQT-Bench evaluation suite, apply a
set of behaviour-preserving algorithmic-complexity fixes that yield a
$4\times$ compile-time speedup with byte-identical output, and evaluate
against \TeleSABRE{} and \dSABRE{} on the same physical topologies at
25, 64, and 80 logical qubits, plus a third initial-layout source
generated by \texttt{pytket-dqc}'s hypergraph partitioner. In the
process we identify and patch a latent out-of-bounds bug in
\TeleSABRE{}'s reference C implementation that crashes it on
small-core architectures, and a filename-handling bug in its argument
parser that had caused an earlier version of this evaluation to
under-report its convergence. Under each router's own best-practice
initial-layout protocol, \SFC{} reduces geometric-mean EPR consumption
by $42.1\%$ against \TeleSABRE{} on the 25-qubit suite and by $16.7\%$
on the 64-qubit suite; it trails \dSABRE{} under this protocol,
particularly on sparse and chain-structured circuits. Feeding both
\dSABRE{} and \SFC{} \TeleSABRE{}'s own initial layout isolates
routing quality from layout quality: on the 25-qubit suite this
reverses the ranking ($-40.1\%$ vs.\ \TeleSABRE{} for \SFC{}, $-37.3\%$
for \dSABRE{}), indicating that part of \dSABRE{}'s own-protocol
advantage there traces to its SabreLayout-based initial allocation
rather than to routing-time decisions alone; on the 64-qubit suite
\dSABRE{} keeps the lead under the shared layout too ($-47.9\%$ vs.\
$-37.8\%$), so the layout-versus-routing split of that advantage is
architecture-dependent. At larger scale, \SFC{} shows a distinct
advantage in completing circuits the other two cannot: on an
80-qubit, 23{,}381-CX random circuit, \SFC{} completes in 40\,s where
\TeleSABRE{} fails to converge and \dSABRE{}'s reference
implementation exhausts a 20{,}000-iteration budget (carried over,
unincreased, from the 64-qubit suite) after already spending 976.8\,s
and placing 7{,}713 EPR pairs on its first of up to nine required
routing passes -- a resource-budget limitation on this unusually dense
circuit rather than a deadlock. Code and raw results are available in
the accompanying repository.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Distributed quantum computers (DQCs) interconnect several small,
high-fidelity quantum processing units (QPUs, or \emph{cores}) with
inter-core links that support remote entanglement
generation~\cite{cuomo2020towards}, offering a near-term path to scale
beyond what a single monolithic chip can host.  Compiling a circuit
onto a DQC decomposes into three coupled sub-problems~\cite{escofet2024review}:
\emph{(i)} statically assigning logical qubits to cores, \emph{(ii)}
choosing, for each non-local two-qubit gate, which communication
primitive realises it, and \emph{(iii)} routing and scheduling the
resulting sequence of intra-core SWAPs and inter-core operations
subject to communication-port capacity.  Every communication
primitive consumes one or more EPR pairs, and EPR generation is slow,
noisy, and rate-limited on current
hardware~\cite{stephenson2020high,daiss2021quantum}, making EPR
minimisation the dominant compilation objective.

\SFC{} addresses stage~(iii): given a fixed initial layout, it orders
intra-core SWAPs and inter-core operations to minimise EPR
consumption.  Unlike \dSABRE{}~\cite{lidsabre}, which restricts itself
to state teleportation (\TeleData{}), \SFC{} follows
\TeleSABRE{}~\cite{russo2025telesabre} in also supporting gate
teleportation (\TeleGate{}) via the cat-entangler
protocol~\cite{eisert2000optimal,yimsiriwattana2004generalized}, and
extends it with a same-control \emph{packet} primitive
(\TeleGatePacket{}) that amortises one EPR pair over an ordered run of
consecutive remote CX gates sharing a control qubit, in the spirit of
gate-sequence amortisation used by AutoComm~\cite{wu2022autocomm}.

The router departs from prior \SABRE{}-style distributed routers in
\emph{when} it commits to core-preparation SWAPs. Both
\TeleSABRE{} and \dSABRE{} score inter-core candidates and their
staging SWAPs together in one flat action pool.  \SFC{} instead
separates the decision into two phases: first, every legal remote
\emph{plan} --- a teleport direction, a single telegate, a same-control
packet, or a capacity-blocked variant of any of these --- is scored
using only its physical and DAG-lookahead properties, independent of
how many preparation SWAPs it would take to make it physically legal
right now; only \emph{after} one plan is selected does the router
generate and apply preparation SWAPs specific to that plan, repeating
until the plan becomes physically ready or no further preparation SWAP
improves it. This plan-first ordering means preparation effort is
never spent scoring or partially staging a remote action that a
better one will preempt.

\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A plan-first remote-action loop} that separates
  candidate scoring from preparation-SWAP generation
  (Section~\ref{sec:sfc-plan-first}), unifying teleport, telegate, and
  same-control packet primitives under one direct/latent/lookahead
  gain decomposition (Section~\ref{sec:sfc-scoring}).
\item \textbf{A two-tier capacity-relief mechanism}: a breadth-first
  search for the nearest reservoir core when a plan is capacity-blocked
  (Section~\ref{sec:sfc-relief}), plus an opportunistic comparator that
  lets a severely blocked candidate pre-empt a merely-good normal one.
\item \textbf{An independent replay verifier}
  (Section~\ref{sec:verification}) that re-executes the emitted
  operation stream against its own physical-layout simulation and
  checks gate coverage, DAG-order faithfulness, and every physical
  legality constraint (coupling-edge adjacency, port occupancy,
  cat-section isolation), used to certify correctness across 60
  randomized circuits and the full evaluation suite.
\item \textbf{A set of behaviour-preserving performance fixes} ---
  incremental DAG-frontier maintenance, copy-on-write DAG-simulation
  snapshots, $O(1)$ free-slot accounting, and apply/restore local-SWAP
  scoring in place of layout cloning --- that cut compile time
  $4\times$ on our suite with byte-identical output, verified by
  hashing the serialized operation stream before and after
  (Section~\ref{sec:perf}).
\item \textbf{A head-to-head evaluation} against \TeleSABRE{} and
  \dSABRE{} on identical physical topologies (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}),
  in the course of which we discovered and patched a latent
  out-of-bounds write in \TeleSABRE{}'s reference C implementation
  that segfaults on cores smaller than its original $4\times4$ design
  point (Section~\ref{sec:telesabre-bug}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Quantum Circuits and Routing}
\label{sec:qcircuits}

A quantum circuit acts on $n$ logical qubits through a sequence of
gates; we work in the restricted basis $\{\texttt{id}, \texttt{rz},
\texttt{sx}, \texttt{x}, \texttt{cx}\}$ used by MQT-Bench's
Qiskim-opt3-transpiled circuits, i.e.\ arbitrary one-qubit (1Q) gates
plus the two-qubit (2Q) CX gate. A circuit is represented as a
directed acyclic graph (DAG): for every logical qubit, consecutive
gates acting on it are connected by a dependency edge. The
\emph{front} (or \emph{DAG front}) is the set of gates with no
unexecuted predecessor; only front gates can execute or be routed
towards execution at a given step.

Physical qubits are partitioned into \emph{cores}; each core has an
intra-core coupling graph, and pairs of cores may be connected by an
inter-core link whose two endpoints are designated \emph{communication
ports}.  A \emph{layout} maps every logical qubit to a physical
position.  A 2Q gate is \emph{local} if both operands share a core and
\emph{remote} otherwise. Routing a local gate that is not yet adjacent
requires intra-core SWAPs; routing a remote gate requires an inter-core
communication primitive.

\subsection{Communication Primitives}
\label{sec:primitives}

\textbf{State teleportation (\TeleData{}).} Consumes one EPR pair to
move a logical qubit's state from a physical position adjacent to a
free source-side communication port to the (necessarily free)
destination-side port in a neighbouring
core~\cite{bennett1993teleporting}. The qubit then participates in
local gates in its new core.

\textbf{Gate teleportation (\TeleGate{}).} Executes a single remote CX
directly across two adjacent cores via a cat-entangler
protocol~\cite{eisert2000optimal,yimsiriwattana2004generalized}: one
EPR pair entangles the two communication ports, the remote CX is
applied using the target-side port as a proxy for the control qubit,
and the entanglement is consumed on disentangling. Neither logical
qubit physically moves.

\textbf{Same-control packets (\TeleGatePacket{}).} When several
consecutive remote CX gates in the DAG front share the same control
qubit and land in the same target core, \SFC{} amortises them onto a
\emph{single} cat-entangler resource: the entangling and disentangling
steps bracket an ordered sequence of remote CX and target-core 1Q
gates, all executed while the two communication ports stay reserved.
This is the same amortisation idea used by
AutoComm~\cite{wu2022autocomm}, specialised to the shared-control case
that arises naturally at a \SABRE{}-style DAG front.

\subsection{SABRE}
\label{sec:sabre}

\SABRE{}~\cite{li2019tackling} routes a single-core circuit by
repeatedly selecting the SWAP that minimises a front-layer distance
term plus a weighted extended-set lookahead term, both expressed as
$\Delta$ (old distance minus new distance) on gates sharing a qubit
with the candidate SWAP. \SFC{} inherits this scoring form for its
intra-core stage (Section~\ref{sec:sfc-intra}) and extends the same
direct/lookahead decomposition to its inter-core plan scorer
(Section~\ref{sec:sfc-scoring}).

\section{The \SFC{} Router}
\label{sec:sfc}

\SFC{} is implemented as \texttt{DistributedRouter.compile} in
\texttt{distributed\_router.py} (2{,}800 lines).  Given a circuit and
an initial layout, it repeats the following loop
(Algorithm~\ref{alg:sfc}) until every gate has been emitted or a
configurable iteration budget is exhausted.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\SFC{} \texttt{compile}$(C, \ell_0)$}
\label{alg:sfc}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE $\ell \leftarrow \ell_0$; initialise DAG active/frontier sets from $C$
\WHILE{DAG has active gates}
  \STATE $F \leftarrow$ DAG front (bounded by \texttt{dag\_front\_limit})
  \STATE classify $F$ into singles, direct-local CX, local-CX-needing-SWAP,
         adjacent-remote CX, non-adjacent-remote CX \hfill\textit{(Sec.~\ref{sec:sfc-classify})}
  \IF{singles or direct-local CX present}
    \STATE execute them all; remove from DAG; \textbf{continue}
  \ENDIF
  \IF{local-CX-needing-SWAP present}
    \STATE generate candidate intra-core SWAPs, apply the best-scoring one
           (Eq.~\ref{eq:sfc-local}); \textbf{continue} \hfill\textit{(Sec.~\ref{sec:sfc-intra})}
  \ENDIF
  \STATE generate normal remote plans $\mathcal{P}$ and blocked-teleport
         plans $\mathcal{B}$ \hfill\textit{(Sec.~\ref{sec:sfc-scoring})}
  \IF{$\mathcal{P}\neq\emptyset$}
    \STATE $p^\ast \leftarrow$ best ready-or-preparable plan in $\mathcal{P}$
    \IF{some $b\in\mathcal{B}$ has gain $\gg$ gain$(p^\ast)$}
      \STATE $p^\ast \leftarrow$ best opportunistic capacity-relief plan for $b$
             \hfill\textit{(Sec.~\ref{sec:sfc-relief})}
    \ENDIF
  \ELSE
    \STATE BFS from each blocked target core to its nearest reservoir;
           $p^\ast \leftarrow$ best tail-relief plan \hfill\textit{(Sec.~\ref{sec:sfc-relief})}
  \ENDIF
  \IF{$p^\ast$ is \texttt{None}} \STATE \textbf{report deadlock; abort} \ENDIF
  \STATE apply $p^\ast$'s own preparation SWAPs until ready, then execute it
\ENDWHILE
\RETURN emitted operations, final layout, stats
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Front Classification}
\label{sec:sfc-classify}

Every gate $g$ in the (size-capped) DAG front is classified under the
current layout $\ell$: 1Q gates are always directly executable; a CX
gate $g=(q_i,q_j)$ with $\kappa_\ell(q_i)=\kappa_\ell(q_j)=c$ (same
core) is \emph{direct-local} if $\dL(c,\ell(q_i),\ell(q_j))=1$ and
\emph{local-needing-SWAP} otherwise; a CX gate with
$\kappa_\ell(q_i)\ne\kappa_\ell(q_j)$ is \emph{adjacent-remote} if the
two cores are core-graph neighbours and \emph{non-adjacent-remote}
otherwise. Directly executable gates are always drained first and
completely, so remote-stage decisions are only made once the front
contains solely gates that need routing.

\subsection{Intra-Core SWAP Scoring}
\label{sec:sfc-intra}

For a local-needing-SWAP gate set $F_\mathrm{loc}$, \SFC{} builds
candidate SWAP edges from the coupling-graph neighbours of every
physical position touched by $F_\mathrm{loc}$, simulates each SWAP,
and scores it as
\begin{equation}
  J(a) = \bar d_{\mathcal{F}}(a) + \lambda_\mathrm{loc}\,\bar d_{\mathcal{E}}(a),
  \qquad \lambda_\mathrm{loc}=0.8,
  \label{eq:sfc-local}
\end{equation}
the mean post-SWAP local distance over $F_\mathrm{loc}$ plus a
weighted mean over an extended set $\mathcal{E}_\mathrm{loc}$: up to
$N_\mathrm{loc}{=}20$ local CX gates collected from the next
$L_\mathrm{loc}{=}12$ DAG layers via a virtual frontier simulation
(Section~\ref{sec:sfc-simstate}). Ties are broken first by the raw
front-layer distance, then, if still tied, by a decision uniformly
sampled from a seeded random-number generator, with every seeded
tie-break recorded in \texttt{RouterStats} for reproducibility
auditing.

\subsection{Plan-First Remote Stage}
\label{sec:sfc-plan-first}

When the front contains only remote CX gates, \SFC{} generates two
disjoint candidate sets from the current layout:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Normal plans} $\mathcal{P}$: for every adjacent-remote CX
  gate, a single \TeleGate{}; if a same-control run of $\ge 2$ remote
  CX gates can be collected by simulating the DAG front forward
  (Section~\ref{sec:sfc-packet}), a \TeleGatePacket{}; and, for either
  endpoint, a \TeleData{} plan into the partner's core \emph{if} that
  core already has at least \texttt{min\_free\_before\_teleport}
  ($f_\mathrm{min}{=}2$) free physical positions. For non-adjacent-remote
  CX gates, only distance-reducing \TeleData{} hops (one core-graph
  step closer to the gate's other endpoint) with sufficient destination
  capacity are generated.
\item \textbf{Blocked plans} $\mathcal{B}$: the same directional
  \TeleData{} candidates, but for destination cores that currently fail
  the $f_\mathrm{min}$ capacity test. These are never selected
  directly; they exist to detect when \emph{no} normal plan exists
  because every candidate destination is saturated, or when a normal
  plan is technically available but a severely blocked plan promises
  much higher gain if capacity is relieved first.
\end{itemize}

Each plan is scored (Section~\ref{sec:sfc-scoring}) \emph{before} any
preparation SWAP for it is generated. The router then:
\begin{enumerate}
\item selects the best plan in $\mathcal{P}$ that is either already
  physically ready or admits at least one improving preparation SWAP
  right now (Section~\ref{sec:sfc-prep});
\item compares its gain against the best plan in $\mathcal{B}$; if the
  blocked plan's gain exceeds the selected normal plan's by more than
  a fixed margin $m_\mathrm{assist}{=}5.0$, the router instead searches
  for an \emph{opportunistic capacity-relief} plan for the blocked
  plan's saturated destination core (Section~\ref{sec:sfc-relief}) and,
  if one exists, executes that instead;
\item if $\mathcal{P}=\emptyset$ (every remote CX gate is
  non-adjacent), the router runs the same breadth-first capacity-relief
  search directly on $\mathcal{B}$'s destination cores, grouped by hop
  count, taking the shortest-hop group with a viable candidate.
\end{enumerate}
Once a plan is committed, the router repeatedly generates and applies
\emph{only that plan's} preparation SWAPs (Section~\ref{sec:sfc-prep})
until it becomes ready, no further improving preparation SWAP exists,
or the full decision state (plan identity, layout, RNG state) repeats
--- the latter raising a hard error rather than looping forever, bounded
by \texttt{max\_prep\_iterations\_per\_plan} ($10^4$).

\subsection{Plan Scoring: Direct, Latent, and Lookahead Gain}
\label{sec:sfc-scoring}

Every plan's score is $\mathrm{total} = \mathrm{direct} +
\mathrm{lookahead}$, computed differently per plan kind but sharing
one $\Delta$-form convention: for a CX gate $g=(q_i,q_j)$ and a
candidate qubit relocation, $\Delta(g) = \dC(\kappa(q_i),\kappa(q_j))
- \dC(\kappa'(q_i),\kappa'(q_j))$, the reduction in core-graph distance
between $g$'s two cores.

\textbf{\TeleGate{}} contributes a constant direct gain of $1$ (one
remote CX resolved) and no lookahead term.

\textbf{\TeleGatePacket{}} contributes a direct gain equal to the
number of remote CX gates in the collected packet -- every gate in an
accepted packet is current, not speculative, work, since the whole
packet executes under one cat-entangler resource once started.

\textbf{\TeleData{}} decomposes its influence into three tiers, all
computed by simulating the DAG frontier under the hypothetical
post-teleport core assignment (Section~\ref{sec:sfc-simstate}):
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Direct}: front-layer CX gates already involving the moving
  qubit, plus the seed gate itself.
\item \emph{Latent-direct}: CX gates on the moving qubit that appear in
  the simulated DAG front while the simulator can still make virtual
  progress with only 1Q gates and now-local CX gates (i.e.\ gates whose
  post-teleport cores coincide); these gates are current pressure, not
  prediction, because the candidate teleport directly determines their
  post-move core distance the moment they surface.
\item \emph{Lookahead}: CX gates on the moving qubit found in up to
  $L_\mathrm{tp}{=}16$ further simulated DAG layers (capped at
  $N_\mathrm{tp}{=}50$ gates) beyond the point where the latent-direct
  simulation naturally stops (no further gate is virtually
  executable). Layer $k$'s contribution is weighted by
  $\max(w_\mathrm{min}, w_0\gamma^{k-1})$ with $w_0{=}0.9$,
  $\gamma{=}0.9$, $w_\mathrm{min}{=}0.5$.
\end{itemize}
\begin{align}
  \mathrm{direct} &= \sum_{g\in\mathrm{direct}}\!\Delta(g)
    + w_\mathrm{ld}\!\!\sum_{g\in\mathrm{latent}}\!\!\Delta(g), \\
  \mathrm{lookahead} &= \sum_{(g,k)\in\mathrm{la}}\!\!w_k\,\Delta(g),
  \label{eq:sfc-teleport}
\end{align}
with $w_\mathrm{ld}{=}1.0$ (\texttt{teleport\_latent\_direct\_weight}).
A capacity-risk term $1/\max(1,f_\mathrm{dst}{-}1)$ (post-move free
count in the destination core) is tracked alongside the score for use
as a tie-break dimension but does not enter $\mathrm{total}$ directly;
plans are primarily ranked by total gain (descending), then readiness,
then preparation cost, then plan kind (\TeleData{} $\succ$
\TeleGatePacket{} $\succ$ \TeleGate{}), then capacity risk, then
lookahead gain, with a fully deterministic stable key as the final
tie-break before any seeded random choice.

\subsection{Same-Control Packet Collection}
\label{sec:sfc-packet}

Starting from a seed adjacent-remote CX gate $g=(q_c,q_t)$ with
control core $c_c$ and target core $c_t$, \SFC{} simulates the DAG
frontier forward for up to \texttt{extended\_packet\_window} (120)
layers, greedily absorbing into the packet: (i) any front CX gate
sharing control $q_c$ whose target is also in $c_t$, up to
\texttt{max\_packet\_size} (8) such gates; and (ii) any front 1Q gate
whose qubit is already in $c_t$, since these execute freely while the
packet's cat resource is live. Collection stops at the first layer
that contributes nothing, or once the size cap is reached. Because
this DAG-frontier simulation admits gates from later generations that
are not literally in the (bounded) current DAG front, a packet may
contain gates unreachable by a naively front-limited scan; the
resulting execution order is preserved and replayed exactly at commit
time (Section~\ref{sec:sfc-apply}).

\subsection{Preparation SWAPs}
\label{sec:sfc-prep}

A committed plan's readiness requirements are exactly two
\emph{(core, neighbour-core, endpoint)} triples (source and
destination for \TeleData{}; control and target for \TeleGate{}/
\TeleGatePacket{}, with \TeleGatePacket{}'s target-side routing
deferred to execution time since each packet member routes
independently). For each unmet requirement, candidate preparation
SWAPs are restricted to edges incident to either the plan's logical
endpoint or a nearest free physical position tied for minimum distance
to the relevant communication port (only while that port remains
occupied); each candidate is scored by the post-SWAP value of the same
readiness-cost function used to rank blocked plans
(Section~\ref{sec:sfc-scoring}), and the router applies at most one
improving SWAP per core side per pass, repeating until the plan is
ready.

\subsection{Capacity Relief}
\label{sec:sfc-relief}

\textbf{Tail-relief BFS.} When every remote plan is capacity-blocked,
\SFC{} runs a breadth-first search from each blocked destination core
to the nearest \emph{reservoir} core -- one with at least $f_\mathrm{min}$
free slots -- optionally chained beyond one hop
(\texttt{capacity\_relief\_chain\_enabled}). Every logical qubit
currently in the BFS path's penultimate core becomes a tail-relief
\TeleData{} candidate into the reservoir, scored and selected by the
same plan machinery, grouped and tried in ascending hop-count order.

\textbf{Opportunistic relief.} Even when a normal plan is available,
a severely blocked plan can still win if relieving its target core's
capacity would unlock substantially more gain
(Section~\ref{sec:sfc-plan-first}); this differs from tail-relief BFS
in firing pre-emptively, before any plan is actually starved, whenever
the margin $m_\mathrm{assist}$ is exceeded.

\subsection{Virtual DAG-Frontier Simulation}
\label{sec:sfc-simstate}

Extended-set collection (Sections~\ref{sec:sfc-intra}--\ref{sec:sfc-packet})
and teleport lookahead (Section~\ref{sec:sfc-scoring}) share one
primitive, \texttt{DagFrontierSimulator}: a copy-on-write view over the
live DAG state that tracks a private \emph{overlay} of predecessor-count
decrements against the live \texttt{active} and \texttt{pred\_base}
maps (shared read-only), so that snapshotting costs $O(|\text{frontier}|)$
rather than $O(|\text{remaining circuit}|)$. Removing a batch of gate
IDs from a snapshot updates the overlay and exposes any successor whose
overlay count reaches zero as newly virtual-frontier. Because the
overlay is private to one snapshot, several independent virtual
simulations can run from the same live DAG state without interfering
(Section~\ref{sec:perf} discusses the performance impact of this
design versus a copying implementation).

\subsection{Applying Committed Operations}
\label{sec:sfc-apply}

Local SWAPs and CX gates update the layout in place. Teleports move a
logical qubit's physical/core mapping and refresh the affected cores'
free-slot queues. Single telegates and packets emit a
\texttt{cat\_entangler} operation (consuming one EPR pair, marking the
two communication ports live), followed by one \texttt{cat\_cx} per
remote CX (packets additionally route the target qubit to its
communication port with ordinary local SWAPs, forbidden from touching
the two live ports), and close with a \texttt{cat\_disentangler}.
Final circuit depth is computed by a physical-qubit timeline pass
matching \TeleSABRE{}'s convention: 1 time unit per local operation, 5
per teleport or (dis)entangler pair, applied per touched physical
qubit and taking the maximum over all qubits.

\subsection{Computational Complexity}
\label{sec:sfc-complexity}

Let $N$ be the circuit's gate count, $n$ its logical-qubit count, $K$
the number of cores, and $M$ the per-core physical-qubit count
($P{=}KM$ total physical qubits). Hardware distance tables ($\dL$
per-core, $\dC$ core-graph) are precomputed once at construction, in
$O(KM^2\log M + K^2\log K)$, matching the standard two-level
Dijkstra decomposition used by comparable
routers~\cite{lidsabre}. Per iteration, front classification and
direct-execution draining cost $O(|F|)$; intra-core SWAP scoring
evaluates $O(M)$ candidate edges each at $O(|F_\mathrm{loc}| +
N_\mathrm{loc})$ via one $O(|\text{frontier}|)$-cost DAG snapshot,
reused across candidates; remote-plan scoring evaluates $O(n)$
candidates each at $O(N_\mathrm{tp})$ via the same amortised snapshot
cost. Free-slot-queue maintenance is $O(1)$ amortised per layout
mutation since only the touched core(s) are refreshed
(Section~\ref{sec:perf}). Summing over $N$ iterations gives
$T_\mathrm{total} = O\!\left(N(Mn + K M^2\log M)\right)$ excluding the
one-time precomputation term, in line with the per-iteration cost of
other core-local \SABRE{}-style distributed
routers~\cite{lidsabre,russo2025telesabre}.

%%────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Correctness Verification}
\label{sec:verification}

Beyond code review, we built an independent replay verifier
(\texttt{verify\_distributedrouter.py}) that treats the router as a
black box: it takes only the emitted \texttt{CompiledOp} stream, the
original circuit, and the initial layout, and re-derives, from
scratch, whether the stream is a faithful and physically legal
execution. The verifier maintains its own physical-position map and a
per-logical-qubit FIFO queue of pending gate IDs (derived directly
from the circuit, independent of any router-internal DAG state), and
checks, per emitted operation:
\begin{itemize}
\item every gate is popped from the head of \emph{every} one of its
  operand qubits' queues before being marked executed, so gates cannot
  execute out of DAG order or twice;
\item every \texttt{local\_swap}/\texttt{local\_cx} acts on a genuine
  intra-core coupling edge, at the physical positions the verifier's
  own simulated layout says the operand logical qubits occupy;
\item every \texttt{teleport} uses the correct inter-core port pair for
  its two cores, both ports were free beforehand, and the source
  qubit was coupling-adjacent to the source port;
\item every \texttt{cat\_entangler}, \texttt{cat\_cx}, and
  \texttt{cat\_disentangler} triple observes port-adjacency and
  free-port preconditions, and no other operation touches either
  communication port while a cat section is open;
\item the emitted \texttt{RouterStats} counters (EPR, teleport,
  telegate, packet, SWAP, local-CX, single counts) match the counts
  the verifier itself derives from the op stream, catching any
  bookkeeping drift between what is emitted and what is counted.
\end{itemize}
We ran the verifier against 60 randomized circuits (4--40 logical
qubits, 20--600 gates, both architectures, seeds 0--59) and against
every circuit in the evaluation suite (Section~\ref{sec:experiments});
all pass. An earlier version of the verifier surfaced a genuine
accounting bug in our own harness -- packet telegates were being
double-counted against the single-telegate stat -- which was fixed
before any performance work began, and every subsequent optimisation
(Section~\ref{sec:perf}) was re-verified end to end.

\section{Performance Engineering}
\label{sec:perf}

Profiling the unoptimised router on the 64-qubit suite (Section
\ref{sec:experiments}) attributed the majority of wall-clock time to
two sources: re-copying the entire remaining-circuit DAG state on every
extended-set or teleport-lookahead snapshot ($\sim$55\% of total time
on the deepest circuit), and re-scanning \texttt{state.remaining} for
the zero-predecessor front on every iteration ($\sim$13\%). We applied
five behaviour-preserving changes:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Copy-on-write DAG snapshots} (Section~\ref{sec:sfc-simstate}):
  a snapshot shares the live active-gate set and predecessor counts
  read-only and only writes to a private overlay, so its cost scales
  with the size of the frontier being explored rather than the number
  of gates left in the whole circuit.
\item \textbf{Incremental DAG-front maintenance}: the router now
  maintains \texttt{RouterState.dag\_frontier} as gates are removed,
  rather than recomputing it by filtering the full remaining-gate list
  every iteration; \texttt{state.remaining} itself is now synced lazily,
  only at the three points where the caller actually reads it.
\item \textbf{Cached hardware distance/adjacency tables}: all-pairs
  core distances, sorted core-neighbour lists, and sorted local-neighbour
  lists are computed once at construction instead of via a fresh BFS on
  every \texttt{core\_distance}/\texttt{core\_neighbors} call.
\item \textbf{$O(1)$ free-slot accounting}: \texttt{Layout.free\_count}
  now reads a per-core occupancy counter maintained incrementally by
  the two layout-mutation entry points, instead of re-scanning every
  physical qubit in the core; free-slot-queue refresh after a layout
  change is restricted to the one or two cores actually touched.
\item \textbf{Apply/restore SWAP scoring}: candidate-SWAP scoring loops
  (intra-core SABRE scoring, Section~\ref{sec:sfc-intra}; preparation-SWAP
  scoring, Section~\ref{sec:sfc-prep}) previously cloned the entire
  layout per candidate; they now apply the SWAP, score, and restore the
  exact pre-swap mapping in place.
\end{enumerate}
We verified behaviour preservation by hashing the fully serialized,
sorted-key JSON operation stream (SHA-256) for eleven representative
circuit/architecture/seed combinations before and after every change,
together with the \texttt{seeded\_tie\_break\_count} statistic (an
exact proxy for RNG consumption): all eleven hashes and tie-break
counts are identical pre- and post-optimisation, and the independent
replay verifier (Section~\ref{sec:verification}) passes on both. Total
wall time on that suite fell from 6.24\,s to 1.55\,s ($4.0\times$); the
64-qubit \texttt{ae} circuit alone fell from 2.77\,s to 0.56\,s, and a
13{,}126-CX \texttt{qnn} stress circuit fell from $\ge$6.2\,s
(measured with only the copy-on-write snapshot change reverted, as a
lower bound) to 1.9\,s.

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\textbf{Architectures.} We use the same two grid-of-grids topologies
introduced by \TeleSABRE{}~\cite{russo2025telesabre} and re-used by
\dSABRE{}~\cite{lidsabre}: architecture~A, a $2{\times}2$ array of
$3{\times}3$ cores (36 physical qubits, 4 inter-core links), and
architecture~H, a $2{\times}3$ array of $4{\times}4$ cores (96 physical
qubits, 7 inter-core links). Both are instantiated identically for all
three routers: we build \SFC{}'s \texttt{Hardware} object once and
convert it into a \dSABRE{} \texttt{DistributedArchitecture} with
matching physical-qubit IDs, intra-core edges, and inter-core ports
(verified programmatically before every run), and \TeleSABRE{} is run
against the project's own \texttt{A\_grid\_2\_2\_3\_3.json} /
\texttt{H\_grid\_2\_3\_4\_4.json} device files, whose inter-core edges
we confirm match exactly.

\textbf{Benchmarks.} We use the same MQT-Bench~\cite{quetschlich2023mqtbench}
circuit files \dSABRE{}'s own evaluation uses (confirmed by exact CX-count
match against its published tables): a 25-qubit suite (AE, GHZ,
Graphstate, QFT, QNN, Random) on architecture~A, and a 64-qubit suite
(the same six families) on architecture~H, with QNN using the
deeper 8{,}126-CX variant. Table~\ref{tab:circuits} lists CX counts.

\textbf{Third suite (80-qubit).} To test at intermediate scale on the

already precedented by \dSABRE{}'s own 100q/200q/360q
suites~\cite{lidsabre}. The generator's current QNN benchmark has no
depth parameter and produces a near-linear circuit at this size (CX
$\approx$ qubit count, Table~\ref{tab:circuits}), so it should be read
as structurally closer to GHZ than to the deeper QNN instances at
25q/64q.

\textbf{Baselines.} We compare against \TeleSABRE{}~\cite{russo2025telesabre}
and \dSABRE{}~\cite{lidsabre}. For \dSABRE{} we report only
\texttt{dSABRE\_BurstExt} (``\texttt{dSE}'' below), its BFS-layer
extended-set variant and the project's own default/production router
(``\dSABRE{}-Topo'', its topological-extended-set ablation, is
omitted).

\textbf{Own-protocol comparison.} Each router is first evaluated under
its own established best-practice initial-layout protocol,
best-of-three, matching the convention already used to compare
\TeleSABRE{} and \dSABRE{} to each other~\cite{lidsabre}:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{\TeleSABRE{}}: the compiled C binary's built-in hungarian
  initial-layout heuristic, config seeds 0--2, best EPR count of the
  successful runs.
\item \textbf{\texttt{dSE}}: Qiskit \texttt{SabreLayout} on the
  corners-removed architecture graph (seeds 0--2) followed by
  \SABRE{}-style forward\,$\to$\,backward\,$\to$\,forward routing
  passes, best EPR count.
\item \textbf{\SFC{}}: two capacity-respecting initial-layout
  heuristics, each run at three seeds (six candidates total), best EPR
  count. The first is \texttt{try15}, a balanced-first-layer placement
  that co-locates the qubits of each first-CX-layer gate and fills the
  rest by remaining core capacity; because its core assignment is
  deterministic given the circuit (only intra-core physical placement
  is seed-dependent), we add a second, genuinely seed-diverse
  candidate: a randomized interaction-graph-greedy placement that
  processes logical qubits in a seeded random order, placing each next
  to its heaviest already-placed CX neighbour when that neighbour's
  core still has capacity headroom above \texttt{min\_free\_before\_teleport}.
  This second heuristic matters specifically on long qubit-chain
  circuits (GHZ): \texttt{try15} only ever co-locates the very first CX
  pair and scatters the rest uniformly at random, since GHZ's circuit
  structure never exposes a second non-overlapping first-layer CX pair
  to its greedy scan; on \texttt{ghz\_25} this alone is the difference
  between 18 and 6 EPR pairs for \SFC{} (Table~\ref{tab:main25}).
\end{itemize}
We do not reuse \texttt{dSE}'s SabreLayout-corners-removed candidates
for \SFC{} in this comparison. \texttt{dSE}'s corner-removal selects
the four lowest-degree vertices of the \emph{whole-chip} graph and
excludes them from the SabreLayout coupling map, guaranteeing they
stay free; on both of our architectures, whose physical-qubit ids are
assigned core-by-core, these four vertices are, by construction, the
four corners of a single core (core~0 in our numbering) -- verified
programmatically for both architectures we test. The other cores
receive no guaranteed free slots from this mechanism at all, and on
this suite it regularly leaves them with zero free physical qubits --
not a valid starting state for \SFC{}'s teleport mechanism, which
requires \texttt{min\_free\_before\_teleport} free slots in a core
before it can ever be used as a teleport destination. This is a
structural precondition mismatch between the two systems' initial-layout
assumptions, not a routing-quality question.

\textbf{Shared-layout comparison.} To isolate routing quality from
initial-layout quality, we additionally route both \texttt{dSE} and
\SFC{} from \TeleSABRE{}'s \emph{own} initial layout: for the winning
seed of the run above, we extract, from \TeleSABRE{}'s optional JSON
trace, the physical-to-logical mapping recorded at iteration~0 -- i.e.\
the layout \TeleSABRE{} itself settled on after its internal
forward/backward/forward layout refinement, immediately before its
accepted forward routing pass -- and feed that identical mapping,
unmodified, to a single \texttt{dSE} run and a single \SFC{} run (no
per-router layout search; the layout is now fixed and given). Unlike
\texttt{dSE}'s own SabreLayout, \TeleSABRE{}'s hungarian heuristic
reserves free-slot headroom per core by construction (tuned to its own
teleport threshold), so it is compatible with \SFC{}'s teleport
precondition without adjustment on every circuit we tested.

\textbf{Pytket-mapping comparison.} As a third, independent layout
source, we also route \texttt{dSE} and \SFC{} from a placement
generated by \texttt{pytket-dqc}'s \texttt{HypergraphPartitioning}
allocator~\cite{pytketdqc2024} (best-of-3 KaHyPar seeds on a
\texttt{NISQNetwork} matching our core graph), converted to a physical
layout by the same topology-aware intra-core assignment \dSABRE{}'s
own pytket benchmark uses~\cite{lidsabre}: each core's assigned
logical qubits are placed by descending interaction-graph degree onto
physical positions ordered by ascending intra-core centrality, then
locally refined by a bounded pairwise-swap pass. \TeleSABRE{} is again
excluded, for the same structural reason as above (no externally
supplied layout support in its CLI). \texttt{HypergraphPartitioning}
does not reserve per-core headroom either (each core's declared
capacity in the \texttt{NISQNetwork} equals its full physical size),
so on some circuits the resulting layout leaves a core fully packed
and \SFC{}'s run is skipped for the same structural reason as the
SabreLayout case above; we report this as a missing entry rather than
suppressing the row. Because \texttt{pytket-dqc} uses gate
teleportation, not state teleportation, its own reported cost is in a
different unit (e-bits per Steiner-tree-amortised hyperedge, not EPR
pairs per qubit move) and is included only as context, following
\dSABRE{}'s own established convention for this
comparison~\cite{lidsabre}.

\textbf{25q, own protocol.} \SFC{} reduces geometric-mean EPR
consumption by \textbf{42.1\%} against \TeleSABRE{} across all six
circuits, with per-circuit reductions ranging from $-26.3\%$ (AE) to
$-55.5\%$ (QNN) and no regressions on this suite. Against
\texttt{dSE}, the picture is reversed: it beats \SFC{} on every
circuit here, most sharply on the two structurally sparse ones (GHZ: 4
vs.\ 6; Graphstate: 4 vs.\ 9), where SabreLayout on the
corners-removed architecture graph, refined over three
forward--backward--forward routing passes, finds a near-ideal core
partition before routing begins -- exactly the regime \dSABRE{}'s own
evaluation identifies as its strongest case~\cite{lidsabre}. On the
three CX-dense circuits (AE, QFT, QNN) the gap to \texttt{dSE} is
narrower, consistent with \SFC{}'s \TeleGatePacket{} primitive
recovering some of the ground a teledata-only router gives away when
many remote CX gates share one control qubit.

\textbf{25q, shared layout.} Under \TeleSABRE{}'s own initial layout
(Table~\ref{tab:shared25}), both \texttt{dSE} and \SFC{} still improve
substantially on \TeleSABRE{} ($-37.3\%$ and $-40.1\%$ gmean
respectively), and \SFC{} is now the better of the two on four of six
circuits (AE, GHZ, QNN, Random) and on the gmean overall, reversing
the own-protocol ranking. \texttt{dSE} still wins on the two circuits
where it wins most decisively under its own protocol too (Graphstate,
QFT). This isolates the effect seen in the own-protocol table: on this
suite, a meaningful share of \dSABRE{}'s own-protocol advantage over
\SFC{} traces to SabreLayout's stronger initial partition rather than
to routing-time decisions alone -- when both routers start from the
identical (weaker, TeleSABRE-optimised-for-itself) layout, \SFC{} edges
ahead on four of six circuits and on the suite gmean, though \texttt{dSE}
retains an edge specifically on the circuits its partition-first
strategy suits best.

\textbf{64q, own protocol.} \TeleSABRE{} fails to converge within
three seeds only on Random here; on QNN (8{,}126 CX) it succeeds at
1{,}365 EPR pairs and 6{,}292 SWAPs, exactly matching \dSABRE{}'s own
published number for this circuit~\cite{lidsabre} -- a useful
cross-check that our harness reproduces \TeleSABRE{} faithfully. (An
earlier version of this evaluation reported QNN as non-convergent;
that was a bug in our own harness, not \TeleSABRE{} -- see
Section~\ref{sec:telesabre-filename-bug}.) The gmean row is computed
over the five circuits on which \TeleSABRE{} converges. \SFC{} beats
\TeleSABRE{} on AE, Graphstate, QFT, and QNN ($-2.2\%$ to $-59.2\%$),
but GHZ regresses sharply ($+163.6\%$, 29 vs.\ 11 EPR pairs). This is
not unique to \SFC{}: \texttt{dSE} regresses on the same circuit in
our reproduction (14 vs.\ 11, $+27.3\%$) and in \dSABRE{}'s own
published 64q table ($+45.5\%$)~\cite{lidsabre}. GHZ's circuit
structure -- every front gate shares one control qubit, so a
per-gate/per-candidate local scorer chases the \emph{next} CX in the
chain -- defeats locally-greedy schedulers generally, not any one
design; \TeleSABRE{}'s front-averaged energy happens to find a better
global rendezvous core on this specific pathological case. On the one
circuit \TeleSABRE{} fails to complete (Random), \SFC{} does (695 EPR),
without any deadlock-recovery mechanism firing.

\textbf{64q, shared layout.} \TeleSABRE{}'s initial layout is now
available for five of six circuits (Table~\ref{tab:shared64}).
\texttt{dSE} wins the gmean here ($-47.9\%$ vs.\ \SFC{}'s $-37.8\%$),
reversing the 25q shared-layout ranking: it wins AE, Graphstate, and
QNN outright, ties \SFC{} on GHZ (both $-27.3\%$), and is edged out
only narrowly on QFT (228 vs.\ 227 EPR). This is the opposite pattern
from the own-protocol table, where \SFC{} beats \texttt{dSE} on AE,
QFT, and QNN; on architecture~H's larger, 16-slot cores, \texttt{dSE}'s
forward--backward--forward routing passes evidently extract more value
from a fixed layout than \SFC{}'s single forward pass does, even
though \SFC{}'s own layout search (which additionally varies the
partition itself, not just the route) still comes out ahead on AE,
QFT, and QNN overall (Table~\ref{tab:main64}). Taken together, the two
suites suggest the layout-versus-routing split of \dSABRE{}'s
advantage is architecture-dependent rather than a fixed property of
either router.

\textbf{80q.} \TeleSABRE{} converges on only three of five circuits
here (Tables~\ref{tab:main80} and~\ref{tab:shared80}); GHZ and QNN are
numerically identical because the installed \texttt{mqt.bench}'s QNN
generator produces a near-linear circuit at this size
(Section~\ref{sec:setup}), so both are effectively the same
chain-structured pathological case discussed above, and \SFC{}
regresses on both by the same $+84.2\%$ under its own protocol. The
striking result is on the two circuits \TeleSABRE{} cannot complete:
on Random (23{,}381 CX, by far the largest circuit in any suite here),
\texttt{dSE}'s own-protocol run also aborts, but \SFC{} completes it
in 40\,s at 10{,}855 EPR pairs. We traced \texttt{dSE}'s failure
directly: it is not a deadlock -- by the time it stops it has already
placed 7{,}713 EPR pairs and 41{,}421 SWAPs, genuine forward progress
-- but an exhausted iteration budget. \texttt{dSE}'s
\texttt{HardwareConfig} for this suite (\texttt{max\_iterations=20000},
carried over unchanged from the 64-qubit suite, per
\dSABRE{}'s own scaling guidance to increase it for larger
circuits~\cite{lidsabre}) is reached after 976.8\,s of its first
forward pass alone, having cleared only 1{,}233 of the circuit's
23{,}381 CX gates; the full own-protocol search evaluates up to three
layout candidates through up to three such passes each, so the
practical cost of simply raising the budget far enough to finish this
circuit would likely run into hours. This is a resource-budget
limitation specific to this unusually dense circuit (23{,}381 CX over
80 qubits, roughly $3$--$18\times$ the CX/qubit ratio of any other
circuit in our suites), not evidence that \texttt{dSE}'s routing
algorithm cannot in principle handle it. On QFT (2{,}660 CX) both
\texttt{dSE} and \SFC{} complete where \TeleSABRE{} cannot, with
\SFC{} using 19.6\% fewer EPR pairs than \texttt{dSE} (539 vs.\ 670).
We read this cautiously -- one non-AE-capable suite with reduced
circuit diversity is not conclusive -- but it is consistent with the
64q finding that \SFC{} tends to complete circuits at scales where
\TeleSABRE{}, and sometimes \texttt{dSE} under a fixed iteration
budget, do not.

\textbf{Pytket-mapping comparison.} Table~\ref{tab:pytket} shows the
same qualitative pattern as the TeleSABRE-shared-layout tables: from
an externally supplied, non-router-specific placement, the two routers
trade wins depending on circuit structure rather than either
dominating uniformly, and \SFC{}'s teleport mechanism is more sensitive
to a layout that leaves cores fully packed (three missing entries: 25q
QNN, 64q AE, 64q QNN) than \texttt{dSE}'s teledata-only mechanism,
which completes on every row. Where both complete, results are mixed:
\SFC{} uses fewer EPR pairs on 25q AE/GHZ/QFT/Random and 64q
GHZ/Random/QFT, while \texttt{dSE} wins on 25q Graphstate and 64q
Graphstate. \texttt{pytket-dqc}'s own e-bit cost is in a structurally
different unit (Steiner-tree-amortised gate teleportation, not
per-qubit state teleportation) and is not directly comparable, per
\dSABRE{}'s own established convention for this
comparison~\cite{lidsabre}; we include it only as context.

\subsection{The \TeleSABRE{} Filename-Handling Bug}
\label{sec:telesabre-filename-bug}

Our comparison harness originally invoked \TeleSABRE{} with the
64-qubit QNN circuit file's on-disk name,
\texttt{qnn\_OLD\_DEEP\ldots.qasm.bak} (the deeper of two QNN variants
present in the upstream repository, selected because its CX count
exactly matches \dSABRE{}'s own published QNN table). \TeleSABRE{}'s
\texttt{main.c} infers file type from the \emph{last} dot in the
filename, so this double extension is parsed as \texttt{.bak} and
rejected outright (\texttt{"does not have a .json or .qasm
extension"}) before any routing is attempted -- a harness bug, not a
genuine \TeleSABRE{} non-convergence. We fixed this by always handing
the binary a temporary copy with a clean \texttt{.qasm} suffix. Once
fixed, \TeleSABRE{} completes this circuit in 21.7\,s at exactly the
EPR and SWAP counts \dSABRE{}'s own evaluation reports
(Section~\ref{sec:main_results}), confirming the fix and giving an
independent cross-check of our harness's fidelity to \TeleSABRE{}'s
actual behaviour. All \TeleSABRE{} numbers in this paper reflect the
corrected harness.

\subsection{The \TeleSABRE{} Reference-Implementation Bug}
\label{sec:telesabre-bug}

Running \TeleSABRE{}'s compiled C binary against architecture~A (four
$3{\times}3{=}9$-qubit cores) with its default hungarian
initial-layout configuration segfaults deterministically on every
circuit we tried. We traced this with source-level instrumentation
(macOS's \texttt{lldb} could not attach headlessly in our environment)
to \texttt{initial\_layout\_hungarian} in \texttt{src/layout.c}: its
second assignment pass places a logical qubit $q$ into the first core
whose remaining capacity exceeds
\texttt{init\_layout\_hun\_min\_free\_qubit} (4 in the reference
config), but if \emph{every} core's remaining capacity is at or below
that threshold -- which routinely happens once a 9-slot core has
already absorbed 5 of its share of a 25-logical-qubit circuit --
\texttt{virt\_to\_core[q]} is left at its sentinel $-1$ and is then
used, unchecked, as a negative array index a few lines later. This is
a latent bug in the reference implementation, not an artefact of our
harness: it reproduces with the project's own \texttt{configs/default.json}
and device file, invoked exactly per the project's README. It is
specific to core capacities materially smaller than the $4{\times}4{=}16$
cores \TeleSABRE{} was originally evaluated on; we did not observe it
on architecture~H. We built a minimal, behaviour-preserving patch
(fall back to the least-loaded core rather than leaving the qubit
unassigned) against a separate copy of the source tree and used the
resulting binary for every \TeleSABRE{} number in this paper; the
project's own repository is untouched.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{\TeleSABRE{}}~\cite{russo2025telesabre} is our closest
architectural relative: both routers support teledata and gate
teleportation and both build on the \SABRE{} extended-set scoring
idea. The two differ chiefly in decision structure: \TeleSABRE{}
scores inter-core candidates via a contracted-communication-graph
Dijkstra energy that mixes staging cost and lookahead into one flat
action pool per candidate, whereas \SFC{} scores remote \emph{plans}
independently of their preparation cost and only generates
preparation SWAPs for the one plan selected
(Section~\ref{sec:sfc-plan-first}), and adds a same-control packet
primitive that amortises runs of shared-control remote CX gates onto
one EPR pair. \SFC{}'s capacity handling is also more proactive:
alongside \TeleSABRE{}'s safety-valve iteration cap, \SFC{} maintains
an explicit blocked-plan set and both a breadth-first tail-relief
search and an opportunistic pre-emptive relief comparator
(Section~\ref{sec:sfc-relief}).

\textbf{\dSABRE{}}~\cite{lidsabre} (we compare against its default
\texttt{dSABRE\_BurstExt} variant, ``\texttt{dSE}'') is the more
EPR-efficient baseline under each router's own protocol
(Section~\ref{sec:main_results}), particularly on sparse, highly
partitionable circuits (GHZ, Graphstate) where its SabreLayout-based
initial allocation plus forward-backward-forward routing passes find a
near-ideal core partition before routing even begins. The
shared-layout comparison (Tables~\ref{tab:shared25}, \ref{tab:shared64},
and \ref{tab:pytket}) attributes a substantial share of that advantage
to initial-layout quality rather than routing-time decisions on the
25-qubit suite -- from \TeleSABRE{}'s own layout, \SFC{} matches or
beats \texttt{dSE} on four of six circuits -- though the effect
reverses on the 64-qubit suite (Section~\ref{sec:main_results}).
\dSABRE{} restricts itself to teledata; \SFC{} instead
follows \TeleSABRE{} in also supporting gate teleportation and extends
it with same-control packets, which matters on circuits with long runs
of shared-control remote CX gates (e.g.\ QFT-like phase-kickback
patterns) where amortising many remote CX gates onto one EPR pair can
outweigh a less-optimal core partition.

\textbf{Single-QPU qubit mapping.} The front+extended-set scoring
loop~\SFC{} and both baselines build on originates with
\SABRE{}~\cite{li2019tackling}; \LightSABRE{}~\cite{zou2024lightsabrelightweightenhancedsabre}
formalises the $\Delta$-form SWAP score \SFC{} reuses for its
intra-core stage (Eq.~\ref{eq:sfc-local}) and inter-core direct-gain
term (Eq.~\ref{eq:sfc-teleport}).

\textbf{Allocation and scheduling.} Hypergraph-partitioning allocation
methods~\cite{andres2019automated,pytketdqc2024} and time-aware
schedulers~\cite{baker2020time,ferrari2021compiler} address the
upstream allocation and downstream scheduling stages respectively; both
are complementary to \SFC{}, which addresses only the routing stage
given a fixed initial layout.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We presented \SFC{}, a \SABRE{}-style router for multi-core
distributed quantum computers whose central design choice is to
separate remote-plan \emph{selection} from remote-plan
\emph{preparation}: every legal teleport, telegate, and same-control
packet candidate (including capacity-blocked variants) is scored on
its physical and DAG-lookahead merits before any core-preparation SWAP
is generated, and preparation effort is then spent exclusively on the
one plan chosen. Combined with a two-tier capacity-relief mechanism
(breadth-first tail relief when every plan is blocked, opportunistic
pre-emptive relief when one is severely blocked), this plan-first
structure lets \SFC{} reduce geometric-mean EPR consumption by
$42.1\%$ against \TeleSABRE{} on a 25-qubit suite and $16.7\%$ on a
64-qubit suite, completing every circuit \TeleSABRE{} cannot, while
remaining behind \dSABRE{} under each router's own best-practice
layout, particularly on sparse, easily-partitioned circuits and on the
chain-structured GHZ pattern where all locally-greedy schedulers we
tested, \SFC{} included, lose ground to \TeleSABRE{}'s front-averaged
energy. Feeding both routers \TeleSABRE{}'s own initial layout instead
of each one's own best-practice layout reverses this ranking on the
25-qubit suite but not on the 64-qubit one, indicating that the share
of \dSABRE{}'s advantage attributable to its initial-layout heuristic
versus its routing-time decisions is architecture-dependent -- a
distinction that matters for anyone choosing where to invest further
engineering effort between the two stages. At the largest scale we
tested (80 qubits, a 23{,}381-CX circuit), \SFC{} completed in 40\,s
where \TeleSABRE{} failed to converge and \dSABRE{}'s reference
implementation exhausted an iteration budget left unincreased from the
64-qubit suite after 976.8\,s of genuine forward progress on its first
routing pass alone (Section~\ref{sec:main_results}) -- a practical
throughput advantage on unusually dense circuits at scale that the
smaller suites do not exercise, though not evidence that \dSABRE{}'s
algorithm is inherently unable to route this circuit given a larger
budget. We
verified the reference implementation with an independent replay
checker across 60 randomized circuits and the full evaluation suite,
applied a set of behaviour-preserving performance fixes yielding a
$4\times$ compile-time speedup with byte-identical output, and, in the
course of building a fair three-way comparison, found and patched a
latent out-of-bounds bug in \TeleSABRE{}'s reference C implementation
that had prevented it from running at all on small-core architectures,
and separately identified and fixed a filename-handling bug in our own
harness that had caused an earlier version of this evaluation to
under-report \TeleSABRE{}'s 64-qubit convergence.

\paragraph*{Artefact availability.} The \SFC{} implementation, the
independent replay verifier, the cross-router comparison harness, and
raw per-circuit JSON results are available in the accompanying
repository.
