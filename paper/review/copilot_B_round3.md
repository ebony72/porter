## Proposed one-sentence contribution statement

PORTER is a deterministic distributed-quantum-circuit router that chooses and commits to a preparation-independent plan for the next inter-core action, combines that decision structure with capacity relief, packetized gate teleportation, a deterministic layout, and per-circuit horizon selection, and evaluates the resulting compiler against TeleSABRE and dSABRE under several layout and operating protocols.

## Ten passages most worth rewriting

1. **“The two prove complementary rather than ranked: neither wins more than eleven of the twenty-four instances, and taking the better of the two per instance uses 9.3% fewer pairs than DSABRE alone, so the designs reach different circuits rather than re-deriving each other.”**  
   The observation is valuable, but “so” turns an oracle portfolio result into a causal claim about design mechanisms and omits the cost of running both.

2. **“A design that merely re-derived DSABRE’s decisions would leave such a portfolio nothing to choose between.”**  
   This counterfactual is rhetorically strong but not needed; different outputs establish non-identity, not which architectural difference produced the gap.

3. **“The routing loops are equivalent within noise; the layouts are not.”**  
   No noise or equivalence analysis is reported, and the sentence over-compresses a nuanced controlled result.

4. **“The spread down a column is two to four times the spread across a row in both blocks, so the initial layout is the weaker component of both compilers rather than an advantage to either.”**  
   “Weaker component” reverses or obscures what the stated spread comparison demonstrates.

5. **“A 9–12% gain instead means the plan-oriented stage, the relief tiers and the horizon reach circuits DSABRE’s hop-granular, fixed-horizon loop does not …”**  
   This attributes the portfolio difference to mechanisms not isolated by the experiment.

6. **“Third, the ablations attribute these results rather than leaving them to the system as a whole …”**  
   This conflicts with the manuscript’s explicit non-attribution of the plan abstraction and commitment rule, and it folds non-ablation evidence into “the ablations.”

7. **“Against dSABRE it is competitive at the sizes both target … and pulls ahead as the machine grows, using 0.72× its EPR pairs on a 100–360-qubit scale-up series.”**  
   The scale-up basis is only QFT and QPEexact; without that phrase the progression sounds broader than the evidence.

8. **“Having committed, PORTER applies only that plan’s preparation SWAPs until it is ready, no improving SWAP remains, or the decision state — plan, layout, RNG — repeats, which raises rather than loops.”**  
   Three distinct terminal conditions are compressed into one sentence, and their different state transitions are not stated.

9. **“Pooled over all twenty-four PORTER is slightly ahead at 0.91×, and at 1.02× DSABRE’s EPR the cheap operating point remains PORTER’s.”**  
   The first ratio lacks its metric and sits beside an EPR ratio, forcing a backward lookup.

10. **“A fifth is a gap rather than a design choice, and it runs DSABRE’s way: its safe mode carries a termination-with-success proof under stated architectural hypotheses, so it guarantees completion, where PORTER guarantees only correctness and termination by construction (Section III-H) and has completion empirically, no instance in this evaluation being left unrouted.”**  
    The final clause conflicts with the fixed-horizon failure reported elsewhere and needs protocol-level scoping.

## Abstract-specific critique

The abstract is understandable without the rest of the paper at the level of problem, design idea, competitors, and top-line outcome. It defines EPR pairs, explains the plan-before-preparation distinction, names the added mechanisms, and distinguishes the comparisons with TeleSABRE and dSABRE. It is therefore broadly self-contained conceptually.

It is not fully self-contained evidentially. Three bases need compact qualification:

- The 59.6%, 41.6%, and 68.2% reductions are geometric means over the instances that both PORTER and TeleSABRE complete, not over every circuit in each suite.
- The 0.72× scale-up result is based on a six-instance series containing QFT and QPEexact only.
- The 9.3% best-of-two result is an oracle portfolio obtained by running both compilers, not a predictive selector or a fifth compiler configuration.

The 1.02× EPR and 0.91× compile-time comparison does state its key basis, “under each compiler’s own protocol,” though the abstract could make the unequal route budgets visible only at unacceptable length. The completion count of 24 versus 17 is clear. The statement that the two are “complementary rather than ranked” matches the per-instance record, but the causal gloss that the designs “reach different circuits rather than re-deriving each other” goes beyond what the oracle record alone establishes. The phrase “pulls ahead as the machine grows” should also be narrowed because the body limits that trend to two circuit families.

The abstract’s final sentence, “Controlled ablations measure what each mechanism contributes,” does not match the body precisely. The paper explicitly says that neither the plan abstraction nor the commitment rule is isolated, while the ablations cover selected mechanisms and include a vocabulary variant that is not a strict one-factor ablation. A safer abstract sentence would say that controlled experiments and ablations probe several mechanisms and expose a negative result, without claiming coverage of each mechanism.

## Judgement on Section III

A reader can follow the normal-path lifecycle of a plan, but not the exceptional path cleanly enough for a section whose central contribution is commitment.

The normal thread is present and mostly well ordered:

1. Section III-B drains immediately executable work and classifies the remaining front.
2. Section III-C resolves local work before the remote stage.
3. Section III-D defines normal and blocked plans and separates plan utility from readiness and preparation.
4. Section III-E scores plans and gives the lexicographic selection key.
5. Section III-D selects the first legal, ready or preparable plan, with relief able to override it.
6. Section III-D then generates preparation SWAPs only for the committed plan.
7. Sections III-F and III-G explain execution for packets and the alternative relief-plan path.
8. Section III-H explains global termination and failure categories.

The thread breaks in three places.

First, Algorithm 1 and the prose disagree at preparation failure. The prose allows abandonment when no improving preparation SWAP remains, but line 23 proceeds from preparation to execution without a branch. This leaves the reader unable to place abandonment in the repeated outer loop.

Second, static plan identity and dynamic readiness are not cleanly separated in the formal tuple. Including `ready(p,ℓ)` in the plan representation makes the central object appear layout-dependent even though commitment is supposed to preserve the chosen action while preparation changes the layout.

Third, capacity legalization is introduced too late for supplied layouts. Section III-A says PORTER can start from any layout; only Section III-H explains that a full core may trigger an eviction and that one class of input is refused. That preprocessing belongs at the start of the lifecycle, especially because later controlled experiments depend on externally supplied layouts.

The section’s length is largely earned by the number of interacting mechanisms, but its organization makes Section III-D carry too much: definition, candidate generation, selection, override, commitment, abandonment, and preparation policy. Without rewriting the section wholesale, the best editorial repair is to add a five- or six-line “plan lifecycle” paragraph at the start of III-D and make Algorithm 1 show the abandonment transition explicitly. The remaining detail can then be read as expansion of a complete state machine rather than as the reader’s first opportunity to reconstruct it.
