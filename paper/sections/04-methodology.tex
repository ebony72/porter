\section{Experimental Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

This section specifies the evaluation questions, experimental inputs, and
comparison protocols. A \emph{route} is one invocation of the router on one
circuit from one initial layout. A \emph{protocol} specifies how layouts are
obtained, how many routes are run, and how the reported route is selected.
Reported compile time is the cost of the complete protocol, including layout
generation and all unsuccessful candidate routes.

\subsection{Research Questions}
\label{sec:rqs}

The experiments address four questions.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.6em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item[\textbf{RQ1}] Under each compiler's native protocol, how do EPR-pair
consumption, completion, and complete compile time compare?
\item[\textbf{RQ2}] When the initial layout is controlled, how much of the
difference between \PORTER{} and \dSABRE{} is due to routing rather than
layout generation?
\item[\textbf{RQ3}] What are the individual effects of capacity relief, the
communication primitives, initial layout, and horizon selection?
\item[\textbf{RQ4}] Do these conclusions persist as circuit width, core
count, core shape, and core-graph topology change?
\end{itemize}

Section~\ref{sec:results} follows this order. Each results subsection states
what it controls and what it measures before interpreting the outcome.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}
\label{sec:perf}

\textbf{Implementation and checking.} \PORTER{} is implemented in Python
and released with the comparison harness. It is deterministic: ties follow a
fixed key order and any residual tie uses a constant-initialised generator.
Thus, a fixed circuit, machine, layout, and horizon produce a byte-identical
operation stream. A separately implemented replay verifier checks every
reported \PORTER{} stream for gate coverage, dependency order, and physical
legality; the verifier is tested by fault injection (\appVerification{}).

\textbf{Architectures.} We use the two grid-of-grids devices evaluated by
\dSABRE{}~\cite{lidsabre}: architecture~A, a $2{\times}2$ array of
$3{\times}3$ cores (36 physical qubits), and architecture~H, a
$2{\times}3$ array of $4{\times}4$ cores (96 physical qubits). RQ4 also
uses \dSABRE{}'s $5{\times}5$-core scale-up series and two networks of IBM
27-qubit heavy-hex tiles. Device descriptions are read from the baselines'
own files so that all compilers use identical physical-qubit identifiers.

\textbf{Benchmarks.} The main inputs are the MQT-Bench files used in the
\dSABRE{} evaluation~\cite{quetschlich2023mqtbench}, verified by exact
CX-count match. Architecture~A uses six 25-qubit families (AE, GHZ,
Graphstate, QFT, QNN, and Random). Architecture~H uses nine 64-qubit
families, adding QPEexact, QAOA, and Multiplier. We also construct an
80-qubit suite of the same nine families; its AE and QNN instances are
reconstructed because the stock generator does not produce them. The
supplement documents these reconstructions (\appSuite{}).

\label{sec:baselines}
\textbf{Baselines and execution.} We compare with \TeleSABRE{}
~\cite{russo2025telesabre} and \dSABRE{}~\cite{lidsabre}. \dSABRE{} uses
its shipped safe mode and layout rule. Its recovery budget is derived from
the architecture as in its drivers, and its pre-routing layout-repair EPR
count is excluded for both compilers because it changes the starting layout
rather than routing the circuit. The stock \TeleSABRE{} binary aborts
reproducibly in its initial-layout pass whenever no core holds more free
positions than its configured margin, which is every 25- and 80-qubit instance
and no 64-qubit one. We therefore use and release a minimal patch that assigns
the logical qubits the stock code leaves unplaced to the least-loaded core and
changes nothing else, so results agree with the stock binary wherever it
completes --- the whole 64-qubit suite.
Each compiler invocation has a 900-s wall-clock limit; an invocation that
exceeds it is recorded as incomplete.

\subsection{Layouts and Comparison Protocols}
\label{sec:protocols}

We use native, shared-layout, and cross-layout comparisons. These controls
separate the quality of an initial layout from the quality of the routing
loop, but they do not make all resource budgets identical.

\textbf{Native protocols (RQ1 and RQ4).} \TeleSABRE{} uses its Hungarian
layout heuristic at seeds 0--2 and reports the best EPR result. \dSABRE{}
uses Qiskit \texttt{SabreLayout} at seeds 0--2, followed for each candidate
by its forward--backward--forward routing schedule, and reports the best
result. \PORTER{} builds one deterministic temporal-locality-greedy layout
(Section~\ref{sec:sfc-layout}), routes it at four horizons, and retains the
lowest-EPR route. \PORTER{}+R additionally performs backward refinement
from that selected route.

For \dSABRE{}, \texttt{SabreLayout} operates on its shipped
corners-removed coupling map. The removed low-degree corner positions begin
empty and provide capacity for arrivals and evictions; they are distinct from
the designated communication ports.

\textbf{Shared layouts (RQ2).} Both \dSABRE{} and \PORTER{} route the same
externally supplied layout, the \texttt{pytket-dqc} hypergraph partition
(\appPytket{}). This controls the initial mapping but not search effort:
\dSABRE{} uses three routing passes, \PORTER{} four horizons, and
\PORTER{}+R eight routes. It therefore isolates layout source rather than
ranking routing loops at a matched budget.

\textbf{Cross layouts (RQ2).} Each compiler also routes the other
compiler's native layout(s). In particular, \PORTER{} routes all three of
\dSABRE{}'s candidate layouts and selects the lowest-EPR outcome. This test
shows whether a native-protocol result is sensitive to the layout source.

\subsubsection{\PORTER{}: fixed horizon portfolio}
\label{sec:horizon}

The teleport-lookahead horizon $L_\mathrm{tp}$ determines how many DAG
layers are examined when scoring a \TeleData{} candidate. An offline sweep
over $\{4,8,\ldots,1024\}$ found no single best value: the per-instance
optimum spans the range, 80-qubit QNN selecting $16$ where all six
100--360-qubit scale-up instances select $128$. We therefore apply the same
fixed portfolio to every circuit,
\[
L_\mathrm{tp}\in\{4,8,16,128\},
\]
route once at each horizon, and retain the completed route with the fewest
EPR pairs.

The configured default, $L_\mathrm{tp}=16$, is evaluated first and wins
ties, so whenever it completes the portfolio cannot report a higher EPR
count than that one-route configuration. This follows from including the
default among the candidates; it implies nothing about horizons outside the
set.

No horizon is predicted from circuit features. The portfolio was itself
fixed after inspecting the 48 evaluated instances, so its measured EPR
improvement is in-sample rather than evidence of held-out generalisation.
Nearby four-value portfolios change the aggregate by less than one percent,
a limited robustness observation and not a substitute for out-of-sample
validation.

Its cost is compile time: four routing invocations instead of one, and long
lookahead is expensive on dense circuits. Reported protocol times include
every horizon probe, successful or not (Section~\ref{sec:metrics}).

\subsubsection{\PORTER{}+R: backward refinement}
\label{sec:sfc-refine}

\PORTER{}+R applies \SABRE{}-style bidirectional refinement
~\cite{li2019tackling} to complete routes. Let $R(C,\ell)$ denote routing
circuit $C$ from layout $\ell$. Pass~1 routes $C$ from $\ell_0$. Pass~2
routes the reversed circuit from the final layout of pass~1 and retains only
its final layout. Pass~3 routes $C$ from that layout. We use five passes by
repeating this forward--backward cycle and report the best forward route.

The first pass is the four-horizon \PORTER{} portfolio. Its selected horizon
is retained for the remaining four passes, rather than being reselected at
each pass. Thus \PORTER{}+R executes eight routes in total and contains the
\PORTER{} result; retaining the best forward route ensures that it cannot
report more EPR pairs than \PORTER{}. Individual refinement passes can still
be worse because a post-routing layout need not preserve the free-slot
reserve of the original layout.

\subsection{Budgets and Metrics}
\label{sec:budgets}
\label{sec:metrics}

Table~\ref{tab:budgets} makes the search budget explicit. ``Routes'' counts
all routing invocations, including horizon probes. Thus route count is a
transparent effort descriptor, not a claim that routes have equal runtime.


The primary outcome is EPR pairs consumed. \TeleSABRE{} counts \TeleData{}
and \TeleGate{} operations; \dSABRE{} counts \TeleData{} operations; and
\PORTER{} counts \TeleData{}, \TeleGate{}, and \TeleGatePacket{} operations,
one pair per packet. Under the cost model of Section~\ref{sec:scope}, these are the
same physical resource. We also report intra-core SWAP counts and wall-clock compile time,
but no selection rule trades either against EPR count.

Aggregate EPR results are unweighted geometric means over circuits. For a
comparison of methods $a$ and $b$, the reported ratio is $b/a$ and the
percentage change $100(b/a-1)$, both computed from the aggregate means. A
ratio below one, equivalently a negative percentage, means $b$ uses fewer
pairs: \PORTER{}'s pooled $169.4$ against \dSABRE{}'s $171.9$ is reported as
$0.99\times$, a $1.5\%$ reduction.

Failures are not imputed: comparisons exclude unsuccessful invocations
rather than assigning them a penalty value. Each paired mean therefore
covers only circuits completed by every column in it, and reports its
completion count. Since that can flatter a baseline by dropping the
instances it could not route, completion counts are co-primary outcomes.

Compile times are complete protocol wall-clock times measured once per
configuration on an otherwise idle Apple M2 system (macOS~26.5,
CPython~3.13), without warm-up. They should be interpreted as indicative
protocol-level ratios, not as portable absolute timings.
