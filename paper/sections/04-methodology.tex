\section{Experimental Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{Research Questions}
\label{sec:rqs}

The evaluation answers four questions, each with its own controls, and
Section~\ref{sec:results} is organised around them.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item[\textbf{RQ1}] \textbf{End-to-end cost.} Under each compiler's own
  recommended protocol, how does \PORTER{}'s EPR consumption and compile
  time compare with \TeleSABRE{}'s and \dSABRE{}'s?
\item[\textbf{RQ2}] \textbf{Routing quality with the layout controlled.}
  Given the \emph{same} externally supplied initial layout, how does
  \PORTER{}'s routing loop compare with \dSABRE{}'s --- first at an equal
  route count, then when each is allowed its own refinement schedule?
\item[\textbf{RQ3}] \textbf{Mechanism attribution.} What does each of
  \PORTER{}'s mechanisms --- plan commitment, capacity relief, the packet
  primitive, the \TeleGate{} family, the initial layout and horizon
  selection --- contribute on its own?
\item[\textbf{RQ4}] \textbf{Robustness.} How do the answers change with
  circuit width, core count and core-graph shape?
\end{itemize}

Each results subsection opens with the question it answers, what is held
fixed, and the metric; the interpretation of the answers, including the
places where they appear to conflict, is collected in
Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.


\subsection{Terminology}
\label{sec:terms}

Budget comparisons depend on using these terms consistently, so we fix
them here and use no synonyms.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=0pt,topsep=2pt]
\item A \textbf{route} (equivalently a \emph{routing pass}) is one
  invocation of the routing loop of Section~\ref{sec:sfc} on one circuit
  from one layout, producing one operation stream.
\item A \textbf{candidate layout} is one initial mapping a protocol
  supplies to the router. A protocol's cost is its layout count times its
  routes per layout.
\item A \textbf{protocol} is a named row of Table~\ref{tab:budgets}: a
  layout source, a route budget and a selection rule. We avoid
  ``attempt'', which the baselines use for both meanings.
\item A \textbf{candidate horizon} is one value of $L_\mathrm{tp}$ offered
  to horizon selection; selecting among them costs one route each.
\item \textbf{Complete compile cost} is the wall-clock time of an entire
  protocol --- layout generation plus every route over every candidate ---
  not of the winning route alone.
\end{itemize}

For the machine and the router we use: \emph{core}, \emph{communication
port}, and \emph{reservoir} (a core holding at least $f_\mathrm{min}$ free
positions); a front CX is \emph{local}, \emph{adjacent-remote} or
\emph{non-adjacent-remote}; a plan is \emph{normal} or \emph{blocked}, and
\emph{ready}, \emph{preparable} or neither. A run that does not complete
does so in one of four ways, distinguished throughout: a capacity
\emph{deadlock} leaves no legal plan and no relief path, and raises a
diagnostic; a \emph{livelock} makes local progress indefinitely while
retired gates and EPR count stand still, and is stopped by the iteration
guard; a \emph{timeout} exceeds the wall-clock cap; and an
\emph{infeasible input} is one the machine has no spare capacity for,
refused before routing begins (Section~\ref{sec:sfc-progress}).

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}
\label{sec:perf}

\textbf{Implementation.} \PORTER{} is implemented in Python and released
with the comparison harness. It takes no seed: heuristic ties resolve
through a fixed chain of deterministic keys and any residual tie through
a constant-initialised generator, so one route on a fixed circuit and
hardware emits a byte-identical stream across runs. Horizon selection is
the exception, because its speculative routes share a wall-clock budget:
on a loaded machine a candidate may be abandoned that would otherwise
have won, so the protocol is reproducible only up to that budget, and the
horizon each run selected is recorded with its result. Every reported
\PORTER{} route is certified by a replay verifier written separately from
the router, which re-derives gate coverage, dependency order and physical
legality from the emitted stream alone and is itself tested by fault
injection (\appVerification{}).

\textbf{Architectures.} We use the two grid-of-grids topologies
\dSABRE{}~\cite{lidsabre} evaluates on --- architecture~A, a $2{\times}2$
array of $3{\times}3$ cores (36 physical qubits), and architecture~H, a
$2{\times}3$ array of $4{\times}4$ cores (96 physical) --- adding its
$5{\times}5$-core scalability series in Section~\ref{sec:scaleup} and two
networks of IBM 27-qubit heavy-hex tiles in Section~\ref{sec:heavyhex}.
All are read from the baselines' own device files, so every compiler sees
identical physical qubit ids.

\textbf{Benchmarks.} We use the same
MQT-Bench~\cite{quetschlich2023mqtbench} circuit files \dSABRE{}'s
evaluation uses, confirmed by exact CX-count match: a 25-qubit suite (AE,
GHZ, Graphstate, QFT, QNN, Random) on architecture~A, and a 64-qubit
suite adding QPEexact, QAOA and Multiplier on architecture~H. We add an
80-qubit suite of the same nine families; its AE and QNN instances are
not produced by the stock generator and were reconstructed to match its
construction, QNN as the deep ZZ-feature-map circuit rather than the
79-CX chain the current generator emits. \appSuite{} gives both
reconstructions and their validation, and the scripts are released.

\label{sec:baselines}
\textbf{Baselines.} We compare against
\TeleSABRE{}~\cite{russo2025telesabre} and \dSABRE{}~\cite{lidsabre}, the
latter in its default configuration --- safe mode, which makes capacity a
legality condition with a per-core reserve and recovers by relaying slack
along a BFS path (Section~\ref{sec:related}).

Three decisions avoid disadvantaging \dSABRE{}. We let its recovery
budgets derive from the architecture as its own drivers do, since a
binding iteration budget would cut its heuristic search short and hand
the remainder to its guaranteed drain at a worse EPR count. We do not
charge it for pre-routing layout repair, which lands in its EPR counter
although it adjusts the starting layout rather than routing anything, and
we net that out for both compilers. And we hand it both families of its
layout candidates rather than picking one
(Section~\ref{sec:protocols}).

\TeleSABRE{}'s stock C binary crashes deterministically on
architecture~A, in its initial-layout pass rather than in routing. All
\TeleSABRE{} numbers use a minimally patched binary, released with ours,
that falls back to the least-loaded core; it changes placement only for
qubits the stock binary leaves unplaced, and architecture-H results are
unchanged by it. Every compiler invocation is capped at $900$\,s
wall-clock, and a call exceeding it counts as a failure to complete.

\subsection{Initial-Layout Protocols}
\label{sec:protocols}

We evaluate under three initial-layout protocols, designed to separate
routing quality from initial-layout quality.

\textbf{Own-protocol comparison.} Each router is first evaluated under
its own established best-practice protocol. For the two baselines that
is best-of-three, matching the convention already used to compare
\TeleSABRE{} and \dSABRE{} to each other~\cite{lidsabre}:
\TeleSABRE{} runs its built-in Hungarian initial-layout heuristic at
config seeds 0--2, and \dSABRE{} runs Qiskit \texttt{SabreLayout} on
its corners-removed architecture graph at seeds 0--2, each layout
routed by \SABRE{}-style forward\,$\to$\,backward\,$\to$\,forward
passes; both report the best EPR count. \PORTER{} instead builds one
deterministic temporal locality-greedy layout
(Section~\ref{sec:sfc-layout}) and routes it at each of the three
candidate lookahead horizons of Section~\ref{sec:horizon}, keeping the
best --- three routing passes over one layout, against nine to eighteen
over three to six. It searches no layouts, so every number is reproducible from the circuit
file alone.

\dSABRE{}'s corner removal reserves low-degree physical qubits as
communication slots by excluding them from the SabreLayout coupling map.
It ships two variants of the rule and neither dominates on our
architectures, so rather than choose we give \dSABRE{} both families and
take its best: up to six candidate layouts instead of three. This can
only strengthen the baseline relative to its own published protocol, and
it is why our \dSABRE{} numbers sit at or below the figures it publishes
for the architectures it reports.

\textbf{Shared-layout comparison.} To separate layout quality from
routing quality, both \dSABRE{} and \PORTER{} are then run from an
identical externally supplied mapping --- \TeleSABRE{}'s own initial
layout, and separately \texttt{pytket-dqc}'s hypergraph partition
(\appPytket{}) --- so the only difference left is the routing loop.
\textbf{Cross-layout comparison.} Finally each compiler routes the
other's own layout, which closes the remaining cell of the design.

\subsection{Operating Protocols}
\label{sec:opprotocols}

Section~\ref{sec:sfc} defines one routing pass. Three protocols run that
pass more than once and keep the best result by EPR; because they invoke
the router repeatedly rather than changing what a single pass does, we
describe them here rather than as part of the algorithm. All three are
deterministic given the circuit, the hardware and the configuration, and
none takes a seed.

\subsubsection{Lookahead-horizon selection}
\label{sec:horizon}

The teleport-lookahead horizon $L_\mathrm{tp}$ is the one parameter of
Section~\ref{sec:sfc-scoring} for which no tested constant is best across
the corpus. In an offline sweep over $\{4,8,\dots,1024\}$ on every instance the best value
spans the whole range: $80$-qubit QNN wants $4$, the $25$-qubit suite
wants $16$, and every $100$-, $200$- and $360$-qubit instance wants
$128$, beyond which EPR saturates. The gap between the shipped $16$ and
the per-instance best is $17.2\%$ geometric-mean EPR.

Interaction density sets the direction. Write $\rho$ for the mean
fraction of the other logical qubits each qubit shares a CX with. As
$\rho\to1$ a long horizon reports the moving qubit as needed in every
core at once, so the gain estimate degenerates into noise and short
horizons win; when $\rho$ is small the signal is coherent and long
horizons win. Across the benchmark $\rho$ ranks the best horizon at
Spearman $-0.765$, but it is not a usable predictor alone: in the band
$0.42\le\rho\le0.53$, where most 64-qubit circuits sit, QAOA wants $8$
and AE wants $128$, and a threshold rule on $\rho$ recovers only $8.0\%$
of the available $17.2\%$.

\PORTER{} therefore uses $\rho$ to \emph{prune} rather than to choose. A
circuit with $\rho\ge0.80$ is routed at $L_\mathrm{tp}\in\{4,8,16\}$,
every other circuit at $\{8,16,128\}$, and the lowest-EPR route is kept.
No instance above the threshold ever selected the long horizon, so
pruning costs no EPR and removes the dominant compile-time term: a long
horizon that fails to converge and consumes the whole protocol's budget.
Two rules bound the cost. The circuit's configured horizon is routed
first, unguarded, and wins ties. The remaining candidates share a
wall-clock budget proportional to what that first route cost, and one
that overruns it is abandoned. The guarantee this gives is conditional
and worth stating exactly: \emph{provided the configured-horizon route
completes}, selection returns no higher an EPR count than that route
would alone. It can still raise compile time, and which speculative
candidates finish --- hence which route is selected --- depends on the
wall-clock budget and so on the machine (Section~\ref{sec:perf}).

\textbf{This rule was fitted on the corpus it is evaluated on.} The
sweep, both candidate sets and the $0.80$ threshold were fixed by
inspecting the $48$ benchmark instances, and there is no held-out
family, so evidence for the \emph{size} of the gain is in-sample. Two
things bound the risk. The rule is a portfolio, not a prediction: the
configured horizon always competes and wins ties, so a poor candidate
set costs compile time but cannot worsen the selected route. And the
direction the sweep exposes --- that no tested constant is best across
circuits whose densities differ by an order of magnitude --- does not
depend on the values chosen. An out-of-sample validation remains to be done.

\subsubsection{Backward refinement (\PORTER{}+R)}
\label{sec:sfc-refine}

\dSABRE{} routes each candidate layout with a
forward\,$\to$\,backward\,$\to$\,forward schedule, so a protocol that
matches its routing effort rather than only its layout count is needed to
separate the two effects. \PORTER{}+R supplies one. The schedule is \SABRE{}'s bidirectional layout
refinement~\cite{li2019tackling} applied to whole routes. Let $R(C,\ell)$
denote routing $C$ from $\ell$ and $\ell'$ the layout it ends on.
Pass~1 computes $R(C,\ell_0)$; pass~2 computes
$R(C^{\mathrm{rev}},\ell_1')$ on the reversed circuit and
\emph{discards its route}, keeping only $\ell_2'$; pass~3 computes
$R(C,\ell_2')$. Reversing $C$ reverses every dependency edge, so
routing it from the forward pass's end state yields a layout adapted to
the \emph{start} of the forward circuit. Odd pass counts above three
repeat the cycle; $P{=}5$ throughout. Because the best forward pass is
selected, \PORTER{}+R can never report a higher EPR count than the pass~1
route it contains --- at the cost of $P$ routes where one would do. It
adds no randomness.

One property of the schedule matters for reading its results. Refinement
consumes a post-routing layout, and routing destroys the per-core free-slot
reservation $\ell_0$ was built with (Section~\ref{sec:sfc-layout}), so an
intermediate pass may start from a layout worse than $\ell_0$ for
\PORTER{}'s own capacity machinery. Individual passes therefore regress on
some instances, and only the best-pass rule keeps that from surfacing.

\subsubsection{Layout-discount search (\PORTER{}+S)}
\label{sec:layoutsearch}

\PORTER{}+S searches the one free scalar in the layout heuristic --- the
per-layer discount $\gamma$ of Section~\ref{sec:sfc-layout}, fixed at
$0.99$ elsewhere --- in two phases.

\emph{Phase 1} builds a layout for each
$\gamma\in\{0.80,0.90,0.95,0.99,1.0\}$ and routes it once at the
configured horizon, without selection: five routes. The lowest-EPR layout
wins.
\emph{Phase 2} runs the $P{=}5$ refinement schedule above from two seed
layouts --- phase 1's winner, and the $\gamma{=}0.99$ layout when that is
not already the winner --- adding $P{-}1{=}4$ routes each. Refining the
winner is not reliably best: on 64q~QNN, $\gamma{=}1.0$ wins phase 1 at
$604$ and refines no further, while refining the $\gamma{=}0.99$ layout
reaches $537$, so both are run.

The total is therefore $5+4=9$ routes when $\gamma{=}0.99$ wins phase 1
and $5+4+4=13$ otherwise, which is the $9$--$13$ of
Table~\ref{tab:budgets}; a schedule that converges early can stop between
the two. $\gamma{=}0.99$ is always a candidate, so \PORTER{}+S cannot
return a higher EPR count than the configured single route.

\PORTER{}+SR runs horizon selection and the $\gamma$-search and keeps the
lower EPR of the two, netting the one route they share. The two look for
different things --- the horizon at a fixed layout against the layout at a
fixed horizon --- so neither subsumes the other.

Three notions of a matched budget are in play and none is universally
fair, so we report against more than one. Equal \emph{route counts}
(shared-layout \PORTER{} against shared-layout \dSABRE{}, three each)
equalise how often the router runs. Equal \emph{layout budgets} put
\PORTER{} on \dSABRE{}'s candidate set. Equal \emph{wall-clock} is not
enforced anywhere; instead every table reports complete compile time so
a reader can renormalise. \PORTER{} costs more per route than \dSABRE{}
(Section~\ref{sec:frontier}), so equal route counts favour it on time
and not on effort, and equal layout budgets do the reverse.

\subsection{Metrics and Aggregation}
\label{sec:metrics}

The primary metric is EPR pairs consumed. \TeleSABRE{} counts teledata
plus telegate operations, \dSABRE{} counts teledata, and \PORTER{} counts
teleports plus telegates plus telegate packets, one pair per packet
however many remote CX gates it amortises; all three count the same
physical resource. Intra-core SWAP counts and compile time are secondary
and are never traded against EPR by any selection rule.

\textbf{Timing.} All compile times are wall-clock on one otherwise idle
machine (Apple M2, macOS 26.5, CPython 3.13), measured once per
configuration rather than as a median of repetitions, with no warm-up:
each invocation is a fresh process, so no cache is carried between
circuits. Two consequences follow. Times are indicative rather than
precise, and should be read at the resolution the paper uses them --- as
ratios between protocols, not as absolute costs. And because horizon
selection guards its speculative routes by wall-clock
(Section~\ref{sec:horizon}), machine load can in principle change which
route it selects; the horizon each reported run chose is recorded with
its result.

Aggregates are unweighted geometric means over circuits,
$\bigl(\prod_i x_i\bigr)^{1/m}$, so each circuit counts equally and a
suite with large absolute counts does not dominate. Percentage changes
are $100(b/a-1)$ against the stated baseline $a$; ratios are $b/a$. Both
are computed from the geometric means, not averaged per circuit.

Failures are never imputed. A configuration that does not complete an
instance is marked and excluded from every geometric mean it appears in,
along with that instance's other columns where the comparison is paired.
A mean labelled $(n)$ is therefore over exactly the $n$ circuits all its
columns completed. This separates quality from completion, but it also
means a baseline that fails its hardest instances is scored only on the
ones it survives, which flatters it. We report completion counts
alongside every such mean.
