\section{Discussion and Future Work}
\label{sec:discussion}

\textbf{The two compilers are complementary, and a portfolio beats
either.} \PORTER{} wins ten of the twenty-four instances against
\dSABRE{} and loses twelve, \PORTER{}+R eleven and eleven
(Table~\ref{tab:main}), so neither dominates instance by instance even
though their pooled means differ by only $2\%$ ($174.9$ against $171.9$).
Taking the lower EPR count of the two per instance gives $155.9$, a
$9.3\%$ reduction on the better of them, and $152.1$ with \PORTER{}+R in
place of \PORTER{}, $11.6\%$ below \dSABRE{}. The gain is largest where
the two disagree most --- $16.3\%$ at 80 qubits against $2.5\%$ at 64 ---
and it is not an artefact of the two cheapest families: over the eighteen
instances excluding GHZ and Graphstate it is still $8.0\%$ and $9.0\%$.

That gap is what the pooled figure conceals, and it is the clearest
evidence that the two designs are not interchangeable: a compiler that
merely re-derived \dSABRE{}'s decisions by another route would leave a
portfolio with nothing to choose between. A $9$--$12\%$ gain instead
means each compiler reaches circuits the other does not, and by wide
margins where it does --- 80-qubit Multiplier at $2{,}411$ pairs against
$4{,}841$, 80-qubit QPEexact at $291$ against $347$, with \dSABRE{} ahead
elsewhere. Which of the differences between them produces any particular
win is not something this measurement can say: the two differ at once in
action vocabulary, candidate granularity, where preparation cost enters,
commitment, initial layout, horizon policy and route budget, and the
portfolio ranks outputs rather than separating causes. It is a property
of the pair rather than of either compiler: it costs both routes
($2.1\times$ \dSABRE{}'s compile time, twelve routes to nine), and no rule
we tested predicts the winner in advance.

\textbf{Why \TeleData{}-only can beat the full action set.} Packets beat
the single telegates they degrade into, yet a vocabulary with gate
teleportation removed altogether --- \TeleData{} and nothing else --- uses
no more EPR pairs than the full action set on any 80-qubit circuit it
completes, and fewer on most. The cause is a currency mismatch in the scorer: a packet's gain
counts remote CX gates resolved immediately, a \TeleData{} plan's a sum of
core-distance reductions discounted over a bounded horizon. Nothing
establishes the two are commensurate, and where a teleport repays past the
horizon the comparator cannot see it. That is evidence the mismatch costs
pairs at scale, not that spreading one pair over a run of gates is a bad
idea.

\subsection{Future Work}
\label{sec:future}

\textbf{On the compiler itself.} Four openings, the first a completion
guarantee to match \dSABRE{}'s. Adopting its recovery wholesale is the
obvious route, and the capacity half is nearly in place --- both treat
free slots as a legality condition with the same floor of two, and both
relieve by breadth-first search to a reservoir
(Section~\ref{sec:related}). What \dSABRE{} adds is atomicity: it relays
the chain in one all-or-nothing transaction, where \PORTER{} re-plans each
hop through the ordinary plan machinery, which is what lets the
opportunistic tier compete with normal work at all
(Section~\ref{sec:sfc-relief}). Nor would it buy what is missing.
\PORTER{} never reaches the capacity deadlock that proof rules out, and
the one failure we observe is a local-SWAP livelock --- heavy-hex star
Multiplier at a fixed horizon --- with capacity healthy throughout. The
guarantee needs a progress argument for the intra-core stage, not a
stronger capacity transaction. One configuration is worth trying first,
because two of our results point at it: \dSABRE{}'s proof is written for a
teleport-only router, and a teleport-only vocabulary is the cheapest we
measured at 80 qubits, so a \PORTER{} restricted to \TeleData{} is both
where the guarantee is likeliest to transfer and where the pairs are ---
though not for free, since that configuration completes twenty-one of the
twenty-four instances here.

The other three: a horizon that scales with the core count $K$ ---
$L_\mathrm{tp}$ is an absolute gate count, so a plan at twenty cores is
scored over proportionally less of the circuit than at four, and every
scale-up instance picks the longest candidate offered; \chg{a circuit
statistic that predicts the horizon rather than merely pruning it, since
$\rho$ averages over every qubit and the whole circuit where the horizon
acts on one moving qubit over a bounded depth --- a per-qubit or
horizon-limited density is the obvious candidate and is untried, and
without one the selection rule stays a portfolio fitted on the corpus it
is measured on (Section~\ref{sec:horizon}); and a comparator that weighs a resolved gate against a latent}
distance reduction on one scale, which the vocabulary result indicates the
present one does \chg{not. The mismatch is confined to
$\mathrm{total}$ itself: the selection key of
Section~\ref{sec:sfc-scoring} no longer carries a separate discounted
term, so a scale on which a resolved gate and a latent distance
reduction are commensurable would have to come from the summation, not
from a tie-break.}

\textbf{Beyond EPR count.} The trace \PORTER{} emits is unscheduled, so a
lower pair count does not by itself imply a shorter makespan or a
higher-fidelity result. A scheduling pass should batch inter-core actions
whose links, ports and preparation SWAPs do not conflict, and account for
entanglement-generation rate, storage lifetime and gate
error~\cite{baker2020time,ferrari2021compiler}; the objective should stay
multi-objective, since pair count, depth and fidelity trade off in
hardware-dependent ways. Lifetime bears on \PORTER{} in particular: a
packet holds one cat resource open across a whole run, so a lifetime bound
caps packet length in a way our cost model does not, and the vocabulary
result above would have to be re-read under it.

\textbf{Richer interconnects.} Every device evaluated here gives adjacent
cores a single link and each core one to four ports. Widening that is the
free variable: a second link turns an occupied port from an obstacle into
an alternative, relieving preparation cost first, and it interacts with
the capacity discipline, since more ports mean more ways to spend and to
recover a free slot. Where the return on added links falls off against
their hardware cost is a question for interconnect co-design.
