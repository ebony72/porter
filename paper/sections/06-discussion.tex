\section{Discussion and Future Work}
\label{sec:discussion}

The evidence supports a qualified native-protocol conclusion. Across
the 24 main-suite instances, \PORTER{} and \dSABRE{} have nearly equal
pooled geometric-mean EPR counts ($169.4$ and $171.9$, respectively), whereas
\PORTER{}+R reduces \PORTER{}'s count to $156.7$ at a higher complete
compile-time cost (Table~\ref{tab:main}). Neither router dominates per
instance: \PORTER{} records 11 wins, 10 losses, and 3 ties against
\dSABRE{}, while \PORTER{}+R records 14 wins, 8 losses, and 2 ties. Thus,
under the reported native protocols, \PORTER{} is competitive with
\dSABRE{} rather than uniformly superior.

\textbf{The two routers are complementary.} They obtain their lower EPR
counts on different instances. Selecting
the lower count for each instance gives $155.7$ with \PORTER{} and $150.5$
with \PORTER{}+R, which are $9.4\%$ and $12.5\%$ below \dSABRE{}'s pooled
geometric-mean EPR count, and $8.2\%$ and $10.3\%$ below it once GHZ and
Graphstate are excluded. The margin is a property of the pair rather than of
either compiler, and it indicates that the two designs are not
interchangeable.

The controlled experiments delimit what can be inferred from this
native-protocol comparison. With the initial layout controlled, \PORTER{}
and \dSABRE{} are close on the reported main suite, while the layout source
produces a larger EPR spread (Table~\ref{tab:shared}); the route budgets
remain different, so this is not a budget-matched ranking of the routing
loops. The ablations support a completion role for capacity
relief and for the \TeleGate{} family under the tested capacity policy, but
do not isolate a universal EPR benefit for either. On the six QFT and
QPEexact scale-up instances, the fixed horizon portfolio accounts for the
reported improvement, although its values were selected using the evaluation
corpus. These observations do not attribute any individual
native-protocol win to a single design choice: the compilers also differ in
primitive vocabulary, candidate granularity, commitment, preparation
scoring, layout construction, horizon policy, and route budget.

\textbf{The primitive ablation identifies a comparator question, not a
verdict against packets.} On the eight 80-qubit instances completed by
both full and no-packet variants, removing packets changes the
geometric-mean EPR count by only $0.9\%$, and the direction varies by
circuit. Disabling relief prevents completion on five of the nine 80-qubit
instances, but the full configuration is $0.5\%$ more expensive on the four
instances completed by both relief variants. Strictly removing \TeleGate{}
deadlocks seven of the nine; the reported teleport-only diagnostic also
changes relief eligibility and is therefore not a one-factor ablation.
Together, these
results motivate a better-calibrated comparison between immediate gate
resolution and the delayed benefit of a \TeleData{} move. They do not
establish that a restricted primitive set is globally preferable.

\subsection{Future Work}
\label{sec:future}

\textbf{Completion and progress guarantees.} \PORTER{} verifies every
emitted route and uses implementation guards to terminate a run, but it does
not claim successful completion for every feasible input. A useful next step
is to separate the capacity and intra-core-progress questions. \dSABRE{}'s
atomic relay mechanism may help establish a capacity argument, whereas the
observed fixed-horizon failure on heavy-hex star Multiplier is a local-SWAP
livelock with capacity still available. A completion proof for \PORTER{}
therefore also requires a progress argument for local preparation. Any
transfer of a teleport-only guarantee to a richer primitive vocabulary should
be proved or tested explicitly rather than inferred from the present
ablation.

\textbf{Horizon selection and scoring.} All six scale-up instances
select $L_\mathrm{tp}=128$, whereas the configured one-route default is
$L_\mathrm{tp}=16$. This motivates two experiments. First, the fixed
four-horizon portfolio should be validated on held-out circuit families or
architectures. Second, a predictor should be tested for its ability to
reduce the number of routing invocations without being fitted and evaluated
on the same corpus. A per-qubit or horizon-limited interaction statistic is
one candidate, because the scoring decision concerns moving one qubit over
bounded future depth. The selection key also compares immediate CX
resolution with predicted distance reduction. Calibrating these quantities on
a common scale, or learning a constrained ranking rule using held-out data,
would test whether the present heuristic comparator contributes to the
vocabulary-dependent outcomes.

\textbf{Scheduling and hardware-aware objectives.} The output trace is
unscheduled, so lower EPR consumption does not imply lower makespan or
higher fidelity. A subsequent scheduler should exploit non-conflicting links,
ports, and preparation SWAPs while accounting for entanglement generation
rate, storage lifetime, and gate error~\cite{baker2020time,ferrari2021compiler}.
The resulting objective should be multi-objective: EPR pairs, depth, and
fidelity can trade off differently across hardware. In particular, a
\TeleGatePacket{} keeps a cat resource live across a run; finite coherence
or entanglement lifetime can limit useful packet length and may change the
observed packet trade-off.

\textbf{Interconnect and layout co-design.} The evaluated machines provide
one link between adjacent cores and one to four ports per core. Additional
links or ports may create alternative preparations when one port is occupied,
but they also alter capacity management and hardware cost. The heavy-hex star
result further suggests that initial-layout reserve should depend on
core-graph role rather than be uniform. A systematic study should vary link
multiplicity, port placement, core-graph centrality, and reserve allocation,
then report EPR, completion, and scheduling-aware objectives together.
