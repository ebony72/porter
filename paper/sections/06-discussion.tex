\section{Discussion and Future Work}
\label{sec:discussion}

The results support a qualified conclusion. Under their native protocols,
\PORTER{} and \dSABRE{} have nearly equal pooled EPR geometric means on the
24 main-suite instances ($169.4$ and $171.9$, respectively), while
\PORTER{}+R reduces the \PORTER{} figure to $156.7$ at a higher complete
compile-time cost (Table~\ref{tab:main}). Neither compiler dominates
per instance: \PORTER{} records 11 wins, 10 losses, and 3 ties against
\dSABRE{}, and \PORTER{}+R records 14 wins, 8 losses, and 2 ties. The
appropriate interpretation is therefore complementarity under the reported
protocols, not a general ranking.

\textbf{Complementarity is an oracle observation, not a deployable
portfolio.} If both compilers are run and the lower EPR result is selected
afterward, the per-instance oracle minimum is $155.7$ for \PORTER{} and
$150.5$ for \PORTER{}+R, respectively. These values are $9.4\%$ and
$12.5\%$ below \dSABRE{}'s pooled geometric mean. The gap persists after
excluding GHZ and Graphstate, but it requires both compilation budgets and
post-hoc knowledge of the outcome. No selector tested here predicts the
winner in advance. Thus the result shows that the routers reach different
local optima; it does not provide an operational portfolio policy.

The controlled experiments help delimit, but do not fully explain, this
difference. With the initial layout controlled, the two routers are close
on the reported main suite, whereas changing the layout can have a larger
effect (Table~\ref{tab:shared}). The ablations show that capacity relief
and the \TeleGate{} family are important for completion under the tested
capacity policy. The scale-up experiment associates the improvement on its
QFT and QPEexact instances with long-horizon selection. These observations
do not attribute any individual native-protocol win to a single design
choice: the compilers also differ in primitive vocabulary, candidate
granularity, commitment, preparation scoring, layout construction, horizon
policy, and route budget.

\textbf{The primitive ablation identifies a comparator question, not a
verdict against packets.} On the 80-qubit suite, removing packets changes
EPR only slightly on their common completed subset, and the direction varies
by circuit. Strictly removing \TeleGate{} largely changes completion, so it
does not yield a clean EPR aggregate. The auxiliary teleport-only diagnostic
is not a one-factor ablation because it also changes relief eligibility.
Together, these results motivate a better-calibrated comparison between
immediate gate resolution and the delayed benefit of a \TeleData{} move.
They do not establish that a restricted primitive set is globally preferable.

\subsection{Future Work}
\label{sec:future}

\textbf{Completion and progress guarantees.} \PORTER{} verifies every
emitted route and uses implementation guards to terminate a run, but it does
not claim successful completion for every feasible input. A useful next step
is to separate the capacity and intra-core progress questions. dSABRE's
atomic relay mechanism may help establish a capacity argument, whereas the
observed fixed-horizon failure on heavy-hex star Multiplier is a local-SWAP
livelock with capacity remaining available. A completion proof for \PORTER{}
therefore also requires a progress argument for local preparation. Any
transfer of a teleport-only guarantee to a richer primitive vocabulary should
be proved or tested explicitly rather than inferred from the present
ablation.

\textbf{Horizon selection and scoring.} The scale-up cases all select
$L_\mathrm{tp}=128$, whereas the configured horizon is 16. This motivates
two experiments: first, validate the fixed four-horizon portfolio on held-out
families or architectures; second, test a predictor that can reduce the
number of routes without being fitted and evaluated on the same corpus. A
per-qubit or horizon-limited interaction statistic is one candidate, because
the scoring decision concerns a moving qubit over bounded future depth.
The selection key also compares immediate CX resolution with predicted
distance reduction. Calibrating these quantities on a common scale, or
learning a constrained ranking rule on held-out data, is a direct way to
test whether the present comparator causes the vocabulary-dependent results.

\textbf{Scheduling and hardware-aware objectives.} The output trace is
unscheduled, so lower EPR consumption does not imply lower makespan or
higher fidelity. A subsequent scheduler should exploit non-conflicting links,
ports, and preparation SWAPs while accounting for entanglement generation
rate, storage lifetime, and gate error~\cite{baker2020time,ferrari2021compiler}.
The resulting objective should be multi-objective: EPR pairs, depth, and
fidelity can trade off differently across hardware. In particular, a
\TeleGatePacket{} keeps a cat resource live across a run; finite coherence
or entanglement lifetime can limit useful packet length and may change the
observed packet trade-off.

\textbf{Interconnect and layout co-design.} The evaluated machines provide
one link between adjacent cores and one to four ports per core. Additional
links or ports may create alternative preparations when one port is occupied,
but they also alter capacity management and hardware cost. The heavy-hex star
result further suggests that initial-layout reserve should depend on
core-graph role rather than be uniform. A systematic study should vary link
multiplicity, port placement, core-graph centrality, and reserve allocation,
then report EPR, completion, and scheduling-aware objectives together.
