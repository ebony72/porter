\section{The \PORTER{} Compiler}
\label{sec:sfc}

\PORTER{} is a deterministic heuristic router for a circuit DAG $C$ and
an initial layout $\ell_0$.  At a fixed teleport-lookahead horizon
$L_\mathrm{tp}$, it repeatedly classifies the DAG front
(Section~\ref{sec:sfc-classify}), executes all immediately executable work,
routes local CX gates by intra-core SWAPs (Section~\ref{sec:sfc-intra}), and
otherwise selects one inter-core \emph{plan}, prepares it, and executes it
(Algorithm~\ref{alg:sfc}).  The plan-first stage
separates the value of an inter-core action from the local SWAPs needed to
make that action executable.  The multi-horizon protocol that invokes this
routing pass repeatedly is defined in Section~\ref{sec:horizon}.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\PORTER{} \texttt{compile}$(C,\ell_0)$}
\label{alg:sfc}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE $\ell\leftarrow\ell_0$; initialise the active and front sets of the DAG
\WHILE{the DAG has active gates and no implementation guard has fired}
\STATE $F\leftarrow$ current DAG front; classify its gates
\IF{a 1Q gate or a directly executable local CX is in $F$}
  \STATE execute all such gates; remove them from the DAG; \textbf{continue}
\ENDIF
\IF{a local CX in $F$ needs routing}
  \STATE apply the best intra-core SWAP under Eq.~\ref{eq:sfc-local}; \textbf{continue}
\ENDIF
\STATE generate normal plans $\mathcal P$ and blocked plans $\mathcal B$
\STATE score all plans and choose the first normal plan in key order
\IF{no normal plan is available}
  \STATE choose a tail-relief plan seeded by $\mathcal B$
\ELSIF{$\mathcal B\ne\emptyset$ and its best member exceeds the selected
normal plan by more than $m_\mathrm{assist}$}
  \STATE replace the selection by an opportunistic-relief plan
\ENDIF
\IF{no plan was selected} \STATE report capacity deadlock; abort \ENDIF
\STATE prepare the selected plan by local SWAPs, then execute it
\ENDWHILE
\RETURN emitted operations, final layout, and statistics
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The implementation additionally bounds the total iterations and consecutive
iterations that retire no gate, and detects repeated preparation states.  These
guards ensure termination of an implementation run (Section~\ref{sec:sfc-progress}).

\subsection{Initial Layout}
\label{sec:sfc-layout}

When no external layout is supplied, \PORTER{} constructs one deterministic
layout with at least $f_\mathrm{min}=2$ initially free physical positions in
each core.  The same value has three distinct uses: it is the initial reserve,
the admission threshold for a normal \TeleData{} destination, and the free-slot
threshold defining a relief reservoir.  It is not a persistent lower bound:
a teleport admitted at exactly $f_\mathrm{min}$ leaves its destination with
one free position.

The layout heuristic is temporal locality-greedy.  Each CX
$g=(q_i,q_j)$ contributes $\gamma^{\mathrm{asap}(g)}$ to an interaction
weight $w(q_i,q_j)$, where $\gamma=0.99$ and $\mathrm{asap}(g)$ is the
CX-only DAG layer of $g$.  Logical qubits are visited in first-CX-occurrence
order and assigned, when possible, to the core containing their heaviest
already assigned neighbour while preserving the reserve.  Within a core,
more strongly interacting logical qubits receive more central physical
positions.  This favours early, repeated interactions. Its one weakness is that the reserve is uniform across
cores, which suits a grid or a ring but not a core graph with a hub
(Section~\ref{sec:heavyhex}).

\subsection{Front Classification}
\label{sec:sfc-classify}

Under layout $\ell$, every front gate is one of the following:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item a 1Q gate, which is directly executable;
\item a direct-local CX, whose operands are adjacent in one core;
\item a local CX needing SWAPs, whose operands share a core but are not adjacent;
\item an adjacent-remote CX, whose operand cores are neighbours; or
\item a non-adjacent-remote CX.
\end{itemize}
\PORTER{} drains directly executable gates first.  It next routes local CX
gates, and enters the remote stage only when every front CX is remote.  This
priority keeps ordinary intra-core routing separate from inter-core decisions.

\subsection{Intra-Core SWAP Scoring}
\label{sec:sfc-intra}

For the local-CX set $F_\mathrm{loc}$, candidate SWAPs are coupling-graph
edges incident to positions touched by $F_\mathrm{loc}$.  A candidate $a$ is
evaluated under its post-SWAP layout $\ell_a$ by the SABRE-style objective
\begin{equation}
J(a)=\bar d_{\mathcal F}(a)+\lambda_\mathrm{loc}\bar d_{\mathcal E}(a),
\qquad \lambda_\mathrm{loc}=0.8,
\label{eq:sfc-local}
\end{equation}
where the two terms are mean intra-core distances over $F_\mathrm{loc}$ and
over up to $N_\mathrm{loc}=20$ additional local CX gates from the next
$L_\mathrm{loc}=12$ DAG layers.  The router applies the candidate with the
smallest $J$, breaking ties by front-layer distance and then deterministically.

\subsection{Plans and the Plan-First Remote Stage}
\label{sec:sfc-plan-first}
\label{sec:sfc-relief}

A \emph{plan} is one candidate inter-core action across a single link.  It
records its primitive, its source and destination cores, the link and ports it
uses, the gates it discharges, and the front CX it was generated from --- for
a relief plan, the CX behind the blocked plan it serves rather than one the
action itself advances.  It deliberately does \emph{not} record a
preparation-SWAP sequence.  Thus plans answer ``what remote action should be
taken next?''; preparation answers ``how do we make that action executable
from the present layout?'' (Section~\ref{sec:sfc-commit}).

The primitive is one of $\{\TeleData{},\TeleGate{},\TeleGatePacket{}\}$.
A \TeleData{} plan moves one logical qubit by one core-graph hop and
immediately discharges no CX.  A \TeleGate{} plan discharges one
adjacent-remote CX.  A \TeleGatePacket{} plan discharges an ordered,
same-control sequence of adjacent-remote CX gates
(Section~\ref{sec:sfc-packet}).  For the gate-teleportation
family, the plan's source and destination denote the control-side and
target-side cores; for \TeleData{} they denote the sending and receiving cores.

\textbf{Three kinds of plan.}  Every plan the router handles is
\emph{normal}, \emph{blocked} or \emph{relief}.  The three share the record
above, the score of Section~\ref{sec:sfc-scoring} and the readiness
predicate of Section~\ref{sec:sfc-commit}; they differ in how they are
generated and in what the router is permitted to do with them.

\textbf{Normal plans} $\mathcal P$ are the actions that could fire once
prepared, generated from the remote front.  For each adjacent-remote front
CX, $\mathcal P$ contains a \TeleGate{} plan, a packet plan when a packet of
at least two gates is available, and each direction of \TeleData{} whose
receiving core has at least $f_\mathrm{min}$ free positions.  For a
non-adjacent-remote CX it contains only one-hop \TeleData{} moves that
strictly reduce the distance between operand cores and meet the same capacity
test.  \PORTER{} commits to the best of them.

\textbf{Blocked plans} $\mathcal B$ are the \TeleData{} moves that would
otherwise be generated but whose destination fails that capacity test.  They
are never executed, since committing one would drive a core below the floor.
They are nevertheless generated and scored, because each of them records a
saturated destination core together with the score of the move that core is
refusing.  Read as a set, $\mathcal B$ therefore reports which cores are
congested and how much progress is being forgone at each, and this is the
only information the two relief rules below consult.

\textbf{Relief plans} are generated on demand, to create space for a
blocked one.  A relief plan is always generated \emph{from} a particular
blocked plan, and $c$ denotes that blocked plan's own destination core ---
the core whose shortage stopped it.  A breadth-first search from $c$ finds a
nearest \emph{reservoir}, a core with at least $f_\mathrm{min}$ free
positions; the relief plan is the \TeleData{} moving one logical qubit from
the penultimate core of that path into the reservoir.
Only that final hop is committed, and after it executes the router
regenerates every plan, so a multi-hop route propagates free capacity one hop
at a time rather than reserving an atomic relay
(Section~\ref{sec:related}).  A relief plan is always newly generated for the
purpose; the blocked plan that motivated it is never itself committed.

\textbf{When each fires.}  Normally the best member of $\mathcal P$ is
committed.  Two rules override that, and they consult $\mathcal B$
differently.  \emph{Tail relief} fires reactively, when $\mathcal P$ is empty
and the front is starved: a reservoir search is run from the destination core
of \emph{every} blocked plan, and the relief plans so generated compete with
one another, shortest search path first and then on the ordinary selection
key.  \emph{Opportunistic relief} fires pre-emptively, while a normal plan is
still available, when the \emph{best-scoring} blocked plan out-scores that
normal plan by more than $m_\mathrm{assist}=5.0$; only that one blocked
plan's destination core seeds the search, the judgement being that clearing
the congestion it names is worth more than taking the best currently legal
action.  Both tiers commit a single hop, and scoring relief on the same key
as ordinary work is what lets the opportunistic tier compete with it.

\subsection{Plan Scoring and the Selection Key}
\label{sec:sfc-scoring}

Every candidate receives the lexicographic key
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{key}(\pln p)=
\bigl(-\mathrm{score}(\pln p),\;\mathrm{prep}(\pln p),\;-f_\mathrm{dst}(\pln p)\bigr),
\label{eq:sfc-key}
\end{equation}
ordered from smallest to largest, with a stable identifier as the final tie
break.  Here $f_\mathrm{dst}$ is the free capacity of a \TeleData{}
destination and is defined to be zero for the \TeleGate{} family.  Hence the
primary comparison is always $\mathrm{score}$; readiness cost matters only
when the score ties, and capacity is a final tie break.  In particular, it also
implements a \TeleData{}-first preference when a teleport and a gate plan
tie on $\mathrm{score}$, because an admissible teleport has
$f_\mathrm{dst}\ge f_\mathrm{min}$.

$\mathrm{score}$ ranks plans; it is not a common physical unit: gate
teleportation counts CX gates discharged immediately, whereas a data teleport
is valued by current and predicted reductions in core distance.  Specifically,
a \TeleGate{} has $\mathrm{score}=1$, and a packet has $\mathrm{score}$
equal to the number of CX gates in its collected sequence.

For a \TeleData{} plan moving $q$ from $c_\mathrm{src}$ to
$c_\mathrm{dst}$, let $\kappa$ and $\kappa'$ map logical qubits to cores
before and after that one-hop move.  The progress of a CX $g=(q_i,q_j)$ is
\begin{equation}
\Delta(g)=\dC\bigl(\kappa(q_i),\kappa(q_j)\bigr)-
\dC\bigl(\kappa'(q_i),\kappa'(q_j)\bigr).
\end{equation}
Thus $\Delta(g)\in\{-1,0,1\}$.  Forward simulation under $\kappa'$ forms
three disjoint sets of CX gates incident on $q$: $D(\pln p)$ contains the
seed front CX; $L(\pln p)$ contains CXs exposed while locally executable
gates are repeatedly retired; and $A_k(\pln p)$ contains gates reached after
that local closure in simulated layer $k$, for $1\le k\le L_\mathrm{tp}$.
The first two describe immediate/local-closure progress; the third is
lookahead.  With
$w_k=\max(w_\mathrm{min},w_0\gamma^{k-1})$,
$w_0=0.9$, $\gamma=0.9$, and $w_\mathrm{min}=0.5$,
\begin{align}
\mathrm{direct}(\pln p)&=
 \sum_{g\in D(\pln p)\cup L(\pln p)}\Delta(g),\\[-2pt]
\mathrm{lookahead}(\pln p)&=
 \sum_{k=1}^{L_\mathrm{tp}}\ \sum_{g\in A_k(\pln p)}w_k\Delta(g),
\nonumber\\[-2pt]
\mathrm{score}(\pln p)&=
 \mathrm{direct}(\pln p)+\mathrm{lookahead}(\pln p).
\nonumber
\end{align}

The best member of $\mathcal P$ under key~\eqref{eq:sfc-key} is
committed, unless one of the two relief rules of
Section~\ref{sec:sfc-plan-first} overrides that choice.

\subsection{Commitment and Preparation}
\label{sec:sfc-commit}

A plan is \emph{ready} in layout $\ell$, written
$\mathrm{ready}(\pln p,\ell)$, when the required logical endpoint(s) are
adjacent to their designated communication ports, the required ports are
available, and, for \TeleData{}, the destination satisfies the capacity rule.
The readiness heuristic $\mathrm{prep}(\pln p,\ell)$ is the sum of the
intra-core distances from required endpoints (or the destination free slot)
to their required ports.  It is zero exactly for a ready plan.  

Because plans are scored before any plan-specific preparation SWAP
exists, the choice is made on what an action is worth rather than on what it
would cost to set up.  \PORTER{} commits to the
selected plan, performs only local SWAPs intended to reduce its readiness
cost, and executes the plan once it is ready.  It does not reopen the remote
choice during that preparation.  Commitment therefore prevents local moves
made for one remote action from being reinterpreted as preparation for a
different action.

For an unmet endpoint requirement, preparation considers incident
intra-core SWAPs.  For an occupied \TeleData{} destination port, it considers
SWAPs that move a nearest free position toward that port without swapping
through the port prematurely.  Candidates are ranked by post-SWAP
$\mathrm{prep}$, and at most one move per plan side is applied in a
preparation round.

Under the stated connectivity and capacity assumptions, a legal preparation
sequence exists for a committed plan.  Every core retains a free position,
a teleport entering a destination only when it has at least
$f_\mathrm{min}=2$ and therefore leaving one, so on a connected core that
free position can be walked to an occupied port to clear it, and the endpoint
then walked to a neighbour of that port along a path meeting no edge incident
to it --- at most a core diameter of local SWAPs apiece.  The two sides lie in
different cores and a local SWAP moves no qubit between cores, so neither side
can undo the other and the capacity rule survives preparation.

\subsection{Same-Control Packet Collection}
\label{sec:sfc-packet}

A packet is discovered by forward simulation because CX gates sharing a
control are generally chained in the DAG and therefore do not appear in the
front together.  From an adjacent-remote seed $g=(q_c,q_t)$ whose target lies
in core $c_t$, the collector simulates up to 120 layers.  It absorbs up to eight
front CX gates with control $q_c$ and target in $c_t$, together with executable
1Q gates already in $c_t$, and stops at the first unproductive layer or the
size cap.  A packet is generated only if at least two CX gates are collected.

Packet readiness means readiness to establish the cat resource, not readiness
of every later target.  Once committed, the router emits a single
cat-entangler; then, for each recorded target in turn, it routes that target
beside the target-side port without disturbing either reserved port and emits
the target's CX against that port; and it finally emits one
cat-disentangler.  The recorded order follows the DAG, so the packet plan may
safely count all of its members as discharged work.

\subsection{Progress and the Failure Model}
\label{sec:sfc-progress}

The capacity discipline prevents routing from creating a completely full core:
every normal or relief \TeleData{} is admitted only when its destination has
at least two free positions, and therefore leaves at least one.  A supplied
layout with a full core is first legalised by an eviction if another core can
receive it; otherwise the input is infeasible.

This invariant does not prove universal successful routing.  If normal plans
are absent but blocked plans identify a reachable reservoir, tail relief creates
a new candidate.  If no such candidate exists, \PORTER{} reports a capacity
deadlock. Independently, the implementation bounds every loop --- repeated
preparation states, an iteration budget and a no-progress limit --- safeguards
on the greedy realisation of the policy rather than conditions the argument of
Section~\ref{sec:sfc-plan-first} needs.  Thus every emitted route is checked
for legality, and every implementation run terminates, but successful
completion on every feasible input is not claimed.
