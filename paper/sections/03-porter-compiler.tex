\section{The \PORTER{} Compiler}
\label{sec:sfc}

Given a circuit and an initial layout $\ell_0$ (which \PORTER{} builds
itself, Section~\ref{sec:sfc-layout}), \PORTER{} repeats the following
loop (Algorithm~\ref{alg:sfc}) until every gate has been emitted or a
configurable iteration budget is exhausted. Algorithm~\ref{alg:sfc}
charts one iteration; its shaded region is the plan-first remote stage
that distinguishes \PORTER{} from compilers deciding at
single-action granularity; \appExample{} walks through it on a concrete
front where it changes the outcome.


\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\PORTER{} \texttt{compile}$(C, \ell_0)$}
\label{alg:sfc}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE $\ell \leftarrow \ell_0$; initialise DAG active/frontier sets from $C$
\WHILE{DAG has active gates}
  \STATE $F \leftarrow$ DAG front (bounded by \texttt{dag\_front\_limit})
  \STATE classify $F$ into singles, direct-local CX, local-CX-needing-SWAP,
         adjacent-remote CX, non-adjacent-remote CX \hfill\textit{(Sec.~\ref{sec:sfc-classify})}
  \IF{singles or direct-local CX present}
    \STATE execute them all; remove from DAG; \textbf{continue}
  \ENDIF
  \IF{local-CX-needing-SWAP present}
    \STATE generate candidate intra-core SWAPs, apply the best-scoring one
           (Eq.~\ref{eq:sfc-local}); \textbf{continue} \hfill\textit{(Sec.~\ref{sec:sfc-intra})}
  \ENDIF
  \STATE generate normal remote plans $\mathcal{P}$ and blocked-teleport
         plans $\mathcal{B}$ \hfill\textit{(Sec.~\ref{sec:sfc-scoring})}
  \IF{$\mathcal{P}\neq\emptyset$}
    \STATE $p^\ast \leftarrow$ best ready-or-preparable plan in $\mathcal{P}$
    \IF{some $b\in\mathcal{B}$ has gain $\gg$ gain$(p^\ast)$}
      \STATE $p^\ast \leftarrow$ best opportunistic capacity-relief plan for $b$
             \hfill\textit{(Sec.~\ref{sec:sfc-relief})}
    \ENDIF
  \ELSE
    \STATE BFS from each blocked target core to its nearest reservoir;
           $p^\ast \leftarrow$ best tail-relief plan \hfill\textit{(Sec.~\ref{sec:sfc-relief})}
  \ENDIF
  \IF{$p^\ast$ is \texttt{None}} \STATE \textbf{report deadlock; abort} \ENDIF
  \STATE apply $p^\ast$'s own preparation SWAPs until ready, then execute it
\ENDWHILE
\RETURN emitted operations, final layout, stats
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Initial Layout}
\label{sec:sfc-layout}

\PORTER{} builds $\ell_0$ with one deterministic heuristic --- no
external partitioner, no best-of-$N$ search --- and enforces the
invariant the routing loop depends on: every core keeps at least
$f_\mathrm{min}{=}2$ free positions, so any core can serve as a
\TeleData{} destination and as a relief reservoir from the first
iteration.

\textbf{Temporal locality-greedy.} Three steps.
\emph{(i) Weighting}: each CX gate $g=(q_i,q_j)$ contributes
$\gamma^{\mathrm{asap}(g)}$ to the interaction weight $w(q_i,q_j)$, where
$\mathrm{asap}(g)$ is $g$'s as-soon-as-possible layer in the CX-only DAG and
$\gamma{=}0.99$, so early interactions weigh more than late ones while
repeated interactions accumulate. \emph{(ii) Core assignment}: logical
qubits are visited in the order they first appear as a CX operand and
each is placed in the core holding its heaviest already-placed
neighbour when that core has headroom above $f_\mathrm{min}$, falling
back to the core with the most remaining reserved capacity.
\emph{(iii) Physical placement}: within each core, logicals are laid
onto positions by descending interaction weight onto ascending
intra-core centrality, so the most-connected qubits take the most
central slots.

The steps are complementary. The discount co-locates the pairs that
must interact soonest; first-appearance order handles chain-structured
circuits, where a balanced-first-layer rule would scatter the chain
across cores; and the centrality step keeps hub qubits cheap to reach.
The construction costs microseconds and uses no randomness, so \PORTER{}
has no seed to report. Its one weakness is that the $f_\mathrm{min}$
reservation is uniform across cores, which suits a grid or a ring but
not a core graph with a hub (Section~\ref{sec:heavyhex}).

\subsection{Front Classification}
\label{sec:sfc-classify}

Every front gate is classified under the current layout $\ell$. 1Q
gates are always directly executable; a CX $g=(q_i,q_j)$ with both
operands in core $c$ is \emph{direct-local} if
$\dL(c,\ell(q_i),\ell(q_j))=1$ and \emph{local-needing-SWAP}
otherwise; a CX whose operands lie in different cores is
\emph{adjacent-remote} if those cores are core-graph neighbours and
\emph{non-adjacent-remote} otherwise. Directly executable gates drain
first and completely, intra-core SWAPs are exhausted next, and
remote-stage decisions are taken only once no front gate is
intra-core. The front is size-capped at $\mathrm{dag\_front\_limit}{=}96$ gates to bound
per-iteration cost. Front gates are pairwise qubit-disjoint, so the front
can never exceed the logical-qubit count $n$; single-qubit gates are not
excluded by that bound, however, and on the three QPEexact instances of the
scale-up suite the front does reach $n$ (100, 200 and 360) and the cap
binds. It is inert where it binds: truncation is by ascending gate index,
directly executable gates drain before any routing decision is taken, and
the discarded gates therefore reappear on the following iteration.
Re-routing all three with the cap removed leaves EPR and SWAP counts
unchanged. On every other instance evaluated here the front stays at or
below $47$ gates and the cap does not bind. Both figures are reproduced by
\texttt{probe\_front\_limit.py} in the artifact.

\subsection{Intra-Core SWAP Scoring}
\label{sec:sfc-intra}

\SABRE{}~\cite{li2019tackling} routes a single-core circuit by
repeatedly applying the SWAP that minimises a front-layer distance term
plus a weighted lookahead term, each a $\Delta$ (old distance minus new)
over gates sharing a qubit with the SWAP. \PORTER{} uses that form here
and extends it to inter-core plans in Section~\ref{sec:sfc-scoring}.

For a local-needing-SWAP gate set $F_\mathrm{loc}$, \PORTER{} builds
candidate SWAP edges from the coupling-graph neighbours of every
physical position touched by $F_\mathrm{loc}$, simulates each SWAP, and
scores it:
\begin{equation}
  J(a) = \bar d_{\mathcal{F}}(a) + \lambda_\mathrm{loc}\,\bar d_{\mathcal{E}}(a),
  \qquad \lambda_\mathrm{loc}=0.8,
  \label{eq:sfc-local}
\end{equation}
where $\bar d_{\mathcal{F}}(a)$ is the mean, over the gates
$(q_i,q_j)\in F_\mathrm{loc}$, of the intra-core distance
$\dL\bigl(c,\ell_a(q_i),\ell_a(q_j)\bigr)$ under the post-SWAP layout
$\ell_a$, and $\bar d_{\mathcal{E}}(a)$ is the same mean taken over an
extended set $\mathcal{E}_\mathrm{loc}$ of up to
$N_\mathrm{loc}{=}20$ local CX gates collected from the next
$L_\mathrm{loc}{=}12$ DAG layers via a virtual frontier simulation
(Section~\ref{sec:sfc-simstate}). Ties are broken first by the raw
front-layer distance, then, if still tied, by a decision uniformly
sampled from a constant-seeded random-number generator, with every
such tie-break recorded in \texttt{RouterStats} for reproducibility
auditing.

\subsection{Plan-First Remote Stage}
\label{sec:sfc-plan-first}

\PORTER{} manipulates remote work as \emph{plans}. A plan is a
complete, committable specification of one inter-core action: which
primitive fires, which gate or same-control run it resolves, its
direction, and consequently its \emph{readiness conditions} --- which
logical endpoint must sit adjacent to which communication port, and
which ports must be free. What it deliberately leaves unspecified is
the SWAP sequence that establishes those conditions. Alternatives are
compared at the level of \emph{what} remote work to do next, each
scored as if its preconditions were met; only the committed plan is
handed to the machinery that works out \emph{how}.

Formally, a plan is a tuple
\[
  p \;=\; \bigl(\pi,\; G(p),\; \mathrm{dir},\; c_\mathrm{src},\;
                c_\mathrm{dst},\; \Pi(p),\; \mathrm{ready}(p,\ell)\bigr),
\]
with $\pi\in\{\TeleData{},\TeleGate{},\TeleGatePacket{}\}$ the primitive,
$G(p)$ the front or collected gates it resolves, $\mathrm{dir}$ its
orientation, $c_\mathrm{src}$ and $c_\mathrm{dst}$ the cores involved,
$\Pi(p)$ the communication ports it reserves, and
$\mathrm{ready}(p,\ell)$ the predicate on layouts that holds when $p$ can
fire. Three quantities are kept distinct: the plan's \emph{utility},
evaluated under the hypothesis $\mathrm{ready}(p,\ell)$ regardless of
whether it currently holds (Section~\ref{sec:sfc-scoring}); its
\emph{readiness cost}, used only to break ties; and the \emph{preparation
policy} that chooses local SWAPs once $p$ is committed. Two design
decisions follow and are what distinguish the remote stage: utility never
includes readiness cost, and a committed plan is not reopened until
$\mathrm{ready}$ holds. Section~\ref{sec:related} places both against the
baselines' choices; Section~\ref{sec:ablation} measures the second.

When the front holds only remote CX gates, \PORTER{} generates two
disjoint candidate sets. \textbf{Normal plans} $\mathcal{P}$ hold, for
each adjacent-remote CX, a \TeleGate{}; a \TeleGatePacket{} if a
same-control run of $\ge 2$ remote CX gates can be collected
(Section~\ref{sec:sfc-packet}); and, for either endpoint, a \TeleData{}
plan into the partner's core if that core has $f_\mathrm{min}{=}2$ free
positions. Non-adjacent-remote CX gates admit only distance-reducing
\TeleData{} hops with sufficient destination capacity.
\textbf{Blocked plans} $\mathcal{B}$ hold the same \TeleData{}
candidates for destinations failing the $f_\mathrm{min}$ test. They are
never executed. They exist to detect two situations: every destination
saturated, so $\mathcal{P}$ is empty; and a blocked plan promising much
more than the best available one.

Every plan is scored (Section~\ref{sec:sfc-scoring}) \emph{before} any
preparation SWAP for it exists. \PORTER{} then takes the best plan in
$\mathcal{P}$ that is ready or admits an improving preparation SWAP. If
the best blocked plan leads it by more than $m_\mathrm{assist}{=}5.0$,
it runs opportunistic relief on that saturated core instead; if
$\mathcal{P}$ is empty, it runs the same relief search on
$\mathcal{B}$'s destinations, shortest hop count first
(Section~\ref{sec:sfc-relief}). Once committed it applies \emph{only
that plan's} preparation SWAPs, until the plan is ready, no improving
SWAP remains, or the decision state --- plan, layout, RNG --- repeats,
which raises rather than loops.

\textbf{Preparation SWAPs.}\label{sec:sfc-prep} A committed plan has
exactly two readiness requirements, one per side: source and destination
for \TeleData{}, control and target for \TeleGate{} and
\TeleGatePacket{}, whose target side is routed at execution time
(Section~\ref{sec:sfc-packet}). For each unmet requirement, candidate
SWAPs are the edges incident to the plan's logical endpoint, or to a
nearest free position tied for minimum distance to the relevant port
while that port stays occupied. Each is scored by the post-SWAP
readiness cost, and at most one improving SWAP per side is applied per
pass.

\subsection{Plan Scoring: Direct, Latent, and Lookahead Gain}
\label{sec:sfc-scoring}

Every plan $p$ scores $\mathrm{total}(p) = \mathrm{direct}(p) +
\mathrm{lookahead}(p)$. All kinds share one convention: for a CX gate
$g=(q_i,q_j)$ and a candidate relocation,
$\Delta(g) = \dC(\kappa(q_i),\kappa(q_j)) -
\dC(\kappa'(q_i),\kappa'(q_j))$ is the reduction in core-graph distance
between $g$'s two cores.

A \textbf{\TeleGate{}} scores a constant direct gain of $1$, one remote
CX resolved, with no lookahead. A \textbf{\TeleGatePacket{}} scores the
number of remote CX gates in the collected packet; those are current
work, not speculation, since the whole packet executes under one
cat-entangler resource once started. A \textbf{\TeleData{}} plan
partitions the affected gates into three sets, all found by simulating
the DAG frontier under the hypothetical post-teleport assignment:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=0pt,topsep=2pt]
\item $D(p)$, front CX gates already on the moving qubit, plus the seed
  gate;
\item $L(p)$, CX gates on the moving qubit that surface while the
  simulator progresses on 1Q and now-local gates alone --- current
  pressure rather than prediction, since the teleport fixes their core
  distance the moment they appear;
\item $A_k(p)$, gates on the moving qubit found in simulated layer
  $k \le L_\mathrm{tp}$, discounted by
  $w_k = \max(w_\mathrm{min}, w_0\gamma^{k-1})$ with $w_0{=}0.9$,
  $\gamma{=}0.9$, $w_\mathrm{min}{=}0.5$.
\end{itemize}
Then
\begin{align}
  \mathrm{direct}(p)    &= \!\!\!\sum_{g\in D(p)\cup L(p)}\!\!\!\Delta(g),
    \label{eq:sfc-teleport}\\[-2pt]
  \mathrm{lookahead}(p) &= \sum_{k\ge1}\ \sum_{g\in A_k(p)} w_k\,\Delta(g),
    \nonumber
\end{align}
so $L(p)$ enters at full weight alongside $D(p)$. A capacity-risk term
$1/\max(1,f_\mathrm{dst}{-}1)$ is tracked for tie-breaking but never
added to the score.

Preparation cost appears nowhere in $\mathrm{total}$. Plans are ranked
on gain; readiness cost enters only as the third tie-break, after gain
and plan kind. The horizon $L_\mathrm{tp}$ counts \emph{layers}, and at
most one gate on the moving qubit can appear per layer, since a qubit
has at most one zero-predecessor gate at a time. A gate cap
$N_\mathrm{tp}$ therefore cannot bind whenever
$N_\mathrm{tp}\ge L_\mathrm{tp}$, so $L_\mathrm{tp}$ alone governs how
far the estimate looks. It is the one parameter \PORTER{} does not fix
by constant; choosing it is a protocol question, deferred to
Section~\ref{sec:horizon}.

\subsection{Same-Control Packet Collection}
\label{sec:sfc-packet}

Same-control CX gates are chained by DAG dependency edges, so they
never appear in the front together: a packet cannot be read off the
front and must be collected by forward simulation.
From a seed adjacent-remote CX $g=(q_c,q_t)$ with target core $c_t$,
\PORTER{} simulates the DAG frontier forward for up to $120$ layers,
absorbing any front CX that shares control $q_c$ and has its target in
$c_t$ (up to $8$ gates), and any front 1Q gate whose qubit is already in
$c_t$, since those execute freely while the cat resource is live.
Collection stops at the first unproductive layer or at the size cap. The
window counts simulated layers, not collected CXs, so it acts as a cost
guard rather than a tuning knob. Because the simulation reaches past the
current front, a packet may contain gates a front-limited scan could not.
Collection is a heuristic in one respect we have not ablated: stopping at
the first unproductive layer can miss a profitable packet separated by
harmless intervening work. The collected order is
replayed exactly at commit time as one \texttt{cat\_entangler}
(consuming the packet's single pair), one \texttt{cat\_cx} per member
--- each target routed to its port by ordinary local SWAPs forbidden
from touching the two live ports --- and a closing
\texttt{cat\_disentangler}.

\subsection{Capacity Relief}
\label{sec:sfc-relief}

\textbf{Tail-relief BFS.} When every remote plan is capacity-blocked,
\PORTER{} runs a breadth-first search from each blocked destination core
to the nearest \emph{reservoir} core --- one with at least $f_\mathrm{min}$
free slots --- optionally chained beyond one hop
(\texttt{capacity\_relief\_chain\_enabled}). Every logical qubit
currently in the BFS path's penultimate core becomes a tail-relief
\TeleData{} candidate into the reservoir, scored and selected by the
same plan machinery, grouped and tried in ascending hop-count order.

\textbf{Opportunistic relief.} Even when a normal plan is available,
a severely blocked plan can still win if relieving its target core's
capacity would unlock substantially more gain
(Section~\ref{sec:sfc-plan-first}); this differs from tail-relief BFS
in firing pre-emptively, before any plan is actually starved, whenever
the margin $m_\mathrm{assist}$ is exceeded.

\subsection{Progress and the Failure Model}
\label{sec:sfc-progress}

Deterministic greedy routing on a capacity-constrained machine can
stall. \PORTER{} layers four defences against that.

\emph{(i) Keep capacity from reaching zero.} A core with no free
physical qubit is terminal: it can never vacate an occupied
communication port with local SWAPs, so no inter-core action can touch
it and its qubits are stranded. \PORTER{} legalises the entry layout so
no core starts full, and admits a \TeleData{} plan into core $c$ as a
normal plan only while $\mathrm{free}(c) \ge f_\mathrm{min}{=}2$.
Routing therefore cannot drive a core into the terminal state. Only an
externally supplied layout can present one, which is why \appPytket{}'s
pytket-dqc source is the only place the compiler reports
\emph{infeasibility}.

\emph{(ii) Detect starvation rather than thrash.} An empty $\mathcal{P}$
alongside a non-empty $\mathcal{B}$ signals that the front is starved by
capacity, and names the cores responsible. \emph{(iii) Manufacture
capacity.} Tail-relief BFS turns that signal into work
(Section~\ref{sec:sfc-relief}). \emph{(iv) Bound every loop.} Plan sides
with infinite preparation score are never selected; the compiler hashes
its decision state once per preparation round and raises if it repeats;
two budgets bound the loops from outside; and when no relief path reaches
a reservoir it raises a diagnostic naming the saturated cores.

These defences give two of the three guarantees a router might offer.
\emph{Correctness}: a returned
operation stream realises every gate of the input circuit and violates no
coupling or port rule. \emph{Termination}: every run ends, either in
success or by raising a diagnostic or tripping a guard.
\emph{Completion}: that a run ends in success. \PORTER{} provides the
first two by construction. It does \emph{not} provide the third, and has
no analogue of \dSABRE{}'s termination-with-success proof under stated
architectural hypotheses (Section~\ref{sec:related}); its completion
record is empirical, and Section~\ref{sec:results} reports it.

A run that does not complete does so in one of four ways, and we use
these terms consistently. A capacity \emph{deadlock} leaves no legal plan
and no relief path, and raises a diagnostic. A \emph{livelock} makes
local progress indefinitely while retired gates and EPR count stand
still, and is stopped by the iteration guard. A \emph{timeout} exceeds
the wall-clock cap. An \emph{infeasible input} is an external layout that
already fills a core, reported rather than routed.

A per-iteration cost analysis of the loop is given in \appComplexity{}.
