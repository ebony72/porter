\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

This work is most closely related to online routers for multi-core
distributed quantum computers that minimise EPR-pair consumption. It also
relates to partition-first distributed compilation and to communication
amortisation techniques.

\textbf{Online EPR-aware routing.} \TeleSABRE{}~\cite{russo2025telesabre}
extends \SABRE{}-style routing to support \TeleData{} and \TeleGate{} under
an EPR-pair cost model. It scores executable local SWAP and inter-core
actions together using a front/extended-set layout objective. Consequently,
preparation for a remote operation is expressed through ordinary SWAPs and
competes directly with immediately executable remote actions.

\PORTER{} instead separates plan selection from plan preparation. It ranks a
complete inter-core plan before generating plan-specific preparation SWAPs,
commits to the selected plan, and then prepares it. It further adds
same-control \TeleGatePacket{} operations and capacity relief. These are
algorithmic differences, not a claim that the two systems are evaluated
under identical search budgets; the native-protocol comparison in
Section~\ref{sec:rq1} reports those budgets explicitly.

\dSABRE{}~\cite{lidsabre} is the closest methodological comparison. Like
\PORTER{}, it is locality-first: it enters the inter-core stage only when
no front CX can be handled within a core. Both treat free capacity as a
legality condition, retain a per-core reserve whose threshold is two free
positions in both defaults, and recover capacity by
searching from a congested core to a reservoir. They differ in the available
remote primitives, the decision unit, the role of preparation cost in
scoring, and the layout protocol. \dSABRE{} routes with \TeleData{}; \PORTER{}
adds \TeleGate{} and \TeleGatePacket{}. \dSABRE{} decides one core-hop at a time,
toward a possibly more distant target core, and executes that hop atomically:
the staging SWAPs it needs are performed inside the action, which is rolled
back to a no-op if any step fails. \PORTER{} decides a whole plan, including
the primitive it will use, then prepares that plan with local SWAPs until it
is ready and executes it, without reopening the remote choice. \PORTER{} uses
preparation cost as a tie-breaker after plan utility, while \dSABRE{}
incorporates it in its move score. \PORTER{} builds one deterministic
initial layout, where \dSABRE{} routes a best-of-$N$ candidate set.

The two systems also differ in guarantees. Under its stated architectural
assumptions, \dSABRE{} safe mode provides a completion guarantee.
\PORTER{} guarantees correctness of emitted routes and implementation
termination through guards, but does not claim completion for every feasible
input (Section~\ref{sec:sfc-progress}). Completion in the reported
selected-horizon experiments is empirical evidence, not a proof.

\textbf{Partition-first and communication-amortising approaches.}
Partition-first compilers assign logical qubits to cores by hypergraph or
community structure and route the remaining inter-core
interactions~\cite{escofet2023hqa,pytketdqc2024,daei2020optimized}.
\texttt{pytket-dqc}~\cite{pytketdqc2024} is used here as an external layout source, not as a directly comparable EPR-router baseline: its e-bit
model assumes different entanglement lifetime and communication-memory
conditions. AutoComm~\cite{wu2022autocomm} identifies remote gate sequences
that can share an entangled resource. \PORTER{} adapts the amortisation idea
inside its online routing loop through \TeleGatePacket{}, rather than using a
separate pre-pass. Finally, \SABRE{}~\cite{li2019tackling} supplies the
front-plus-lookahead routing framework from which the online routers draw
their scoring structure.
