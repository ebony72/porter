\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

\PORTER{} is a deterministic DQC compiler built on a plan-oriented remote
stage: it scores a complete inter-core action on the gate progress it
buys, before any preparation SWAP for it exists, and commits before
preparing. Around that it places a capacity discipline with two relief
tiers, a seed-free initial layout, and a per-circuit choice of
teleport-lookahead horizon.

Three conclusions are supported. First, on the evaluated MQT-Bench suites
\PORTER{} uses substantially fewer EPR pairs than published
\TeleSABRE{} --- $59.6\%$, $41.6\%$ and $68.2\%$ less in geometric mean at
25, 64 and 80 logical qubits, at three routes each --- and
completes every instance in all three suites, including the seven on
which \TeleSABRE{} does not converge. Second, against \dSABRE{}, \PORTER{} is competitive rather than
dominant, and which compiler leads depends on both the initial layout and
the routing budget. Under each compiler's own protocol \PORTER{} uses
$7\%$ more pooled EPR at $0.58\times$ the compile time. From an identical
externally supplied layout at three routes each, \dSABRE{} has the lower
pooled count; adding \PORTER{}'s five-route refinement schedule reverses
that, on those layouts and on both heavy-hex core graphs. On the
$100$--$360$-qubit scale-up series \PORTER{} leads outright. Third, the
ablations attribute these results rather than leaving them to the system
as a whole: capacity relief is what lets a deterministic-greedy design
complete in practice; the initial layout dominates the difference between
the two compilers at the smaller sizes evaluated, and the lookahead
horizon at the larger ones.

Two ablation results are negative and they delimit the contribution.
Plan commitment does not lower EPR consumption --- it makes the same
number of routing moves as re-scoring the front each iteration and lands
within $0.6\%$ --- so it is a compile-time mechanism, and no experiment
here isolates the plan abstraction it rests on. And a richer
gate-teleportation vocabulary does not always pay: at 80 qubits, removing
gate teleportation entirely beats the full action set by $32.8\%$ on the
packet-heaviest circuits, which we read as the plan scorer undervaluing
teleportation's latent return rather than as a verdict on amortisation.

The evaluation is bounded: two circuit families at the largest widths,
two non-grid core graphs, horizon rules fitted on the corpus they are
measured on, and a cost model charging one pair per inter-core action
that ignores link contention, latency and fidelity
(Section~\ref{sec:threats}). Five openings follow: a completion
guarantee to match \dSABRE{}'s, an out-of-sample validation of the
horizon rule, a horizon that scales with core count rather than being
chosen by trial, a comparator that weighs a resolved gate against a
latent distance reduction on one scale, and a hardware cost model under
which EPR count is not the whole objective.
