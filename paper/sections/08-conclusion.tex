\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

\PORTER{} is a deterministic DQC compiler built on a plan-oriented remote
stage: it scores a complete inter-core action on the gate progress it
buys, before any preparation SWAP for it exists, and commits before
preparing. Around that it places a capacity discipline with two relief
tiers, a seed-free initial layout, and a per-circuit choice of
teleport-lookahead horizon.

Three conclusions are supported. First, on the evaluated MQT-Bench suites
\PORTER{} uses substantially fewer EPR pairs than published
\TeleSABRE{} --- $59.6\%$, $41.6\%$ and $68.2\%$ less in geometric mean at
25, 64 and 80 logical qubits, at a matched attempt budget --- and
completes every instance in all three suites, including the seven on
which \TeleSABRE{} does not converge. Second, against the concurrent
\dSABRE{} revision measured here, \PORTER{} is competitive rather than
dominant, and which compiler leads depends on the operating point: it uses
$7\%$ more pooled EPR at $0.58\times$ the compile time under each
compiler's own protocol, and leads on identical externally supplied
layouts, on both heavy-hex core graphs, and on the $100$--$360$-qubit
scale-up series. Third, the ablations attribute those results rather than
leaving them to the system as a whole: capacity relief is what makes a
deterministic-greedy design complete in practice, the initial layout is
what separates the two compilers below roughly a hundred physical qubits,
and the lookahead horizon is what separates them above it.

Two ablation results are negative and they delimit the contribution.
Plan commitment does not lower EPR consumption --- it makes the same
number of routing moves as re-scoring the front each iteration and lands
within $0.6\%$ --- so it is a compile-time mechanism, and no experiment
here isolates the plan abstraction it rests on. And a richer
gate-teleportation vocabulary does not always pay: at 80 qubits, removing
gate teleportation entirely beats the full action set by $32.8\%$ on the
packet-heaviest circuits, which we read as the plan scorer undervaluing
teleportation's latent return rather than as a verdict on amortisation.

The evaluation is bounded in ways worth restating: two circuit families at
the largest widths, two non-grid core graphs, horizon-selection rules
fitted on the same corpus they are measured on, and a cost model that
charges one pair per inter-core action and ignores link contention,
latency and fidelity (Section~\ref{sec:threats}). The openings that
follow are correspondingly concrete: a completion guarantee to match
\dSABRE{}'s, an out-of-sample validation of the horizon rule, a lookahead
horizon that scales with the core count rather than being selected by
trial, a plan comparator that weighs a resolved gate against a latent
distance reduction on a common scale, and a richer hardware cost model
under which EPR count is no longer the whole objective.
