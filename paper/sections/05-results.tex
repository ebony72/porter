Table~\ref{tab:main} summarises the three suites; \appTables{} gives the
per-circuit EPR and SWAP counts. Baseline counts come from each
baseline's own harness; on the eight
64-qubit circuits \TeleSABRE{} completes, ours reproduces the counts
\dSABRE{} publishes for it~\cite{lidsabre} exactly.

Against \TeleSABRE{} the ordering is consistent across the suite. Block
\emph{(a)} of Table~\ref{tab:main} is the basis, since \TeleSABRE{}
converges on only seventeen of the twenty-four and a pooled figure would
otherwise compare different circuit sets. From one deterministic layout \PORTER{} lowers geometric-mean EPR by
$59.6\%$, $41.6\%$ and $68.2\%$ at 25, 64 and 80 qubits, spending three
routes against \TeleSABRE{}'s three seeded runs, and every circuit
\TeleSABRE{} completes improves, from $-16.0\%$ to $-81.0\%$. Refinement
adds to this at 25 and 64 qubits ($63.6\%$ and $45.1\%$) and little at 80,
where the selected route already accounts for most of it ($68.2\%$ against
$69.8\%$) --- the suite where the horizon, not the extra routes, does the
work. \PORTER{} and \dSABRE{} complete all twenty-four
instances; \TeleSABRE{} completes seventeen, failing on 64q~Random and
six of the nine 80-qubit circuits.

Against \dSABRE{} the answer varies by suite. The comparison uses block
\emph{(b)} of Table~\ref{tab:main} --- every circuit in each suite, since
both compilers complete all twenty-four and nothing needs excluding.
Routing its three candidate lookahead horizons and keeping the
lowest-EPR result (Section~\ref{sec:horizon}), against \dSABRE{}'s three
candidate layouts at three passes each, \PORTER{} takes the geometric
mean at 25 qubits ($43.1$ against $45.8$, $0.94\times$) and at 80
($396.7$ against $412.1$, $0.96\times$) and trails at 64 ($196.1$
against $173.1$, $1.13\times$), winning ten instances, losing twelve and
tying two; pooled over all twenty-four it is within $2\%$, $174.9$
against $171.9$. Refinement takes the lead pooled ($163.0$) at seven
routes to \dSABRE{}'s nine and narrows 64 qubits to $1.07\times$, and
the $\gamma$-search extends that to $159.1$ pooled and $1.02\times$ at
64 qubits (Section~\ref{sec:frontier}), so the one suite \PORTER{} loses
under its own protocol is also the one the extra search nearly closes.
\PORTER{}'s largest saving is 80q~Multiplier, $2{,}132$ against
$4{,}841$ with refinement; its worst loss is 64q~QFT ($262$ against
$179$).

The three sources in Table~\ref{tab:shared} are those that place all
twenty-four circuits. As \TeleSABRE{} converges only on
seventeen instances (Section~\ref{sec:rq1}) and emits an initial layout
only where it converges, we therefore don't include it in the comparison.

\textbf{The routing loops are equivalent within noise; the layouts are
not.} Read across any row of Table~\ref{tab:shared}: from the same
starting layout at three routes each, the two land within $2\%$ of one
another everywhere, \dSABRE{} ahead in all three rows and winning twelve
of the twenty-four instances to eight on the \texttt{pytket-dqc} row.
This is the controlled test and \PORTER{} loses it. Read down the
columns, though, and \texttt{pytket-dqc}'s hypergraph partition is the
best start for \emph{both} routers --- $4.5\%$ better than \dSABRE{}'s
own layout is for \dSABRE{}, $4.8\%$ better than \PORTER{}'s own is for
\PORTER{}. The spread between layout sources exceeds the spread between
routing loops, so the initial layout is the weaker component of both
compilers rather than an advantage to either.

\textbf{Refinement pays from every starting layout, once the horizon is
free to move.} \PORTER{}+R gains $9.5\%$ over \PORTER{} on \dSABRE{}'s
layout ($175.2 \to 158.5$) and $6.8\%$ on both the others
($174.9 \to 163.0$, $166.4 \to 155.2$), overtaking \dSABRE{}'s own
protocol from all three at seven routes to its nine. An earlier version
of this schedule, which held the lookahead horizon at its configured
value, gained on \dSABRE{}'s layout and nothing at all on
\texttt{pytket-dqc}'s --- which invited the reading that refinement
merely repairs a poor starting layout. That reading was an artefact of
the fixed horizon: refining from the selected route instead
(Section~\ref{sec:sfc-refine}) recovers as much from the best layout
source as from the worst.

Table~\ref{tab:ablation} disables each mechanism in isolation, holding
the initial layout and every other parameter fixed, over every circuit
of all three suites; \appMix{} tabulates the per-action counts behind
them. Reading the three lines of each column down the table gives the
scale trend, which for two of the mechanisms is the result. Each configuration routes one forward pass at the configured
horizon, so the Full column is not the selected route of
Table~\ref{tab:main} but the single pass every column shares.

\textbf{Packets.} Disabling \TeleGatePacket{} makes every
shared-control remote CX run pay per gate, and the cost tracks the
packet count: $+42\%$ EPR on 64q~QFT (262 $\to$ 372, 45 packets),
$+47\%$ on 80q~QAOA (1122 $\to$ 1650, 303 packets), $+40\%$ on 80q~AE
(490 $\to$ 688, 264 packets), against a $+7.6\%$ gmean over all
twenty-four. Three circuits move the other way --- 64q~Multiplier
($1358 \to 1171$), 80q~QFT ($446 \to 435$) and 80q~Multiplier
($3501 \to 3437$) --- and all three are telegate-heavy and
packet-light, so packet formation there is a heuristic commitment that
displaces a slightly better teleport plan. Read alone this column is a
win for the primitive; the next overturns that reading.

\textbf{\TeleData{} only.} This column is two configurations.
\emph{Strict removal} --- deleting the \TeleGate{} family and changing
nothing else --- is the one-factor ablation, and it fails: it completes
on eight of the twenty-four instances, and on only two of the eighteen
rows shown. The \TeleGate{} is the only remote action
that needs no free \emph{data} slot in the destination core. Like every
remote action it needs the two inter-core communication qubits free, but
the cat copy it creates lives on the target-side communication qubit
rather than displacing a logical qubit, so unlike \TeleData{} it is not
gated on $\mathrm{free}(c) \ge f_\mathrm{min}$ and is always available
for an adjacent-remote CX. Without it, a front of core-adjacent remote
CXs whose destinations all sit at or below $f_\mathrm{min}$ has an empty
plan set, and the tail-relief BFS is offered only non-adjacent
candidates. The
\TeleGate{} is thus essential to
\emph{completion} here under the unchanged relief policy --- not to
termination, which the guards of Section~\ref{sec:sfc-progress} provide
either way.

\emph{The vocabulary variant}, marked $^{\ddagger}$, additionally feeds
adjacent-blocked candidates to that BFS, which the full configuration
never needs; it recovers all but the two Random rows, the most
telegate-heavy in the suite. Adding the same tier to the full
configuration leaves every row EPR-identical, so what follows is a
difference of vocabulary and not of relief --- but not a strict
ablation. On that basis a teleport-only vocabulary is the cheaper one,
and monotonically so with scale --- $+1.3\%$ at 25 qubits, $-1.6\%$ at
64 and $-21.4\%$ at 80, for $7.9\%$ below Full over the twenty-one
instances it completes ---
and the circuits it wins are the packet-heaviest, giving
$\text{\TeleData{} only} < \text{Full} < \text{No packets}$: packets
beat the single telegates they degrade into, but pure teleportation
beats both. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} argues this is a
currency mismatch in the plan comparator rather than a verdict on
packets. It also answers a question \dSABRE{} leaves open: its
teledata-only vocabulary was chosen by measurement, with grouping
untested. Grouping does close the per-EPR gap, and still does not
justify spending pairs that teleporting would have spent better.

\textbf{Capacity relief.} Disabling both tiers costs completion, not
just EPR pairs: five 80-qubit circuits --- AE, QFT, QNN, QPEexact and
Random --- all fail, because with every destination below
$f_\mathrm{min}$ and no way to drain qubits toward reservoirs the loop
has no legal remote plan left. Where routing does complete, dense
circuits pay heavily ($+89\%$ on 25q~QFT, $+85\%$ on 64q~AE, $+79\%$ on
64q~QPEexact): $+26.4\%$ at 25 qubits and $+24.0\%$ at 64, but only
$+4.0\%$ at 80. The mechanism changes character with scale. Below 80
qubits it saves EPR pairs and nothing fails without it; at 80 it saves
few pairs and is instead what makes five of the nine circuits routable
at all. Three instances are cheaper \emph{without} it --- 64q~QNN
($864 \to 600$), 80q~Multiplier ($3501 \to 3055$) and marginally
25q~Random --- so the opportunistic tier can overpay where congestion
would have cleared itself. With both tiers on no instance in this
evaluation reached the deadlock path of
Section~\ref{sec:sfc-progress}; with both off five did. That is a
statement about these instances, not a guarantee. The initial
layout,
which this table holds fixed, is assessed in
Sections~\ref{sec:shared_results} and~\ref{sec:heavyhex}.

Every result so far is at 108 physical qubits or fewer.
Table~\ref{tab:scaleup} takes the two circuit families that exist at
100, 200 and 360 logical qubits onto \dSABRE{}'s scalability series:
$5{\times}5$ cores throughout, core count $6 \to 12 \to 20$, so
occupancy stays roughly constant while the core graph grows and
inter-core traffic with it --- \dSABRE{}'s own cost rises $200 \to 765
\to 1{,}890$ on QFT.

\PORTER{} uses fewer EPR pairs than \dSABRE{} on all six instances, by a
widening margin: $0.90\times$ at 100 logical qubits, $0.67\times$ at
200, $0.62\times$ at 360, for $0.72\times$ overall. It does so at
$0.54\times$ \dSABRE{}'s complete compile cost ($8.4$\,s against
$15.4$\,s), routing three times where \dSABRE{} routes nine.

\textbf{The horizon, not the layout, accounts for the reversal.} The
intervention that closes \PORTER{}'s gap at the smaller sizes does not
work here: handing \PORTER{} \dSABRE{}'s own \texttt{SabreLayout}
candidates --- the control that reaches parity at 25 and 64 qubits and
wins on both heavy-hex core graphs --- makes it \emph{worse}
($0.96\times$ against $0.72\times$ in our released results), worst on
the largest instance, where \dSABRE{}'s layout costs $1{,}791$ pairs
against the $1{,}156$ \PORTER{}'s own layout reaches. The horizon does:
every instance in this series selects $L_\mathrm{tp}{=}128$, and at the
configured $16$ \PORTER{} trails \dSABRE{} by $1.45\times$
(Section~\ref{sec:horizon}). Among the mechanisms we varied, that is the
one that accounts for the change.

This separates two deficits the smaller suites conflate. At $36$ to
$108$ physical qubits the initial layout limits \PORTER{}: control it,
match the route count, and the difference largely disappears. At $150$
and above it does not, and the horizon does. Selecting it per circuit is
a workaround, not a fix; a $K$-aware horizon remains open.

To test how much of \PORTER{}'s behaviour depends on square cores wired
in a grid we adopt \dSABRE{}'s non-grid experiment~\cite{lidsabre}: the
same nine-circuit 64-qubit suite on four IBM 27-qubit heavy-hex tiles.
Two things change at once --- the \emph{core} becomes irregular (degrees
over $\{1,2,3\}$, six degree-1 leaves, diameter 12 against a
$4{\times}4$ grid's 6) and the \emph{core graph} is either a ring, every
core of degree 2, or a star with core~0 a hub. \PORTER{}'s hardware
object is derived from the architecture \dSABRE{}'s own builder
produces, and a topology assertion checks that both compilers see
identical qubit ids, edges and port endpoints before anything is routed.
Neither compiler is specialised for a heavy-hex tile.

\textbf{The core graph matters more than the core shape.} On the ring
(Table~\ref{tab:heavyhex}) the two compilers are within $1\%$ of one
another, $86.1$ against $85.2$: doubling the intra-core diameter and
replacing degree-4 interiors with degree-1 leaves --- a tile neither
compiler was designed for --- disturbs neither. The star does. There
\PORTER{} trails by $21\%$, $122.3$ against $101.1$, winning two of nine.
The core shape is the same in both rows; only the core graph differs.

\textbf{Star Multiplier is a livelock the horizon escapes.} At a fixed
$L_\mathrm{tp}{=}16$ this instance does not terminate. It fails as a
local-SWAP livelock rather than the capacity deadlock of
Section~\ref{sec:sfc-progress} --- retired gates freeze at $3{,}226$ of
$13{,}040$ and the EPR count at $538$ while intra-core SWAPs accumulate
one per iteration, so the iteration guard stops it --- and the failure
is configuration-dependent: the same circuit, architecture, layout and
router complete at $L_\mathrm{tp}{=}128$, at $455$ pairs against
\dSABRE{}'s $722$, a $37\%$ reduction and \PORTER{}'s widest margin
anywhere on these two architectures. Since horizon selection routes that
candidate as a matter of course, \PORTER{} has no unrouted instance
anywhere in this evaluation.

\textbf{The initial layout is the dominant observed source of the star
gap.} Routed instead from \dSABRE{}'s own \texttt{SabreLayout}
candidates (\appTables{}), \PORTER{} overtakes \dSABRE{} on \emph{both}
core graphs --- $82.9$ against $85.2$ on the ring, $98.5$ against
$101.1$ on the star --- and the reversal is larger on the topology it
loses under its own layout. The cause
is in the layout: \PORTER{} reserves $f_\mathrm{min}$ in every core,
treating cores as interchangeable, which they are on a grid and a ring
and are not on a star, where Multiplier opens with free counts
$(2,2,13,27)$ --- the hub every route must cross
pinned at the floor while a whole core sits empty. Raising the
reservation uniformly does not repair it (we tried
$f_\mathrm{min}{\in}\{4,6\}$); a reservation scaled by a core's position
in the core graph is untried.

Taken with Section~\ref{sec:scaleup}, this bounds the layout reading
rather than extending it: up to $108$ physical qubits, across two core
shapes and three core graphs, the initial layout separates the two
compilers and controlling it erases the difference; above $150$ the
horizon takes over.

Table~\ref{tab:protocols} places \PORTER{}'s operating points
(Section~\ref{sec:opprotocols}) against \dSABRE{}, pooled over all
twenty-four instances. Each is the previous one plus a search: \PORTER{}
selects the horizon, \PORTER{}+R refines backward from the route it
selects, and \PORTER{}+S searches the layout discount as well. Because
every schedule contains the one before it and keeps the best result by
EPR, the column is monotone by construction, and none of them can report
more EPR than routing once at the configured horizon.

The trade is time against EPR pairs. \PORTER{} costs $1.02\times$
\dSABRE{}'s EPR at $0.91\times$ its compile time; refinement takes the
lead at $0.95\times$ the EPR for $2.34\times$ the time, on seven routes
against \dSABRE{}'s nine; the layout search reaches $0.93\times$ at
$6.55\times$. Increasing \PORTER{}'s deterministic search budget
therefore closes and reverses the own-protocol gap. We stop short of
calling that gap a search-budget gap: the added schedules change the
initial layout as well as the route, and the comparison equalises route
counts rather than wall-clock time --- which is what \PORTER{} actually
spends, since a deep selected horizon makes each pass dearer rather than
adding passes.

\PORTER{}+SR, which took the better of horizon selection and the
$\gamma$-search, is no longer a distinct operating point. The search
refines from the $\gamma{=}0.99$ layout among others, and that is the
layout \PORTER{} builds by default, so with refinement running on top of
selection (Section~\ref{sec:sfc-refine}) the search contains the
selected route and cannot lose to it. It does not differ from
\PORTER{}+S on any of the twenty-four instances, at $19\%$ more compile
time, so we drop it. The same argument makes each of the three listed
schedules safe against the one below it, which is why none of them
needed to be reported with a caveat about losing to a cheaper
configuration: at a fixed horizon they did, and refining from the
selected route is what removed that.

All times are \emph{complete} compile costs --- layout generation plus
every route over every candidate, not the winning route alone --- and
\PORTER{}'s advantage there is in the count of routes, not their price.
It is the dearer of the two per route on all nine 64-qubit circuits,
taking $3.6\times$ as long in geometric mean: enumerating whole plans,
collecting packets by forward simulation and running a two-tier capacity
search all cost more per iteration than scoring a flat list of hops, and
a deep horizon costs more still. On that subset \dSABRE{} is cheaper end
to end ($1.77$\,s against $1.98$\,s); pooled over all twenty-four
\PORTER{} is slightly ahead at $0.91\times$, and at $1.02\times$
\dSABRE{}'s EPR the cheap operating point remains \PORTER{}'s.
