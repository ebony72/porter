\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

This section answers RQ1--RQ4 (Section~\ref{sec:rqs}). Protocol names,
route counts, and complete compile-time accounting are defined in
Table~\ref{tab:budgets}. Lower EPR consumption is better throughout.

\subsection{RQ1: Native-Protocol Comparison}
\label{sec:rq1}
\label{sec:main_results}

layout and routing protocol? \emph{Control:} none; this is an end-to-end
comparison. \emph{Outcomes:} EPR geometric mean over the stated completed
instances, completion count, and complete protocol time.

\textbf{Comparison with \TeleSABRE{}.} On the common completed subsets in
block~(a), \PORTER{} reduces geometric-mean EPR consumption by $59.7\%$,
$44.9\%$, and $68.8\%$ at 25, 64, and 80 qubits, \chg{respectively, and every
circuit \TeleSABRE{} completes improves, from $-23.2\%$ to $-76.9\%$.
\PORTER{}+R}
changes these reductions to $63.5\%$, $46.9\%$, and $70.3\%$. \TeleSABRE{}
completes 17 of 24 instances; it does not complete 64q Random or six
80-qubit instances. \PORTER{} and \dSABRE{} complete all 24. This comparison
uses different native search budgets (four \PORTER{} routes and three
\TeleSABRE{} seeded runs), so it establishes end-to-end performance under
these protocols rather than an equal-effort routing comparison.

\textbf{Comparison with \dSABRE{}.} Since both complete all instances,
block~(b) gives the main comparison. With four routes, \PORTER{} uses
$0.94\times$ \dSABRE{}'s EPR at 25 qubits and $0.93\times$ at 80 qubits,
but uses $1.07\times$ at 64 qubits. Pooled across all 24 instances, the
protocols are nearly equal: $169.4$ versus $171.9$ EPR pairs
($0.99\times$). \PORTER{} wins 11 instances, loses 10, and ties 3.
Refinement improves the pooled result to $156.7$ ($0.91\times$ \dSABRE{}),
but raises complete compile time from $1.68$ to $3.00$ seconds per instance.
The largest \PORTER{}+R saving is on 80q Multiplier ($2{,}130$ versus
$4{,}841$); its largest loss is on 80q Random ($10{,}848$ versus $10{,}551$).

The oracle columns quantify complementarity only: taking the lower EPR
count after running both compilers produces $155.7$ for \PORTER{} and
$150.5$ for \PORTER{}+R. \chg{Realising either costs both compile budgets, and
because no tested rule predicts the per-instance}
winner, these values should not be interpreted as the performance of an
operational portfolio.

\subsection{RQ2: Routing With Layout Controlled}
\label{sec:shared_results}

\emph{Question:} Given the same initial layout, do the routing loops differ
materially in EPR cost? \emph{Control:} layout source. \emph{Outcome:}
geometric-mean EPR over all 24 instances. The schedules are not
budget-matched: \dSABRE{} uses three passes per layout, \PORTER{} four
horizon routes, and \PORTER{}+R eight routes.

\TeleSABRE{} is not included because it supplies a successful layout for
only 17 instances. For a fixed layout, \dSABRE{} and \PORTER{} are close:
\chg{across any row of Table~\ref{tab:shared} their difference is at most
$1.9\%$ on block~(a) and at most $6.1\%$ on block~(b). By contrast, changing the layout produces}
a $9.2$--$14.5\%$ spread across protocols on the 18 non-trivial instances.
Within this experiment, layout choice therefore accounts for more variation
than the choice between the two routing loops. This is a descriptive result,
not a matched-budget ranking.

The \texttt{pytket-dqc} partition is best in every column over all 24
instances but worst in every column after GHZ and Graphstate are excluded.
Its advantage is concentrated in those simple families; it is the worse
start on 14 of 24 instances for \dSABRE{} and 16 of 24 for \chg{\PORTER{},
plausibly because it optimises e-bits on a network more permissive than the
device~\cite{lidsabre}.}
Moreover, three KaHyPar seeds cost $0.6$--$627$ seconds, compared with
$0.3$--$9.0$ ms for \PORTER{}'s layout construction, and the partitioner
does not finish on the scale-up inputs. We therefore retain \PORTER{}'s
layout as the default and treat this partition as an opt-in alternative
(\appOptin{}).

Backward refinement improves every layout row: it reduces EPR by $8.2\%$
from \dSABRE{}'s layout, $8.0\%$ from the partition, and $7.5\%$ from
\PORTER{}'s layout. These gains cost four additional routes and should be
read with the timing results in Section~\ref{sec:frontier}.

\textbf{Packets.} Removing packets changes the EPR geometric mean by only
$0.9\%$ on the eight rows both variants \chg{complete
(Table~\ref{tab:ablation}). The direction is not}
uniform: packets reduce cost on AE, QFT, QNN, and QPEexact, but the
no-packet variant is cheaper on QAOA and Multiplier. This result is
consistent with packet collection being useful when a shared-control run is
long enough to amortise one pair, but occasionally displacing a preferable
teleport decision. The smaller-width ablations show larger packet savings
($3.0\%$ at 25 qubits and $7.4\%$ at 64 qubits); the detailed action counts
are in \appMix{}.

\textbf{Gate teleportation.} \chg{This column changes two settings, and
reporting them as one teleport-only configuration is the natural choice.}
Unlike \TeleData{}, \TeleGate{} does not
require a free data position at its target core: the cat-state copy occupies
the target-side communication port. \chg{Removing it also closes a recovery
path --- while a telegate or packet plan exists for an adjacent remote CX,
the last-resort relief tier never sees an adjacent blocked plan --- so
strict removal deadlocks outright. Re-admitting those seeds restores it and
is inert whenever a \TeleGate{} is available, leaving the merged column a
comparison of vocabularies.}

\textbf{Capacity relief.} With both relief tiers disabled, five of the nine
80-qubit circuits do not complete. On the four shared completed rows, the
full configuration is $0.5\%$ more expensive than no relief
($174.1$ versus $173.3$), so this ablation does not establish an EPR saving
on that restricted subset. It establishes that relief is important for
completion under the tested capacity \chg{policy, though pooled over the
nineteen instances that complete both ways it does save $24.8\%$. No claim
beyond these instances is warranted.}

\PORTER{} uses fewer EPR pairs on all six \chg{instances of
Table~\ref{tab:scaleup}: $0.90\times$}

On the ring, the native protocols are within \chg{$1.1\%$ (86.1 versus}

does not prove that layout alone explains the gap, because the two routers
still use different routing schedules. The scale-up series, where this
layout control does not produce the same reversal, further limits the scope
of this conclusion.

The schedules \chg{of Table~\ref{tab:protocols}
(Section~\ref{sec:opprotocols}) form an EPR/time frontier. Because each
contains the one before it and keeps the best result by EPR, the EPR column
is monotone by construction, though compile time is not. \PORTER{} improves
the pooled EPR}
geometric mean slightly relative to \dSABRE{} ($0.99\times$) while taking
$1.20\times$ the time. \PORTER{}+R improves EPR to $0.91\times$ but takes
$2.15\times$ the time. These comparisons use complete protocol costs:
layout construction and every candidate route are included. Fewer routes do
not imply faster compilation, because a \PORTER{} route performs plan
enumeration, packet simulation, and capacity-relief search. The timing
results are single-run, machine-specific measurements and should therefore
be interpreted as indicative ratios rather than precise performance
estimates.
