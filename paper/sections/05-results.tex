\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

This section answers RQ1--RQ4 (Section~\ref{sec:rqs}).
Table~\ref{tab:budgets} defines the protocol names, routing-invocation
counts, and complete compile-time accounting. Lower EPR consumption is
better throughout.

\subsection{RQ1: Native-Protocol Comparison}
\label{sec:rq1}
\label{sec:main_results}

layout and routing protocol? \emph{Control:} none; this is an end-to-end
comparison. \emph{Outcomes:} geometric-mean EPR consumption over the stated

\textbf{Comparison with \TeleSABRE{}.} On the common completed subsets in
block~(a), \PORTER{} reduces geometric-mean EPR consumption relative to
\TeleSABRE{} by $59.7\%$, $44.9\%$, and $68.8\%$ at 25, 64, and 80 qubits,
respectively. Every circuit completed by \TeleSABRE{} improves, with
per-instance reductions from $23.2\%$ to $76.9\%$. \PORTER{}+R changes the
suite-level reductions to $63.5\%$, $46.9\%$, and $70.3\%$.
\TeleSABRE{} completes 17 of 24 main-suite instances; it does not complete
64q Random or six 80-qubit instances. \PORTER{} and \dSABRE{} complete all
24.

\textbf{Comparison with \dSABRE{}: native-protocol outcome.} Both methods
complete all 24 main-suite instances, so block~(b) gives the primary
comparison. \PORTER{} uses $0.94\times$ \dSABRE{}'s geometric-mean EPR
count at 25 qubits and $0.93\times$ at 80 qubits, but $1.07\times$ at
64 qubits. Over all 24 instances, \PORTER{} uses $169.4$ EPR pairs in
geometric mean versus \dSABRE{}'s $171.9$, or $0.99\times$ the \dSABRE{}
count. \PORTER{}+R reduces the pooled result to $156.7$
($0.91\times$ \dSABRE{}), while increasing complete compile time from $1.68$
to $3.00$ seconds per instance.

\textbf{Per-instance variation.} \PORTER{} obtains the lower EPR count on
11 instances, \dSABRE{} on 10, and the methods tie on 3. \PORTER{}+R has
14 wins, 8 losses, and 2 ties against \dSABRE{}. The largest
\PORTER{}+R saving is on 80q Multiplier ($2{,}130$ versus $4{,}841$); its largest
loss is on 80q Random ($10{,}848$ versus $10{,}551$).

\textbf{Oracle diagnostic.} The oracle columns diagnose the diversity of
outcomes, rather than define an additional compiler configuration. After
both compilers have run, selecting the lower EPR count for each instance
produces geometric means of $155.7$ with \PORTER{} and $150.5$ with
\PORTER{}+R.

\subsection{RQ2: Routing With Layout Controlled}
\label{sec:shared_results}

\emph{Question:} Given the same initial layout, do the routing loops differ
materially in EPR cost? \emph{Control:} layout source. \emph{Outcome:}
geometric-mean EPR over all 24 instances. The schedules are not
budget-matched: \dSABRE{} uses three passes per layout, \PORTER{} four
horizon routes, and \PORTER{}+R eight routes.

\TeleSABRE{} is not included because it supplies a successful layout for
only 17 instances. For a fixed layout, \dSABRE{} and \PORTER{} are close:
across the rows of Table~\ref{tab:shared}, their difference is at most
$1.9\%$ in block~(a) and at most $6.1\%$ in block~(b). By contrast,
changing the layout source produces a $9.2$--$14.5\%$ spread across
protocols on the 18 non-trivial instances. The layout source therefore
produces a larger EPR spread than the choice between the two routing loops.

The \texttt{pytket-dqc} partition is best in every column over all 24
instances, but worst in every column after GHZ and Graphstate are excluded.
Its advantage is therefore concentrated in those simple families. It is the
worse start on 14 of 24 instances for \dSABRE{} and 16 of 24 for \PORTER{}.
The partition optimises e-bits on a network whose communication assumptions
are more permissive than those used here~\cite{lidsabre}.
Moreover, three KaHyPar seeds cost $0.6$--$627$ seconds, compared with
$0.3$--$9.0$ ms for \PORTER{}'s layout construction, and the partitioner
does not finish on the scale-up inputs. We therefore retain \PORTER{}'s
layout as the default and treat this partition as an opt-in alternative.

Backward refinement improves every layout row: it reduces EPR by $8.2\%$
from \dSABRE{}'s layout, $8.0\%$ from the partition, and $7.5\%$ from
\PORTER{}'s layout.

\textbf{Completion.} Only the full configuration routes all nine 80-qubit
circuits. Disabling both relief tiers leaves four, restricting the vocabulary
to \TeleData{} leaves five, and disabling packets leaves eight
(Table~\ref{tab:ablation}). Completion, not EPR count, is where three of the
four mechanisms are decisive.

\textbf{Packets.} On the eight circuits completed with and without packets,
the geometric-mean EPR count is unchanged within $0.9\%$, and the direction
varies by circuit: packets reduce cost on AE, QFT, QNN, and QPEexact and
raise it on QAOA and Multiplier. Packets matter more at smaller widths,
saving $3.0\%$ at 25 qubits and $7.4\%$ at 64 qubits.

\textbf{Gate teleportation.} Deleting the \TeleGate{} family alone
deadlocks seven of the nine circuits. A \TeleGate{} needs no free data
position in the target core, because the cat-state copy occupies the
target-side communication port; and while a \TeleGate{} plan exists for an
adjacent remote CX, the last-resort relief tier never receives an adjacent
blocked plan. The \TeleData{} column therefore also re-admits those blocked
plans to the relief tier, a change that is inert whenever a \TeleGate{} is
available. It measures a teleport-only vocabulary rather than the removal of
one mechanism. Five circuits complete, using $121.4$ EPR pairs in geometric
mean against $124.8$ for the full configuration.

\textbf{Capacity relief.} Disabling both relief tiers prevents completion on
five of the nine 80-qubit circuits. Relief earns its place on completion
rather than cost: on the four circuits completed either way the full
configuration uses $174.1$ EPR pairs against $173.3$, while across the 19
instances that complete both ways it saves $24.8\%$.

$L_\mathrm{tp}=128$.

Table~\ref{tab:protocols} summarises the EPR/time trade-off among the
