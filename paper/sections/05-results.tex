Table~\ref{tab:main} summarises the three suites; \appTables{} gives the
per-circuit EPR and SWAP counts. Baseline counts come from each baseline's
own harness, and on the eight 64-qubit circuits \TeleSABRE{} completes ours
reproduces the counts \dSABRE{} publishes for it~\cite{lidsabre} exactly.

\textbf{Against \TeleSABRE{}} the ordering is consistent across the suite.
On block \emph{(a)}, from one deterministic layout, \PORTER{} lowers
geometric-mean EPR by $59.7\%$, $41.6\%$ and $68.2\%$ at 25, 64 and 80
qubits, spending four routes against \TeleSABRE{}'s three seeded runs, and
every circuit \TeleSABRE{} completes improves, from $-16.0\%$ to $-81.0\%$.
Refinement adds to that at 25 and 64 qubits ($63.5\%$ and $45.1\%$) but
little at 80 ($69.8\%$), where the horizon rather than the extra routes does
the work. \TeleSABRE{} completes seventeen instances, failing on 64q~Random
and six of the nine 80-qubit circuits; \PORTER{} and \dSABRE{} complete all
twenty-four.

\textbf{Against \dSABRE{}} the answer varies by suite, and block \emph{(b)}
is the basis since both complete everything. At four routes to \dSABRE{}'s
nine, \PORTER{} takes the geometric mean at 25 qubits ($0.94\times$) and at
80 ($0.96\times$) and trails at 64 ($196.1$ against $173.1$, $1.13\times$),
winning ten instances, losing twelve and tying two; pooled it is within
$2\%$, \chg{$175.0$ against $171.9$. Refinement takes the lead pooled ($163.1$)}
at eight routes and narrows 64 qubits to $1.07\times$, so the one suite
\PORTER{} loses under its own protocol is also the one extra routes largely
close. \PORTER{}+R's largest saving is 80q~Multiplier, $2{,}313$ against
$4{,}841$; its worst loss 64q~QFT, $262$ against $179$.

The three sources are those that place all twenty-four circuits.
\TeleSABRE{} is not among them: it converges on seventeen and emits a
layout only where it converges (Section~\ref{sec:rq1}).

\textbf{Changing the routing loop moves less than changing the
layout.} Across any row of Table~\ref{tab:shared}, from the same layout,
the two land within \chg{$3.5\%$ of one another ---}
\dSABRE{} ahead in all three rows of block \emph{(a)} and in two of three
of \emph{(b)}, \PORTER{} taking its own layout there. The layout is
controlled but the budget is not: \PORTER{} spends four routes on its
candidate horizons to \dSABRE{}'s three passes, so the comparison is if
anything generous to \PORTER{}, and \PORTER{} still does not win it. The spread down a column
is two to four times the spread across a row in both blocks, so the
initial layout is the larger source of variation in both compilers, and
the component in which each has more to gain. That much is stable.

\textbf{Which layout is best is not, and \PORTER{} does not adopt the
partition.} Over all twenty-four instances \texttt{pytket-dqc}'s
hypergraph partition is the lowest cell in every column; drop GHZ and
Graphstate and it is the highest in every column, raising the mean by
$10.7\%$ for \dSABRE{} and $9.7\%$ for \PORTER{}. Those six rows carry
the whole result, and the partition is the worse start on fourteen of the
twenty-four instances for \dSABRE{} and fifteen for \PORTER{}, at a median
ratio of $1.03$ for both. Its
gain is real but confined to the two structurally simplest families,
whose interaction graphs a partitioner cuts almost perfectly and whose
absolute cost is least. It is also expensive: best-of-three KaHyPar seeds
costs $0.6$--$627$\,s against $0.3$--$9.0$\,ms for \PORTER{}'s
construction, on most circuits more than \PORTER{}'s whole four-route
protocol, and it does not finish at the widths of
Section~\ref{sec:scaleup}. Cost and confinement are why \PORTER{} keeps
its own layout and treats the partition as an opt-in candidate
(\appOptin{}). Neither effect is specific to \PORTER{} --- the same
partitions are the worst start for \dSABRE{}'s loop on the same eighteen
instances --- plausibly because \texttt{pytket-dqc} optimises e-bits on a
network more permissive than the device~\cite{lidsabre}.

\textbf{Refinement pays from every starting layout, once the horizon is
free to move.} \PORTER{}+R gains $11.4\%$ over \PORTER{} on \dSABRE{}'s
layout ($175.8 \to 155.7$), $8.0\%$ on \texttt{pytket-dqc}'s
($167.2 \to 153.8$) and $6.8\%$ on its own ($175.0 \to 163.1$), overtaking
\dSABRE{}'s own protocol from all three at eight routes to its nine.

Table~\ref{tab:ablation} disables each mechanism on the 80-qubit suite,
holding layout, horizon and every other parameter fixed; \appMix{} gives the
per-action counts. Every column is one forward pass at the configured
horizon, so Full is that pass, not Table~\ref{tab:main}'s selected route.
What each column asks is whether its mechanism earns its place.

\textbf{Packets.} Disabling \TeleGatePacket{} makes every shared-control
remote CX pay per gate, costing $12.0\%$ EPR and tracking the packet count:
$+47\%$ on QAOA (303 packets), $+40\%$ on AE. QFT and Multiplier move the
other way, both telegate-heavy and packet-light, where packet formation
displaces a better teleport plan. Read alone the column favours the
primitive; the next qualifies it.

\textbf{\TeleData{} only.} This column is two configurations. \emph{Strict
removal} of the \TeleGate{} family is the one-factor ablation, and it fails
on all but two rows: the \TeleGate{} is the only remote action needing no
free \emph{data} slot, since its cat copy occupies the target-side
communication qubit instead of displacing a logical qubit, so it alone is
not gated on $\mathrm{free}(c)\ge f_\mathrm{min}$. Without it a front of
adjacent remote CXs whose destinations all sit at $f_\mathrm{min}$ has an
empty plan set. The \TeleGate{} earns its place on \emph{completion}, then,
not on termination, which the guards of Section~\ref{sec:sfc-progress} give
either way.

\emph{The vocabulary variant} ($^{\ddagger}$) also feeds adjacent-blocked
candidates to the relief BFS, recovering every row but Random. That tier
leaves the full configuration EPR-identical, so what it isolates is
vocabulary and not relief --- though it is no longer a strict ablation. On
that basis teleport-only is $19.8\%$ cheaper, and cheaper on every circuit
it completes but QAOA, where it is dearer by $0.6\%$ ($1{,}129$ against
$1{,}122$); on three of the five rows where all three complete and any
mechanism is exercised --- GHZ and Graphstate tie in every column ---
$\text{\TeleData{} only} < \text{Full} < \text{No packets}$, QFT and QAOA
excepted --- QFT as in the packets column, QAOA as the one row teleport-only
loses. Packets beat the single telegates they degrade into,
but pure teleportation beats both. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} reads this
as a currency mismatch in the plan comparator, not a verdict on packets. It
also answers \dSABRE{}'s open question: it chose teledata-only by
measurement, leaving grouping untested, and grouping closes the per-EPR gap
without justifying pairs that teleporting would have spent better.

\textbf{Capacity relief.} Disabling both tiers costs completion, not EPR
pairs. Five of the nine circuits fail outright: with every destination below
$f_\mathrm{min}$ and no way to drain qubits toward reservoirs, no legal
remote plan remains. Where routing completes it saves only $4.0\%$, and
Multiplier is cheaper without it, so the opportunistic tier can overpay
where congestion would have cleared itself: relief earns its place on
completion, not on cost. With both tiers on no instance here reached the
deadlock path of Section~\ref{sec:sfc-progress}; with both off, five did ---
a statement about these instances, not a guarantee.

Every result so far is at 108 physical qubits or fewer.
Table~\ref{tab:scaleup} takes the two circuit families that exist at
100, 200 and 360 logical qubits onto \dSABRE{}'s scalability series:
$5{\times}5$ cores throughout, core count $6 \to 12 \to 20$, so
occupancy stays roughly constant while the core graph grows and
inter-core traffic with it --- \dSABRE{}'s own cost rises $200 \to 765
\to 1{,}890$ on QFT.

\PORTER{} uses fewer EPR pairs than \dSABRE{} on all six instances, by a
widening margin: $0.90\times$ at 100 logical qubits, $0.67\times$ at
200, $0.63\times$ at 360, for $0.72\times$ overall. It does so at
$0.50\times$ \dSABRE{}'s complete compile cost ($7.8$\,s against
$15.4$\,s), routing four times where \dSABRE{} routes nine.

The horizon is what makes that difference, and the comparison is
available directly. Routing the same six instances from the same layouts
at the configured horizon alone --- one route, no selection --- gives a
geometric mean of $1{,}026.5$ pairs against \dSABRE{}'s $705.7$, which
is $1.45\times$ and loses all six. Selection changes nothing else about
the compiler and reaches \chg{$508.7$, which is $0.72\times$ and wins all}
six. Every instance selects $L_\mathrm{tp}{=}128$, the longest candidate
offered, and none selects the configured $16$: at these widths a plan's
value is realised further into the circuit than the default horizon can
see.

We adopt \dSABRE{}'s non-grid experiment~\cite{lidsabre}: the same
nine-circuit 64-qubit suite on four IBM 27-qubit heavy-hex tiles. Two
things change at once --- the \emph{core} becomes irregular (degrees over
$\{1,2,3\}$, six degree-1 leaves, diameter 12 against a $4{\times}4$
grid's 6) and the \emph{core graph} is either a ring, every core of
degree 2, or a star with core~0 a hub. Neither compiler is specialised
for a heavy-hex tile, and both are given the identical device.

\textbf{The core graph matters more than the core shape.} On the ring
(Table~\ref{tab:heavyhex}) the two compilers are within $1\%$ of one
another, $86.1$ against $85.2$: doubling the intra-core diameter and
replacing degree-4 interiors with degree-1 leaves disturbs neither. The
star does. There \PORTER{} trails by $20\%$, $121.2$ against $101.1$,
winning two of nine. The core shape is the same in both rows; only the
core graph differs.

\textbf{The initial layout is the dominant observed source of the star
gap.} The last column of Table~\ref{tab:heavyhex} holds the routing loop
against \dSABRE{}'s own \texttt{SabreLayout} candidates: routed from
those, \PORTER{} overtakes \dSABRE{} on \emph{both} core graphs ---
$82.9$ against $85.2$ on the ring, $97.1$ against $101.1$ on the star ---
and the reversal is larger on the topology it loses under its own layout.
The cause is in the layout, and it is specific to the star: \PORTER{}
reserves $f_\mathrm{min}$ in every core, treating cores as
interchangeable, which they are on a grid and a ring and are not where
one core is a hub every route must cross. On star Multiplier that
reservation opens with free counts $(2,2,13,27)$, the hub pinned at the
floor while a whole core sits empty. 
%Raising the reservation uniformly does not repair it; a reservation scaled by a core's position in the core graph is untried.

This bounds the layout reading rather than extending it: up to $108$
physical qubits, across two core shapes and three core graphs, the initial
layout separates the two compilers and controlling it erases the
difference --- a bound that the scale-up series of
Section~\ref{sec:scaleup}, where the same control does not help, keeps
from being read as a general one.

The trade is time against EPR pairs. \PORTER{} costs $1.02\times$
\dSABRE{}'s EPR at $1.18\times$ its compile time; refinement buys the EPR
lead, $0.95\times$, for $2.10\times$ the time, on eight routes against
\dSABRE{}'s nine.

All times are \emph{complete} compile costs --- layout generation plus
every route over every candidate, not the winning route alone. \PORTER{}
routes fewer times than \dSABRE{} at every operating point, four against
nine and eight against nine, and is still the slower of the two: its
routes are the dearer ones, because each enumerates whole plans, forward-
simulates packets and searches two tiers of capacity relief. Compile time
is therefore not an argument for \PORTER{} on these suites --- we report
it because the EPR figures are only meaningful beside the budget that
produced them. It does become one at scale, where the same four routes
run in $0.50\times$ \dSABRE{}'s complete cost
(Section~\ref{sec:scaleup}).
