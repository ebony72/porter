\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A distributed quantum computer (DQC) runs one circuit across several small
cores joined by links that distribute entanglement on
demand~\cite{cuomo2020towards,escofet2024review}. Any two-qubit gate
whose operands sit in different cores must be paid for with
Einstein--Podolsky--Rosen (EPR) pairs, and distributing a pair is far
slower and less reliable than any operation inside a
core~\cite{stephenson2020high,daiss2021quantum}. Pairs consumed is
therefore the metric we minimise (Section~\ref{sec:scope}).

A pair can be spent three ways. Teleporting a \emph{state} (\TeleData{})
relocates a logical qubit, making every later gate it meets in its new
core local~\cite{bennett1993teleporting}. Teleporting a \emph{gate}
(\TeleGate{}) executes one straddling controlled gate through a
cat-entangler, with neither operand
moving~\cite{eisert2000optimal,yimsiriwattana2004generalized}. Holding
that resource open across a run of straddling gates sharing a control
costs one pair for the whole run --- a \emph{packet}, following
AutoComm's amortisation~\cite{wu2022autocomm}. Which is cheapest depends
on the circuit structure where it is applied, so none dominates.

The choice must be made before its price is known. Each alternative
becomes executable only after its own preparation --- the right operand
beside the right communication port, that port free, the destination
holding a spare slot --- and a compiler performs that work only for the
alternative it selects. Scoring an action together with the preparation
it currently needs confuses what remote work is worth doing with how far
the layout is from doing it: candidates are ranked by the layout the
compiler happens to occupy rather than by the progress they buy, and a
router that reopens the choice mid-preparation can be left carrying SWAPs
towards an action it abandons.

\PORTER{} --- a \emph{Plan-Oriented Router for Teleportation-Enabled
aRchitectures} --- separates the two questions. Its unit of choice is a
\emph{plan}: a complete specification of one inter-core action and the
conditions it needs, omitting the SWAP sequence that would establish
them. Plans are compared on the gate progress they buy, one is committed,
and only then are preparation SWAPs generated for it. Around this loop
\PORTER{} adds two-tier capacity relief, which manufactures free slots
when every candidate is blocked, and a single deterministic initial
layout in place of the best-of-$N$ search its predecessors run.

\textbf{Results.} We evaluate on MQT-Bench~\cite{quetschlich2023mqtbench}
suites at 25, 64 and 80 logical qubits, a $100$--$360$-qubit scale-up
series, and two networks of IBM heavy-hex cores --- forty-eight
circuit--architecture instances. Against
\TeleSABRE{}~\cite{russo2025telesabre}, the strongest published router we
identified under this cost model, \PORTER{} lowers geometric-mean EPR
consumption by $59.6\%$, $41.6\%$ and $68.2\%$ on the three suites at
three routes each, over the instances both complete, and it completes all
twenty-four --- including the seven \TeleSABRE{} does not converge on,
among them an 80-qubit, $23{,}381$-CX circuit.

The second comparison is with \dSABRE{}~\cite{lidsabre}, concurrent work
on the same problem and the closest point of reference we have. \PORTER{}
is competitive on the three benchmark suites, spending $1.02\times$ its pooled
EPR for $0.91\times$ its compile time under each compiler's own protocol,
and pulls ahead as the machine grows: on the scale-up series it uses
$0.72\times$ \dSABRE{}'s pairs and wins every instance.

A pooled figure that close invites the question of what a second compiler
adds, and the per-instance record answers it: neither wins more than
eleven of the twenty-four, and taking the better of the two per instance
uses $9.3\%$ fewer EPR pairs than \dSABRE{} alone --- $8.0\%$ with the two
cheapest circuit families set aside. A design that merely re-derived
\dSABRE{}'s decisions would leave such a portfolio nothing to choose
between. The two are close relatives and share four design decisions, set
out in Section~\ref{sec:related} so neither is credited with the other's;
what the portfolio measures is the part that is not shared.

\textbf{Problem.} Given a circuit, a multi-core machine and an initial
layout, emit a legal operation stream realising every gate while
minimising EPR pairs consumed. Section~\ref{sec:scope} states this
formally, with the cost model it assumes and what that excludes.

\textbf{Contributions.} We make three.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=2pt,label=(\roman*)]
\item \emph{The \PORTER{} compiler}: a plan-oriented remote stage that
  scores a complete inter-core action before any preparation SWAP for it
  exists and commits before preparing
  (Section~\ref{sec:sfc-plan-first}), a capacity discipline that treats
  free slots as a legality condition and manufactures them by a two-tier
  relief search (Section~\ref{sec:sfc-relief}), and a deterministic
  initial layout replacing the best-of-$N$ search its predecessors run
  (Section~\ref{sec:sfc-layout}).
\item \emph{Per-circuit selection of the lookahead horizon.} Every
  \SABRE{}-descended router fixes this parameter by constant, and no
  tested constant is best across our benchmark --- the best value spans a
  factor of thirty-two. \PORTER{} measures how dense the circuit's
  interaction graph is, prunes a three-value candidate set with it,
  routes each and keeps the lowest-EPR result; the configured horizon
  always competes and wins ties, so selection cannot return a worse route
  than routing once. It is what carries \PORTER{} past \dSABRE{} at scale
  (Section~\ref{sec:horizon}).
\item \emph{A controlled study over forty-eight instances}, under native,
  shared-layout and cross-layout protocols, with ablations measuring what
  each mechanism contributes --- including a negative finding, which
  Section~\ref{sec:discussion} interprets. Every route is certified by a
  separately implemented replay verifier, and the harness reproducing
  every table is released.
\end{enumerate}

Section~\ref{sec:rqs} states the four questions the evaluation answers
and ties each mechanism to the experiment that assesses it. The
implementation, the replay verifier, the comparison harness and the raw
per-circuit results are available at
\url{https://github.com/ebony72/porter}.
