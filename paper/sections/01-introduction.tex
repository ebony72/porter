\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Distributed quantum computers (DQCs) execute a circuit across several
small quantum cores connected by entanglement-distribution links
\cite{cuomo2020towards,escofet2025review,ang2024arquin}. A two-qubit gate whose operands
are in different cores requires inter-core communication. Under the cost
model considered here, that communication consumes
Einstein--Podolsky--Rosen (EPR) pairs; distributing such pairs is much
slower and less reliable than operating within a core
\cite{stephenson2020high,daiss2021quantum}. We accordingly minimise
EPR-pair consumption (Section~\ref{sec:scope}).

A compiler can use an EPR pair in three ways. \TeleData{} moves a logical
qubit to another core, potentially making several later gates local
\cite{bennett1993teleporting}. \TeleGate{} executes one remote controlled
gate through a cat-entangler, without moving either operand
\cite{eisert2000optimal,yimsiriwattana2004generalized}. A same-control
\TeleGatePacket{} keeps that resource open for an ordered run
of remote gates with a common control, so that the run consumes one pair
rather than one pair per gate \cite{wu2022autocomm}. The best choice depends
on the circuit structure where it is applied: data teleportation has
possible future benefit, gate teleportation resolves one gate immediately,
and a packet is beneficial only when a suitable run can be found.

This choice is coupled to local routing. Before an inter-core action can
execute, its operands may need to be moved to designated communication
ports, the ports must be free, and a \TeleData{} destination must have
space. If a router scores an action together with all of the SWAPs required
by the current layout, it can favour an easy-to-prepare action over one
that resolves more useful remote work. Moreover, changing the selected
remote action during preparation can leave the router with SWAPs that no
longer serve its decision.

We present \PORTER{} (\emph{Plan-Oriented Router for Teleportation-Enabled
aRchitectures}), a deterministic router that separates action selection
from action preparation. \PORTER{} represents each candidate as a
\emph{plan}: the chosen inter-core primitive, the gates it can discharge,
and the conditions needed to execute it, but not the SWAP sequence that
will satisfy those conditions. It first ranks plans by the progress they
provide, uses preparation cost only to break ties, commits to one plan,
and then generates the local SWAPs needed to make that plan ready.
\PORTER{} also includes capacity relief for congested cores and a
deterministic initial-layout heuristic. Its operating protocol then routes each
circuit at a fixed portfolio of teleport-lookahead horizons.

\textbf{Problem.} Given a circuit, a multi-core architecture, and an initial
layout, produce a legal operation stream that implements the circuit while
minimising the number of EPR pairs consumed. Section~\ref{sec:scope}
defines the model, objective, and assumptions precisely.

\textbf{Contributions.} This paper makes three contributions.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=2pt,label=(\roman*)]
\item \emph{A plan-committed distributed router.} \PORTER{} selects a
complete inter-core plan before generating plan-specific preparation
SWAPs (Section~\ref{sec:sfc-plan-first}). It combines this stage with a
two-tier capacity-relief mechanism and a deterministic initial-layout
heuristic (Sections~\ref{sec:sfc-relief} and~\ref{sec:sfc-layout}).
\item \emph{A fixed lookahead-horizon portfolio.} Rather than assuming that
one teleport-lookahead horizon suits every circuit, \PORTER{} runs
the same four horizons and retains the completed route with the lowest EPR
count. Since the configured horizon is included and wins ties, this
portfolio cannot return a higher EPR count than that configured one-route
baseline when the baseline completes (Section~\ref{sec:horizon}).
\item \emph{A controlled empirical evaluation.} We evaluate 48
circuit--architecture instances using native, shared-layout, and
cross-layout protocols, and we ablate the principal mechanisms.
Every reported \PORTER{} route is checked by an independently implemented
replay verifier; the implementation, evaluation harness, and per-circuit
results are released.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Results in brief.} On MQT-Bench~\cite{quetschlich2023mqtbench}
suites with 25, 64, and 80 logical
qubits, \PORTER{} reduces geometric-mean EPR consumption relative to
\TeleSABRE{}~\cite{russo2025telesabre} by $59.7\%$, $44.9\%$, and
$68.8\%$, respectively, on the
instances completed by both methods; \PORTER{} completes all 24 main-suite
instances whereas \TeleSABRE{} completes 17. Compared with
\dSABRE{}~\cite{lidsabre},
\PORTER{} is competitive rather than uniformly superior on the main suites:
it uses $0.99\times$ \dSABRE{}'s pooled EPR count at $1.20\times$ its
complete compile time under the respective native protocols. On the
100--360-qubit QFT and QPEexact scale-up series, \PORTER{} uses
$0.69\times$ \dSABRE{}'s EPR count and $0.52\times$ its compile time,
and it obtains the lower EPR count on all six instances.

These are results under the stated protocols.
The route budgets differ: \PORTER{} spends four routes against
\TeleSABRE{}'s three seeds and \dSABRE{}'s nine. 

\textbf{Organisation.} Section~\ref{sec:background} fixes the machine model,
the communication primitives, and the problem statement.
Section~\ref{sec:sfc} presents \PORTER{}. Section~\ref{sec:methodology} states the
research questions, the experimental inputs, and the comparison protocols,
including the multi-horizon and refinement protocols that invoke the router
repeatedly. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the results in the order of
those questions. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses what the evidence
does and does not establish and states the open problems.
Section~\ref{sec:related} places \PORTER{} among closely related routers,
especially \dSABRE{}, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

The implementation, replay verifier, comparison harness, and raw
per-circuit results are available at
\url{https://github.com/ebony72/porter}. Supplementary material provides benchmark-construction details, external
layouts, verifier checks, a worked routing example, per-instance results,
parameter settings, complexity analysis, and threats to validity.
