\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A distributed quantum computer (DQC) runs one circuit across several small
quantum processing units --- \emph{cores} --- joined by links that
distribute entanglement on demand~\cite{cuomo2020towards,escofet2024review}.
What such a machine is short of is not gates but entanglement. Any two-qubit
gate whose operands sit in different cores has to be bought with
Einstein--Podolsky--Rosen (EPR) pairs, and on current hardware distributing
a pair is far slower and far less reliable than any operation performed
inside a core~\cite{stephenson2020high,daiss2021quantum}. How few pairs a
compiler spends is therefore the primary optimisation metric under the
cost model used throughout this paper (Section~\ref{sec:scope}), and the
one we minimise.

There is more than one way to spend a pair. A \emph{state} can be
teleported (\TeleData{}), relocating a logical qubit so every later
gate it meets in its new core is free~\cite{bennett1993teleporting}. A
\emph{gate} can be teleported (\TeleGate{}) through a cat-entangler,
executing one remote CX with neither operand
moving~\cite{eisert2000optimal,yimsiriwattana2004generalized}. Or one
cat-entangled resource can be held open across a run of remote CX gates
sharing a control, so the run costs one pair instead of one each --- a
\emph{packet}, in the spirit of AutoComm's gate-sequence
amortisation~\cite{wu2022autocomm}. Which of the three is cheapest
depends on the circuit structure where it is applied, not on the
primitive, so none of them dominates.

The choice must be made before its price is known. Each alternative
becomes executable only after its own preparation --- the right operand
beside the right communication port, that port free, the destination
holding a spare slot --- and preparation is work the compiler will not
perform unless it selects that alternative. Scoring an action together
with the preparation it currently needs therefore ranks candidates by
the layout the compiler happens to be sitting in rather than by the
progress they buy, and staging a losing candidate still perturbs the
layout, so effort is spent preparing actions never taken.

\PORTER{} --- a \emph{Plan-Oriented Router for Teleportation-Enabled
aRchitectures} --- keeps the two questions apart. Its unit of choice is
a \emph{plan}: a complete specification of one inter-core action that
fixes what the action is and what conditions it needs, but deliberately
omits the SWAP sequence that would establish them. Whole plans are
compared on the gate progress they buy; one is committed; only then are
preparation SWAPs generated, for that plan alone. Around this loop
\PORTER{} adds a two-tier
capacity-relief mechanism that manufactures free slots when every
candidate is capacity-blocked, and a deterministic, seed-free initial
layout replacing the seeded best-of-$N$ search its predecessors run.

\textbf{Results.} We evaluate on MQT-Bench~\cite{quetschlich2023mqtbench}
suites at 25, 64 and 80 logical qubits, on a $100$--$360$-qubit
scale-up series, and on two networks of IBM heavy-hex cores --- forty-eight
circuit--architecture instances. Against
\TeleSABRE{}~\cite{russo2025telesabre}, the published state of the art
for this cost model, \PORTER{} uses substantially fewer EPR pairs: over
the instances both complete it lowers geometric-mean consumption by
$59.6\%$, $41.6\%$ and $68.2\%$ on the three suites at the same
three-attempt budget, and it completes every instance,
including the seven --- among them an 80-qubit, $23{,}381$-CX circuit
--- on which \TeleSABRE{} does not converge in three seeds.

The second comparison is less one-sided and we report it in full.
\dSABRE{}~\cite{lidsabre} is concurrent work on the same problem whose
router was substantially revised while this paper was in preparation,
so we re-ran it from source rather than quoting its tables. Against
that version \PORTER{} is \emph{competitive rather than dominant}.
Under each compiler's own protocol \PORTER{} costs $1.07\times$
\dSABRE{}'s pooled geometric-mean EPR at $0.58\times$ its compile time;
restoring a comparable pass budget reaches $1.05\times$ at
$0.74\times$. Routed from identical externally supplied layouts at matched pass
budgets \PORTER{} moves \emph{ahead} across forty-one instances
($126.6$ against $134.2$), and the same control takes both heavy-hex
core graphs. On the $100$--$360$-qubit series it \emph{leads} outright by
$0.72\times$ --- a reversal traced in Section~\ref{sec:horizon} to a
single mistuned constant, the lookahead horizon, which no one value
serves across the benchmark. The two compilers share four
load-bearing design decisions, set out in Section~\ref{sec:related} so
that neither is credited with the other's.

\textbf{What the mechanisms are worth.} Because \PORTER{} and \dSABRE{}
are close relatives, a head-to-head number says little about which design
decision earns its place, so we ablate \PORTER{}'s in isolation and report
the outcome as measured. Capacity relief is what makes a
deterministic-greedy design complete in practice: without it four of six
80-qubit instances fail to route. The initial layout is what separates the
two compilers below roughly a hundred physical qubits, and the lookahead
horizon is what separates them above it. Two answers are negative, and we
state them plainly. Plan \emph{commitment} --- the rule that a selected
plan is not reopened until it is ready --- reaches the same EPR as
re-scoring the whole front every iteration, within $0.6\%$ and on the same
number of routing moves, so what it buys is compile time rather than
better routes; note that this ablates the commitment rule and not the
plan abstraction it sits on, which no experiment here removes
(Section~\ref{sec:discussion}). And the packet primitive, which exists to
amortise a pair across a run of gates, is outperformed at 80 qubits by
removing gate teleportation altogether, by $32.8\%$ --- an interaction
between the action vocabulary and the plan scorer rather than a verdict
on amortisation.

\textbf{Problem.} Given a circuit DAG on $n$ logical qubits, a machine of
$K$ cores with intra-core coupling graphs, inter-core links and designated
communication ports, and an initial layout, emit a legal operation stream
realising every gate while minimising the number of EPR pairs consumed.
Section~\ref{sec:scope} states the cost model this objective assumes and
what it excludes.

\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt,label=(\roman*)]
\item \emph{Algorithmic.} A plan-oriented remote stage that scores a
  complete inter-core action on the gate progress it buys, before any
  preparation SWAP for it exists, and commits to one before generating
  preparation (Section~\ref{sec:sfc-plan-first}).
\item \emph{Algorithmic.} A capacity discipline that treats free slots as
  a legality condition and manufactures them by a two-tier relief search
  when every candidate is blocked (Section~\ref{sec:sfc-relief}).
\item \emph{Algorithmic.} A deterministic, seed-free initial layout that
  replaces the seeded best-of-$N$ search its predecessors run
  (Section~\ref{sec:sfc-layout}).
\item \emph{Empirical.} The observation that the teleport-lookahead
  horizon --- a parameter every \SABRE{}-descended router fixes by
  constant --- has no good constant, its best value spanning a factor of
  thirty-two across our benchmark, and can instead be chosen per circuit
  by routing a small pruned candidate set (Section~\ref{sec:horizon}).
\item \emph{Empirical.} A controlled study over forty-eight
  circuit--architecture instances under native, shared-layout and
  cross-layout protocols, with mechanism ablations that include two
  negative results (Sections~\ref{sec:results}--\ref{sec:discussion}).
\item \emph{Infrastructural.} A separately implemented replay verifier
  that re-derives gate coverage, dependency order and physical legality
  from the emitted operation stream alone, tested by fault injection, and
  a released harness reproducing every table.
\end{enumerate}
No single mechanism accounts for the results, and
Section~\ref{sec:rqs} poses the four questions the evaluation answers.
Each mechanism is tied to the experiment that assesses it: plan
commitment to the hop-wise ablation, capacity relief to the completion
ablation, the initial layout to the shared- and cross-layout controls,
horizon selection to the scale-up series and the star-Multiplier livelock,
packets to the action-vocabulary study, and the verifier to fault
injection.

The \PORTER{} implementation, the replay verifier, the full cross-router
comparison harness, and the raw per-circuit results are publicly available at
\url{https://github.com/ebony72/porter}.
