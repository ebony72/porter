\section{The 80-Qubit Suite: Reconstruction Detail}
\label{app:suite80}

The 80-qubit suite (\mainSetup{} of the main paper) was generated with
\texttt{mqt.bench}~2.2.2~\cite{quetschlich2023mqtbench}
(device-independent generation followed by
transpilation to our basis, since the installed \texttt{mqt.bench}'s
target-aware native-gate path raised an unrelated \texttt{qiskit}
compatibility error on every circuit we tried). AE at this size is not
directly producible by stock \texttt{mqt.bench}: its phase-estimation
construction evaluates $\mathrm{Operator}(Q)^{2^{j}}$ for each
evaluation bit $j$, and the accumulated floating-point non-unitarity
trips \texttt{qiskit}'s unitarity check
(\texttt{"Input matrix is not unitary"}) beyond roughly $27$ evaluation
qubits, which is why published AE files stop near $54$~qubits. Since the
Bernoulli operator $Q$ is a single-qubit rotation, we instead supply the
power analytically, $Q^{2^{j}}=R_Y(2^{j+1}\theta_p)$ ($R_Y$ periodic in
$4\pi$), and otherwise run \texttt{mqt.bench}'s own native-gate
transpilation unchanged. The CNOT sub-sequence---which alone determines
routing---is decomposition-invariant in these rotation angles and is
byte-identical to stock \texttt{mqt.bench} at every size the latter can
build (verified at $10$, $20$, $25$, and $28$~qubits); only the
routing-irrelevant single-qubit angles carry floating-point error at
high bit indices.

QNN required an analogous reconstruction. Between
\texttt{mqt.bench}~1.1.0 (which produced the deep 8{,}126-CX 64q QNN
this suite uses) and the installed 2.2.2, the benchmark's definition
was changed from a ZZ feature map (full all-pairs entanglement) plus a
\texttt{RealAmplitudes} ansatz to a plain Z feature map (no
entanglement) plus the same ansatz. The current generator therefore
emits a near-linear 79-CX circuit at 80 qubits whose two-qubit
interaction graph is \emph{identical} to GHZ's --- a degenerate
duplicate, not a distinct benchmark. We restore the original deep QNN
by rebuilding the 1.1.0 construction (ZZ feature map, \texttt{reps}=2,
full entanglement; \texttt{RealAmplitudes}, \texttt{reps}=1) and
transpiling it to our basis; at 64 qubits this reproduces the checked-in
1.1.0 file's CNOT sub-sequence byte-for-byte (identical ordered pair
sequence, all $\binom{64}{2}$ interacting pairs, 8{,}126 CX), so the
80-qubit instance (a fully-connected $\binom{80}{2}$-edge interaction
graph, 12{,}718 CX) is a faithful extrapolation of the same
construction.

\section{External Layouts from pytket-dqc}
\label{app:pytket}

As a third, independent layout source complementing the shared-layout
protocol of \mainProtocols{} of the main paper, we route \dSABRE{} and
\PORTER{} from a placement generated by \texttt{pytket-dqc}'s
\texttt{HypergraphPartitioning} allocator~\cite{pytketdqc2024}
(best-of-3 KaHyPar seeds on a \texttt{NISQNetwork} matching our core
graph), converted to a physical layout by the same topology-aware
intra-core assignment \dSABRE{}'s own pytket benchmark
uses~\cite{lidsabre}: each core's assigned logical qubits are placed
by descending interaction-graph degree onto physical positions ordered
by ascending intra-core centrality, then locally refined by a bounded
pairwise-swap pass. \TeleSABRE{} is excluded for the same structural
reason as in \mainProtocols{} (no externally supplied
layout support in its CLI). \texttt{HypergraphPartitioning} only
decides which \emph{core} each logical qubit goes to; the capacity it
sees for a core is the length of that core's declared qubit list in
the \texttt{NISQNetwork}. We withhold from every core's declared capacity, and from the later
physical-placement step, the four minimum-degree (corner) qubits of the
whole-chip graph --- the chip-wide corner rule \dSABRE{} shipped when
these legs were run, rather than the per-core count its own protocol now
uses (\mainProtocols{}). As there, the reserved qubits hold spare
capacity; they are not communication ports. This guarantees only a
four-qubit margin \emph{chip-wide}, not per-core, so a core can still
end up fully packed; rather than skip the run in that case,
\PORTER{}'s general safeguard against fully packed cores
(\mainInitialLayout{}) legalizes the layout automatically
before routing begins. \dSABRE{}'s teledata-only mechanism does not
need a free port on the side it teleports \emph{from} and was never
affected by this precondition. Because \texttt{pytket-dqc} uses gate
teleportation, not state teleportation, its own reported cost is in a
different unit (e-bits per Steiner-tree-amortised hyperedge, not EPR
pairs per qubit move) and is included only as context, following
\dSABRE{}'s own established convention for this
comparison~\cite{lidsabre}.

Table~\ref{tab:pytket} reports
a complete grid: \PORTER{}'s full-core safeguard
legalizes the three layouts that would
otherwise violate its teleport precondition (25q QNN, 64q AE,
64q QNN), at the cost of the layout-quality hit an emergency eviction
inevitably takes -- most visibly on 64q QNN, where the evicted layout
lands \PORTER{} at 1{,}031 EPR pairs against \dSABRE{}'s 535, its worst
relative result on this suite. \dSABRE{} takes this grid: it uses
fewer EPR pairs on seven of the twelve rows (25q AE/QFT, 64q
AE/Graphstate/QFT/QNN/Random) to \PORTER{}'s two (25q QNN/Random),
with three ties, and its geometric mean is $11\%$ lower
($63.1$ vs.\ \PORTER{} $70.2$). Pooled with the \TeleSABRE{}-layout
grid of \mainSharedTable{} of the main paper, \dSABRE{} uses fewer EPR
pairs on twenty-three of the forty-one fixed-layout instances, \PORTER{}
on thirteen, with five ties (\mainSharedResults{}).

The grid above feeds pytket-dqc's partition to \PORTER{} as a
controlled probe. The complementary pipeline question -- offering the
same partition to \PORTER{} as an optional extra layout candidate
beside its own, which lowers geometric-mean EPR by a further $16\%$ on
the most partitionable circuits but is kept opt-in on cost and
robustness grounds -- is a configuration study of \PORTER{} itself
rather than part of this comparison, and is deferred to
Appendix~\ref{app:optin}.

\section{pytket-dqc as an Opt-In \PORTER{} Layout Candidate}
\label{app:optin}

Appendix~\ref{app:pytket} feeds pytket-dqc's partition to \PORTER{} as a
controlled probe. The same partition can also be offered to \PORTER{} as
one extra layout candidate beside its own (\mainInitialLayout{}), routing
both and keeping the lower EPR count. That construction cannot lose, so
the question is only what it gains and what it costs.
\mainSharedResults{} states the conclusion; this appendix gives the
figures behind it.

\textbf{What it gains.} Over all twenty-four instances the best-of-two
lowers geometric-mean EPR from $174.9$ to $153.8$ ($-12.1\%$). Over the
eighteen excluding GHZ and Graphstate it lowers it from $464.6$ to
$456.1$ --- $\mathbf{-1.8\%}$. The partition is selected on nine of the
twenty-four, and the three largest margins are Graphstate at each width
($7\!\to\!4$, $20\!\to\!10$, $28\!\to\!10$); GHZ contributes
$4\!\to\!3$, $6\!\to\!5$ and a tie. Everything else it wins, it wins
narrowly. On the dense circuits it is the worse start and the best-of
simply keeps \PORTER{}'s own layout (64q~QNN $638$ against $740$,
64q~Random $737$ against $787$, 80q~AE $299$ against $389$).

\textbf{What it costs.} Table~\ref{tab:pytkettime} times the
best-of-three KaHyPar partition against \PORTER{}'s own layout
construction and against \PORTER{}'s complete three-route protocol, on
the same machine as every other measurement in this paper. The partition
is three to five orders of magnitude dearer than the layout it would
replace, and on six of the eight circuits shown it costs more than
routing the circuit three times. It also scales worse than the routing
does: an $80\times$ increase in CX count from 25q~AE to 80q~Random buys a
$200\times$ increase in partition time, and at the widths of
\mainScaleup{} it does not finish at all.

\textbf{Why it stays opt-in.} The gain is $1.8\%$ once the two smallest
circuit families are set aside, and it is bought at a preprocessing cost
that exceeds the whole compilation. A third consideration is
robustness: the partition reintroduces an external dependency that
\PORTER{}'s self-contained layout avoids, and unlike it can leave a core
fully packed, relying on the full-core repair of \mainInitialLayout{} to
become routable at all. \PORTER{} therefore defaults to its own layout
and treats an external partition as an optional refinement, worth its
price only where EPR is at a premium, the circuit is cleanly separable,
and the partitioning time is acceptable.

\section{Independent Replay Verification}
\label{app:verification}

Beyond code review, we built an independent replay verifier that
treats the router as a black box: it takes only the emitted
\texttt{CompiledOp} stream, the original circuit, and the initial
layout, and re-derives, from scratch, whether the stream is a faithful
and physically legal execution. The verifier maintains its own
physical-position map and a per-logical-qubit FIFO queue of pending
gate IDs (derived directly from the circuit, independent of any
router-internal DAG state), and checks, per emitted operation:
\begin{itemize}
\item every gate is popped from the head of \emph{every} one of its
  operand qubits' queues before being marked executed, so gates cannot
  execute out of DAG order or twice;
\item every \texttt{local\_swap}/\texttt{local\_cx} acts on a genuine
  intra-core coupling edge, at the physical positions the verifier's
  own simulated layout says the operand logical qubits occupy;
\item every \texttt{teleport} uses the correct inter-core port pair for
  its two cores, both ports were free beforehand, and the source
  qubit was coupling-adjacent to the source port;
\item every \texttt{cat\_entangler}, \texttt{cat\_cx}, and
  \texttt{cat\_disentangler} triple observes port-adjacency and
  free-port preconditions, and no other operation touches either
  communication port while a cat section is open;
\item the emitted \texttt{RouterStats} counters (EPR, teleport,
  telegate, packet, SWAP, local-CX, single counts) match the counts
  the verifier itself derives from the op stream, catching any
  bookkeeping drift between what is emitted and what is counted.
\end{itemize}
We ran the verifier against 60 randomized circuits (4--40 logical
qubits, 20--600 gates, both architectures, seeds 0--59) and against
every circuit in the evaluation suite (\mainEvaluation{} of the main
paper);
all pass. Two further artefacts make these checks concrete: a
\emph{certified trace} (Figure~\ref{fig:trace}) and a
\emph{fault-injection study} (Table~\ref{tab:faults}).

\textbf{A certified trace.} Figure~\ref{fig:trace} replays the
stream \PORTER{} emits for the running example of the main paper
(\mainRunningExample{}) --- fourteen operations exercising
every op type --- and lists, per operation, exactly what the verifier
asserts before accepting it. The
trace makes the three primitives'
legality conditions operational: the \TeleGatePacket{} is one
\emph{bracketed section} in which the two reserved ports are
untouchable and every interleaved SWAP must carry packet provenance;
the \TeleData{} at op 11 must name the unique port pair of its core
pair, find both ports free, start port-adjacent, and land exactly on
the destination port; and every gate execution, local or remote, is
popped from the head of each operand's pending-gate queue, so DAG
order is enforced gate by gate rather than assumed from the router's
own bookkeeping.

\textbf{Fault injection.} On streams that always pass, a verifier
that checks nothing is indistinguishable from one that checks
everything, so we also certify that each check \emph{fires}:
\texttt{verify\_fault\_injection.py} takes passing op streams and
applies the ten single-point corruptions of Table~\ref{tab:faults},
one mutant per corruption, spanning misuse of all three communication
primitives (wrong ports, free-port violations, broken cat-section
bracketing), gate-coverage and dependency-order violations, illegal
SWAPs, and statistics drift. Across the running example (which
admits all ten corruption classes) and thirty fuzzed circuits on both
architectures, all 276 applicable mutants are rejected, each by the
intended check; the same harness runs on any benchmark QASM file.

\section{Running Example: Why Commit to a Plan?}
\label{sec:sfc-example}

Figure~\ref{fig:example} shows a front where plan commitment, the
packet vocabulary, and the $f_\mathrm{min}$ capacity gate all
interact, and where \PORTER{}'s decision differs from what either
baseline would choose. Two adjacent cores of architecture~A are shown. Core $c_0$
holds $\logi{0}$, $\logi{4}$ (adjacent to the port), and $\logi{6}$,
with six free positions; core $c_1$ holds eight qubits --- the targets
$\logi{1},\logi{2},\logi{3}$, the port-adjacent $\logi{5}$, and four
spectators --- so its \emph{only} free position is its port. The
remaining circuit fragment is
$g_1{=}\mathrm{CX}(\logi{0},\logi{1})$,
$g_2{=}\mathrm{CX}(\logi{0},\logi{2})$,
$g_3{=}\mathrm{CX}(\logi{0},\logi{3})$,
$g_5{=}\mathrm{CX}(\logi{0},\logi{6})$, chained on $\logi{0}$, plus an
independent $g_4{=}\mathrm{CX}(\logi{4},\logi{5})$. The front is
therefore $\{g_1, g_4\}$ --- $g_2$ and $g_3$ share $\logi{0}$ with
$g_1$ and only surface after it executes. All front gates are
adjacent-remote, so the plan-first stage fires.

\textbf{What \PORTER{} does.} Table~\ref{tab:example-plans} lists the
generated plans. Seeding packet collection at $g_1$, the frontier
simulation absorbs $g_2$ and $g_3$ (same control $\logi{0}$, targets
in $c_1$): a three-gate packet with direct gain $3$. Both single
telegates and all \TeleData{} plans into $c_0$ gain ${\approx}1$.
\TeleData{}($\logi{0}\!\to\!c_1$) would gain more --- direct $1$ for
$g_1$, latent-direct $2$ for $g_2,g_3$, minus $0.9$ of lookahead for
$g_5$, whose partner $\logi{6}$ stays in $c_0$ --- but $c_1$ fails the
$f_\mathrm{min}$ test, so it lands in $\mathcal{B}$; its gain
${\approx}2.1$ does not exceed the packet's $3.0$ by
$m_\mathrm{assist}{=}5$, so no capacity relief pre-empts. Plans are
ranked by gain first --- \chg{preparation cost is only a tie-break --- so
the \emph{unready} packet (gain $3$) beats the \emph{ready} telegate for}
$g_4$ (gain $1$). \PORTER{} commits the packet, generates its one
preparation SWAP ($\logi{0}$ next to the port), then emits one
cat-entangler, three cat-CXs (each target routed beside the port in
turn), and the disentangler: \emph{one} EPR pair for $g_1,g_2,g_3$.
The packet's completion exposes $g_4$ and $g_5$ together; the
locality-first stage drains the now-local $g_5$ first (one SWAP plus
a local CX), and the remote stage then re-generates plans for $g_4$:
the single telegate and \TeleData{}($\logi{5}\!\to\!c_0$) now tie at
gain $1$, and the \chg{capacity tie-break --- negative for the \TeleData{},
zero for the \TeleGate{} (Section~\ref{main-sec:sfc-scoring}) --- commits
the teleport ---}
$\logi{5}$ lands on $c_0$'s port and $g_4$ finishes as a local CX.
Two EPR pairs for the whole fragment. This is not a stylised
trace: it is exactly the operation stream the implementation emits
on this instance, replayed and certified by the independent verifier
of Appendix~D, which includes the full
operation-by-operation certified trace.

\textbf{What finer-grained routers do here.} A router that scores
individual actions --- SWAPs and inter-core operations in one pool, as
\TeleSABRE{} does --- fires the zero-cost ready telegate for $g_4$
first. The deeper problem is the staging SWAP the packet needs:
exchanging $\logi{0}$ and $\logi{4}$ moves $g_1$'s control one step
closer to the port while moving $g_4$'s one step away, so under a
front-averaged distance metric of the kind used by \SABRE{} descendants
its score is ${\approx}0$; the information that makes this SWAP worth
applying --- three remote CXs behind $g_1$ amortised onto one EPR pair
--- is a property of a \emph{whole plan}, invisible at single-action
granularity. That blind spot does not require a flat pool: \dSABRE{}
reaches its inter-core stage on this front (no front gate is
intra-core) and scores hops, not plans, so the same amortisation is
outside what its candidate can express.

Vocabulary compounds the gap. Gates $g_2$ and $g_3$ enter the front one
at a time, so \TeleSABRE{} pays one telegate each, four EPR pairs for
the fragment. \dSABRE{}, restricted to \TeleData{}, cannot move
$\logi{0}$ into the saturated $c_1$ at all --- under its capacity
floor a teleport into a core with fewer than two free slots is not a
legal candidate --- so it must first relay a free slot into $c_1$ from
elsewhere, and ferrying $\logi{1},\logi{2},\logi{3}$ out to $c_0$
instead costs three EPR pairs before $g_4$ is even addressed.
Commitment finally protects the choice once made: only the packet's
preparation SWAPs are generated --- no staging effort is spent on the
six losing plans --- and the committed plan cannot be pre-empted
mid-preparation by a candidate that its own staging transiently
improved, a churn per-action re-scoring permits by construction.

Table~\ref{tab:actionset} puts the three compilers' primitives side by
side on the 64-qubit suite, which is the sharpest statement of what a
larger action vocabulary is worth: every action listed costs exactly one
EPR pair, so an action count and an EPR count are the same number ---
except for \PORTER{}'s packets, where one action executes several gates.

\TeleSABRE{} spends $289$ of its $4{,}478$ actions ($6.5\%$) on single
telegates, each serving exactly one remote CX, and uses the most EPR
pairs of the three. \dSABRE{} spends none. \PORTER{}'s cat-entangler
actions are $1{,}438$ of $4{,}500$ ($32.0\%$), but they are not the same
object: $801$ packets carry $1{,}976$ remote CX gates, $2.47$ per EPR
pair, against $1.00$ for the $637$ single telegates. Packets are $56\%$
of \PORTER{}'s cat-entangler actions and $76\%$ of the gates those
actions serve.

Two features of the table shape the ablation of
Section~\ref{main-sec:ablation}. First, the vocabulary is used very unevenly:
Random and Multiplier account for $612$ of \PORTER{}'s $637$ single
telegates, while QNN and QAOA account for $571$ of its $801$ packets.
Second, amortisation and EPR consumption do not move together ---
\PORTER{} spends more cat-entangler actions than \TeleSABRE{} and fewer
EPR pairs than it, but also more EPR pairs than \dSABRE{}, which spends
none at all. Whether the extra vocabulary pays is therefore not
answerable from this table; Section~\ref{main-sec:ablation} answers it
directly, and the answer is not the one the design assumed.

\section{Computational Complexity}
\label{sec:sfc-complexity}

Let $N$ be the gate count, $n$ the logical-qubit count, $K$ the core
count and $M$ the per-core physical-qubit count. Distance tables are
precomputed once at construction in $O(KM^2\log M + K^2\log K)$,
matching the two-level Dijkstra decomposition comparable compilers
use~\cite{lidsabre}. Per iteration, classification and draining cost
$O(|F|)$; intra-core SWAP scoring evaluates $O(M)$ candidate edges,
each at $O(|F_\mathrm{loc}| + N_\mathrm{loc})$ over one DAG snapshot
reused across candidates; remote-plan scoring evaluates $O(n)$
candidates at $O(N_\mathrm{tp})$ over the same snapshot. Free-slot
queues are $O(1)$ amortised per layout mutation. Writing
$C_\mathrm{iter} = O(\lvert F\rvert + M(\lvert F_\mathrm{loc}\rvert +
N_\mathrm{loc}) + n N_\mathrm{tp})$ for that per-iteration cost, a route
of $I$ iterations costs $T_\mathrm{pre} + O(I \cdot C_\mathrm{iter})$.
We keep $I$ as an independent quantity: an iteration may apply a local or
preparation SWAP without retiring a gate, and capacity relief adds further
inter-core actions, so nothing here bounds $I$ by the gate count $N$ ---
the iteration and repeated-state guards of \mainProgress{} bound it by
construction instead. The shape is in line with other core-local
distributed compilers~\cite{lidsabre,russo2025telesabre}. This is an
operation count for \emph{one} route, and it is not what the reported
timings measure: those are complete protocol time over several routes
(\mainProtocols{}), so they neither validate this bound nor are bounded by
it.

\section{The Layout-Discount Search (\PORTER{}+S)}
\label{app:layoutsearch}

\PORTER{}+S is a third operating protocol, reported here rather than in
the main paper because it is a parameter search rather than a design
decision, and because what it returns does not justify what it costs. It
searches the one free scalar in the initial-layout heuristic --- the
per-layer discount $\gamma$ of \mainInitialLayout{}, fixed at $0.99$
everywhere else --- in two phases.

Phase~1 builds a layout for each $\gamma\in\{0.80,0.90,0.95,0.99,1.0\}$
and routes it once at the configured horizon --- five routes --- keeping
the lowest-EPR layout. Phase~2 runs the $P{=}5$ backward-refinement
schedule of \mainRefine{} from two seeds: phase~1's winner, and the
$\gamma{=}0.99$ layout when that is not already the winner. Refining the
phase-1 winner alone is not reliably best, so both are run. Each seed
selects a horizon before refining, so each costs
$\lvert\mathcal{H}\rvert+P-1=7$ routes as \PORTER{}+R does, and the
nominal budget is $5+7=12$ invocations of the routing loop when
$\gamma{=}0.99$ wins phase~1 and $5+7+7=19$ otherwise. One invocation per
seed re-routes a layout at a horizon phase~1 already covered, so the
number of \emph{distinct} routes --- which is what the released harness
records --- is $11$ or $17$, falling to $10$ where a per-pass guard stops
a pass short. Since $\gamma{=}0.99$ always competes and is refined from
its selected route, \PORTER{}+S cannot return a higher EPR count than
\PORTER{} or \PORTER{}+R do.

No single $\gamma$ dominates --- all five win somewhere, $0.80$ most
often (nine of twenty-four instances) --- which is why the value is
searched rather than retuned. The search is deterministic and takes no
seed.

\textbf{What it buys.} Table~\ref{tab:layoutsearch} gives the result.
Pooled over the twenty-four instances \PORTER{}+S reaches $159.1$ EPR
pairs against \PORTER{}+R's $163.0$, a $2.4\%$ reduction, and
$0.93\times$ \dSABRE{}'s $171.9$ against \PORTER{}+R's $0.95\times$. It
improves on ten instances, ties on fourteen and --- being a superset
schedule --- loses on none. Its one qualitative effect is at 64 qubits,
the single suite \PORTER{} loses under its own protocol: the ratio
against \dSABRE{} falls from \PORTER{}+R's $1.07\times$ to $1.02\times$.

\textbf{What it costs.} The same twenty-four instances take $4{,}267$\,s
against \PORTER{}+R's $1{,}468$\,s --- $2.9\times$ the wall clock, and
$23\times$ \dSABRE{}'s complete protocol cost of $182$\,s. The
expenditure is badly distributed: the 80-qubit suite consumes $4{,}045$
of those seconds and returns $1.2\%$, and 80q~AE alone spends $928$\,s
against \PORTER{}+R's $14.9$\,s --- a factor of $62$ --- to move from
$299$ pairs to $284$. On more than half the suite the search returns
nothing at all.

\textbf{Why it is not in the main paper.} Three reasons beyond the
return. It cannot appear in the layout-against-router grid of
\mainSharedTable{}, since its mechanism \emph{is} choosing the initial
layout and a supplied layout would destroy that control --- so it can
only ever be a fourth own-protocol column. It introduces a second
in-sample-fitted quantity, the $\gamma$ set, alongside the horizon
candidate sets whose in-sample fitting \mainHorizon{} already
declares. And at $12$--$19$ routing-loop invocations and $6.55\times$ \dSABRE{}'s compile
time it sits on the wrong side of the argument \mainCompileTime{} makes,
that \PORTER{}'s time advantage lies in the number of routes it spends
rather than their individual price. We report it here so the shape of
the EPR/time frontier past \PORTER{}+R is on the record.

\textbf{\PORTER{}+SR is subsumed.} An earlier version of this work also
reported \PORTER{}+SR, the better of horizon selection and the
$\gamma$-search. Phase~2 refines from the $\gamma{=}0.99$ layout among
its seeds, and that is the layout \PORTER{} builds by default, so once
refinement runs on top of horizon selection the search contains the
selected route and cannot lose to it. \PORTER{}+SR does not differ from
\PORTER{}+S on any of the twenty-four instances, at $19\%$ more compile
time, and is retired.


\section{Threats to Validity}
\label{app:threats}

This appendix collects the assumptions and limitations the evaluation of
the main paper rests on. Each is stated where it first matters; they are
gathered here so a reader can weigh them together.

\textbf{Mechanism attribution.} No experiment replaces a plan with a hop
(\mainPlanFirst{}), so neither the plan abstraction nor the commitment
rule is isolated, and the reductions of \mainOwnProtocol{} belong to the whole
system. Separating their share needs a hop-granular candidate set, which
is a different router.

\textbf{Completion is empirical.} \PORTER{} guarantees correctness and
termination by construction but not completion (\mainRelated{}). Every
instance completes under the selected-horizon protocol; at a fixed horizon
one does not, so that record belongs to the protocol and this benchmark
rather than to the router.

\textbf{Benchmark coverage.} Nine MQT-Bench families at three widths, but
the scale-up series is two families only --- nothing supports a claim about
scaling on irregular circuits at those widths --- and the heavy-hex study
is two core graphs, not a survey.

\textbf{Parameter rules fitted in-sample.} The horizon candidate sets and
the density threshold were chosen by inspecting these same instances
(\mainHorizon{}), so the \emph{magnitude} of that gain is in-sample; the
portfolio construction bounds the downside, but no out-of-sample
validation has been performed. The same caution applies in weaker form to
$f_\mathrm{min}$, $m_\mathrm{assist}$ and the packet window, fixed early
and not swept.

\textbf{Reconstructed benchmark instances.} The 80-qubit AE and QNN
circuits were reconstructed rather than generated
(Appendix~\ref{app:suite80}). We release the scripts and validate QNN
against the 64-qubit file, but they remain our circuits, not the
generator's.

\textbf{Patched baseline binary.} All \TeleSABRE{} numbers use the patched
binary of \mainBaselines{}. Beyond the bound stated there --- it moves only
qubits the stock binary leaves unplaced, and architecture~H is unchanged
--- we have not audited its effect.

\textbf{Unequal native budgets.} The own-protocol comparison gives
\dSABRE{} nine routes to \PORTER{}'s three. That is each compiler's own
convention, not a controlled budget --- hence the shared-layout experiment,
and hence compile time throughout.

\textbf{Aggregation.} Geometric means are taken over jointly completed
instances, so a baseline failing hard instances is scored only on easier
ones; completion counts accompany every such mean. Being unweighted, they
also let the two cheapest circuit families move a suite figure out of
proportion to their cost --- which is why \mainSharedTable{} reports both
bases.

\textbf{Implementation and timing.} \PORTER{} and \dSABRE{} are Python and
\TeleSABRE{} is C, so per-route times compare implementations as much as
algorithms, and all are wall-clock on one machine. Horizon selection
guards its speculative routes with a wall-clock budget, so a loaded
machine may abandon a candidate that would otherwise have won: the
protocol is reproducible only up to that budget, each individual route
exactly.

\textbf{Cost model.} One EPR pair per inter-core action, independent of
hop count and generation protocol; link contention, entanglement latency
and fidelity, purification and classical communication are outside the
model (\mainScope{}). Conclusions are relative to it.
